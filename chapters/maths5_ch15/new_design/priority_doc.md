## SECTION A: Chapter Understanding

### A1. Chapter name
Data Through Pictures (Class 5, Maths Mela, Chapter 15).

### A2. Main learning objectives
Students will be able to:
1. Record the answers of many people in a table, one row for each kind of answer, and read the table (how many, more than, less than).
2. Explain that a pictograph shows a number with icons, and that a scale (one icon stands for several items) lets a large number be shown with few icons.
3. Read a pictograph with a scale (icons times the scale), compare its rows (most, total, how many more, how many fewer) and choose another scale for the same data.
4. See that a bar graph shows the same data as a row of squares, with one bar for each activity whose height tells the number, and name its parts (the vertical line for the numbers, the horizontal base for the activities, the bars).
5. Read a bar graph and compare two sets of data in it (who spends more, how many more, the same amount).
6. Make a bar graph from a table (and of real lengths in cm) on a given graph.
7. Use a bar graph to find the highest and the lowest, the total, a result from a rule (1 kg of food feeds 3 children), compare two days and make a prediction.
8. Judge statements about a picture by counting what the picture shows (true or false), including "more than half" and "some".

### A5. Natural learning sequence
Collect and record data in a table -> show data with pictures and a scale -> read and compare a pictograph -> change squares into bars (bar graph) -> read and compare bars -> make a bar graph (own day, real lengths) -> use a bar graph (totals, a rule, a prediction) -> read a picture by counting -> revision. This is the book's own order. The one change: the "Suggest some ways to him" prompt becomes the prediction step at the start of the scale video, and the notebook table (a blank in the book) is filled first, so that its four questions can be answered.

### A6. Points the textbook leaves unclear (to confirm; the plan does not guess)
1. **The counts in the notebook are not printed and the table has no "fill me" instruction.** The four questions on p. 180 need the counts, so the plan counts the 35 speech bubbles (zoomed 4.5 times, twice): 1/2 hr 9, 1 hr 13, 1 1/2 hr 7, 2 hr 3, 2 1/2 hr 3, in all 35 (the number the book gives). The three "2 1/2 hr" answers belong to the row "More than 2 hours". Filling the table is a coverage item of its own (a prompt) whose wording is the plan's; the book prints only the empty table.
2. **"More than half an hour" and "less than two hours" (p. 180).** The plan reads "more than half an hour" as every row after 1/2 hour (13 + 7 + 3 + 3 = 26) and "less than two hours" as the rows 1/2 hour, 1 hour and 1 1/2 hour (9 + 13 + 7 = 29). The row "2 hours" is not "less than two hours".
3. **Whose Index Finger is Longer? has no printed data.** Its four questions depend on the student's friends. The plan answers them with a sample whose numbers are clearly practice values: Mine 6 cm, Friend 1 7 cm, Friend 2 5 cm, Friend 3 8 cm, Friend 4 6 cm. The page has five places for strips (Mine and four blanks), so the sample has five strips. Each answer says "Your answer will differ". The cutting and tearing of paper strips is hands-on and is not replaced by a tool; the tool takes the measured lengths.
4. **Day in My Life has no printed data either, and the book says "in hours and minutes, as necessary"** but the graph's vertical line is marked in whole hours from 0 to 9 and says nothing about minutes. The plan's sample uses whole hours only (9 + 5 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 3 = 24). The table has four named rows (Sleeping, Time in school, Studying, Eating) and two blank rows; the plan uses the blank rows for "Playing" and "Other activities". How to show part of an hour on the graph is not taught and is not introduced.
5. **"How is Sheela's recording different from Raman's?"** The book does not say Raman's table is a pictograph. The plan calls it Raman's table of squares (one square = 1 hour), which is a row of icons, and uses it as the bridge from icons to bars. The five activity names differ slightly (Raman: "Time spent sleeping", Sheela: "Sleeping"); the plan reads them as the same five activities.
6. **Recording a Day, Q4 ("Is there any activity on which they spend the same amount of time?")** has no printed answer. Raman 9, 6, 2, 6, 1 and Sheela 8, 7, 4, 3, 2 are different in every row, so the answer is "No".
7. **Recording a Day, Q5 ("whose routine is more balanced? Why?")** is a matter of opinion. The plan gives one sample (Sheela's: her study time 4 hours and play time 3 hours are closer than Raman's 2 and 6) and says another answer is right if its reason uses the numbers.
8. **Food Wastage, Q5 (two blanks) and Q6 (a prediction)** have no single answer. The plan gives a sample (Friday had less food wastage than Monday: 4 kg and 5 kg; Khichdi is likely to be wasted the most again) and says a prediction is a careful guess from the graph.
9. **The notice board (13 kg could have fed 25 people) and Q4 (1 kg can feed 3 children) use different rates** (13 times 3 is 39, not 25). The plan uses the question's own rule for Q4 (30 kg gives 90 children) and does not use the notice board's numbers in any calculation.
10. **True or False: gender and hidden children.** The plan reads boys and girls from the uniform (girls: blue pinafore; boys: shirt and shorts) and the hair. Some back-row children are partly hidden. Statements 3 (7 boys and 7 girls hold books) and 6 (4 boys and 3 girls wear glasses) depend on this reading. The counts read off the picture (zoomed 5 to 9 times): 17 children, 8 girls, 9 boys, 10 caps, 7 pairs of glasses, 14 books, 4 backpacks. Please confirm against the original artwork.
11. **True or False, statement 8** prints a dash ("all three items, a cap, a book and a backpack"); the plan writes it with commas. One girl in the back row has a cap, a book and a backpack, so the statement is false.
12. **"Choose your scale and show the same data using a pictograph in your notebook"** (p. 181, and Q5 on p. 182) is a notebook task. The plan keeps both as open prompts with one sample each (scale 10 for the toys, board games and sports items: 4, 5 and 6 icons; scale 6 for the two-wheelers: 4, 2 and 6 icons) and says the student's own scale may differ.

## SECTION C: Interactive Tool Specifications

Common to every tool (details in sections D and E): one activity at a time; Back and Next always in the same place; progress dots; Hint, Retry, Reset, and (where moving objects) Undo; specific feedback ("3 icons of 5 make 15"), never only right or wrong; the data object (table, icons, bars, picture) is the largest thing on screen. Practice values the plan adds are labelled "practice values"; every other number is the book's.

### Tool 1. Reporter's Notebook
- **Connected topic:** Child TV Reporter (the opening topic, free first try right after the hook; the concept video follows it). Pages 179 and 180.
- **Learning objective:** Students sort the answers of 35 friends into the rows of a table and read how many answered each way.
- **Why a tool:** the notebook is a crowd of 35 bubbles; moving each answer into its row is what turns a crowd into a table, and the counts are then seen, not told.
- **Environment:** the class newsroom desk: Samaira and Kabir's notebook open on the desk, the table next to it.
- **Objects:** the notebook page with 35 faces, each with a speech bubble showing its answer (1/2 hr, 1 hr, 1 1/2 hr, 2 hr, 2 1/2 hr, as in your maths book); the table with five rows (1/2 hour, 1 hour, 1 1/2 hour, 2 hours, More than 2 hours) and a count box on each row; a counter "answers still to sort".
- **Controls:** drag a bubble to a row (or tap the bubble, then tap the row); tap a placed bubble to send it back; the count box of a row grows by one at every drop.
- **What the student does:** The hook's picture of the two reporters and the question "How many hours a day do you spend watching TV?"; instruction "Every answer goes in the row where it belongs." A small practice notebook of 8 answers (practice values) sorted into rows 1 hour, 2 hours and 3 hours; read the count of each row and check the counts add up to 8; one practice question "How many answered more than 1 hour?" answered by adding rows. The book's table filled from the real notebook (35 answers), then the book's four questions unchanged: "How many children watch TV for more than half an hour?", "How many children watch TV for less than two hours?", "The number of children who watch TV for more than two hours is ________.", "More children watch TV for two hours than half an hour. (True/False)".
- **Feedback and hints:** a bubble dropped in the wrong row bounces back with "This answer says 2 hours, so it goes in the row 2 hours." Hints: which row has the same words as the bubble; what does 2 1/2 hr mean next to 2 hours; which rows are more than half an hour; bottom-out places the bubble.
- **Retry and reset:** Retry returns the bubbles of this step; Reset empties the table.

### Tool 2. Pictograph Maker
- **Connected topic:** Stock-Taking in a Shop (with Two-wheelers on the Road). Pages 180 to 182.
- **Learning objective:** Students choose a scale for a pictograph, place icons for each number and read a number back as icons times the scale.
- **Why a tool:** placing icons and watching the number of icons change when the scale changes is what makes "a scale helps record a large number using fewer icons" something the student sees.
- **Environment:** Joseph Uncle's shop shelf in the class newsroom: a table of items on the left, a pictograph strip on the right.
- **Objects:** a table of items with their numbers; a scale box "1 icon = ___" with the choices 1, 2, 5, 10 and others as needed; a tray of icons (a train, a board game, a racket, a scooter); three or more rows to drop icons into; a read-out "icons x scale = number".
- **Controls:** pick the scale; drag icons from the tray into a row (or tap to add, tap to remove); "Show the number" turns the icons of a row into the number; Check.
- **What the student does:** The video's picture of a shop shelf with one icon for 5 items; instruction "Show each number with icons. One icon stands for the scale." (1) set one icon = 5 and show a practice stock of 15, 30 and 25 items (practice values); (2) read a ready pictograph back into numbers; (3) change the scale of practice data 20, 30 and 50 and see 2, 3, 5 icons for scale 10 but a scale of 4 that does not fit. The book's items unchanged: Dipesh's pictograph (toys, board games, sports items) with "How many toys does Joseph Uncle have?", "How many board games...", "How many total play items..."; "Is there any other scale that you can use to make the pictograph? Choose your scale and show the same data using a pictograph in your notebook."; and Deepti's "Choose any other scale and represent the same data using a pictograph in your notebook."
- **Feedback and hints:** "You put 7 icons for 30 items. One icon is 5 items, so 30 items need 6 icons." A scale that leaves a part icon: "With one icon = 4, 18 items need 4 icons and 2 items are left over. Try another scale." Hints: what does one icon stand for; how many 5s are in the number; add icons until the read-out shows the number; bottom-out places the icons.
- **Retry and reset:** Retry clears the icons of this step; Reset clears the strip and the scale.

### Tool 3. Squares to Bars
- **Connected topic:** Recording a Day (Raman's table and Sheela's bar graph). Pages 182 to 183.
- **Learning objective:** Students turn a row of squares into a bar of the same height, read the height of each bar on the vertical line and compare two children's bars.
- **Why a tool:** seeing the squares of a row stand up into a bar is the link between icons and bars; it makes "the height of the bar is the number" real.
- **Environment:** the graph wall of the class newsroom: Raman's table on the left, an empty graph on the right.
- **Objects:** a table with rows of blue squares (1 square = 1 hour); a graph with the vertical line (0 to 10, "Number of Hours") and the horizontal base (the five activities); a reading line that slides up and down; a compare view that puts two graphs side by side.
- **Controls:** tap a row of squares to stand it up into a bar; drag the reading line to the top of a bar and read the number; tap two bars to compare ("more", "the same", "how many more"); switch the compare view on and off.
- **What the student does:** The video's picture of three rows of squares standing up into bars; instruction "Stand each row up. The bar is as tall as the row is long." A practice table (Samaira's week: homework 4, games 2, TV 1 hours; practice values): stand up the rows, read the heights with the reading line, then make a bar as tall as another bar; a second practice graph to compare for "how many more". Raman's table of squares stood up into bars and Sheela's bar graph side by side, and the book's questions unchanged: "Whose daily routine shows more time spent on sleeping?", "Who spends more hours in the school?", "How many more hours does Sheela spend studying compared to Raman?", "Is there any activity on which they spend the same amount of time? If yes, name the activity", "Based on their data, whose routine do you think is more balanced? Why?".
- **Feedback and hints:** "The row has 9 squares, so the bar goes up to 9." Hints: how many squares are in the row; where does the top of the bar touch the vertical line; which bar is taller; bottom-out shows the reading line on the bar.
- **Retry and reset:** Retry lays the squares of this step down again; Reset lays all rows down.

### Tool 4. Bar Graph Builder
- **Connected topic:** Making a Bar Graph (Day in My Life and Whose Index Finger is Longer?). Pages 183 to 185.
- **Learning objective:** Students make a bar graph from a table of numbers, with the activities on the horizontal base and the numbers on the vertical line, and make bars of real lengths in cm.
- **Why a tool:** making the graph (names on the base, a bar up to each number) is a sequence of steps that is easy to check and repeat; the student can use their own times and measured lengths.
- **Environment:** the graph wall of the class newsroom: a blank graph sheet with a table beside it, a strip rack for the lengths.
- **Objects:** a table (activity or name and a number box); a graph sheet like the book's (vertical line with equal marks, a horizontal base with places for names); bars that grow to the number typed; for lengths, paper strips of one width and a cm ruler along the vertical line (1 cm at the foot).
- **Controls:** type or step a number in the table; type names in the base places; drag the top of a bar up or down to a mark; in the lengths mode drag a strip to its measured length; "Total" read-out; Check.
- **What the student does:** The video's picture of a table next to its bar graph; instruction "Write the names on the base. Make each bar as tall as its number." (1) a practice table of three numbers (practice values: 5, 3, 7 hours) built into a bar graph; (2) find the mistake in a graph whose bar is too tall; (3) the lengths mode with practice strips of 7 cm, 5 cm and 3 cm, longest and shortest. The book's items unchanged: "Record your daily routine (24 hours) in hours and minutes, as necessary. Note the time spent on activities like sleeping, studying, playing, eating, and others.", "Make a bar graph of the time you spend on different activities in the space given below.", and Whose Index Finger is Longer? with "Whose index finger is the longest?", "The length of the longest index finger is ________ cm.", "The smallest index finger is ________ cm long.", "It belongs to ________." The student types their own times (the tool checks they add up to 24) and their five measured lengths.
- **Feedback and hints:** "Sleeping is 9 hours, so the bar goes up to 9." The Total read-out says "Your hours add up to 22. A day has 24 hours." Hints: which number goes with this name; where does the bar stop on the vertical line; check the total; bottom-out draws the bar.
- **Retry and reset:** Retry clears the bars of this step; Reset clears the table and the sheet.

### Tool 5. Picture Detective
- **Connected topic:** True or False (reading a picture). Page 187.
- **Learning objective:** Students count the children of a picture who have a chosen thing (a cap, glasses, a book, a backpack, or two together), compare the counts and use them to judge a statement as true or false.
- **Why a tool:** switching a group on and watching its members light up makes the counting exact, and the counts side by side make "more than" and "more than half" easy to judge.
- **Environment:** the photo board of the class newsroom: the picture of the school group pinned on it, the statement cards below.
- **Objects:** the picture of 17 school children as in your maths book (9 boys, 8 girls); filter chips Girls, Boys, Cap, Glasses, Book, Backpack; a combine switch "and" for two chips; a counter and one small bar for each group counted; a half-way line at half of the number of children; statement cards with TRUE and FALSE buttons.
- **Controls:** tap a chip (or two chips with "and") to light up the matching children; the counter shows how many; tap TRUE or FALSE on a card; Check.
- **What the student does:** The video's picture of a group with some children wearing hats; instruction "To check a statement, count the children it talks about." A practice picture of 10 children with scarves and bags (practice values): count each group, compare two groups, find "more than half" with the half-way line. The book's eight statements unchanged ("All girls are wearing glasses." and so on, to "There is no child who has all three items, a cap, a book and a backpack.").
- **Feedback and hints:** "You said TRUE. 7 children wear glasses and half of 17 is 8 and a half, so 7 is not more than half." Hints: which children does the statement talk about; light them up and count; compare with the other number or with half; bottom-out lights the group.
- **Retry and reset:** Retry clears the highlights and the answer of this card; Reset clears all cards.

## SECTION D: Shared Visual Language

- **Overall illustration style:** warm, colourful, polished educational-game illustrations; soft shadows, clear shapes, clean uncluttered compositions; gentle animation. The data (the notebook, the icons, the bars, the picture) is always the largest, clearest thing on screen.
- **Chapter theme and environment:** "Samaira and Kabir's Class Newsroom". The two child reporters have turned a corner of the classroom into a newsroom wall: a desk with the notebook, a shop shelf for the icon strips, a graph wall for the bars and a photo board for the picture. Each tool is one board of this wall, and the contexts of the book (TV, a toy shop, scooters on a road, a school day, a canteen, a group of schoolchildren) appear as small pinned pictures on the boards. Nothing is added that does not help the mathematics.
- **Characters:** only the book's: Samaira, Kabir, Joseph Uncle, Dipesh, Deepti, Raman, Sheela and Rani. They set a situation, never teach.
- **Colour and visual direction:** soft cream and blue-grey wall tones; the icons keep the book's own pictures (train, board game, racket, scooter, squares); bars are one friendly colour per graph (orange as in Sheela's graph, blue as in the food graph); the number or bar being asked about is highlighted in orange, correct is green, "look again" is soft amber (never red).
- **Main visual objects:** a table with clear rows; icons in a row with the icon box beside it; bars standing on a horizontal base with equal marks on the vertical line and the numbers 0, 1, 2, ... labelled.
- **Animation style:** only meaningful movement: a bubble slides into its row, icons drop into a row, a row of squares stands up into a bar, a reading line slides to the top of a bar, a group of children lights up. No decoration that moves.
- **UI layout and navigation:** one activity per screen; instruction strip at the top (one sentence, large); the working area in the middle; Hint, Retry, Reset (and Undo) in a fixed row at the bottom; Back and Next fixed at the bottom corners; progress dots under the instruction. Same placement in all five tools.
- **Common interaction patterns:** tap to select, drag to move or place, a stepper or number box for a value, a Check button that gives specific feedback, a three-step hint ladder with a worked step at the end, Retry for the step, Reset for the tool.

## SECTION E: Learning and Platform Rules

1. Videos explain; tools let the student explore, apply and get feedback. No classroom teacher is given a teaching role anywhere.
2. Every tool has one distinct mathematical purpose; no interaction is repeated across tools without a learning reason. Tool 1 sorts answers into a table; Tool 2 shows numbers with icons and a scale; Tool 3 turns squares into bars and reads them; Tool 4 makes a bar graph from numbers; Tool 5 counts groups in a picture.
3. Each tool has three phases, in order: Recall and get ready, Play and apply, NCERT challenge.
4. A tool card that quotes the book uses the book's own questions, with their original wording, numbers and conditions. The plan does not change them and does not invent a challenge where the book has none.
5. Preserve the book's data: the 35 answers of the notebook (9, 13, 7, 3, 3), the pictographs (8, 10, 12 icons of 5; 8, 4, 12 icons of 3), Raman's and Sheela's days (9, 6, 2, 6, 1 and 8, 7, 4, 3, 2), the food graph (5, 6, 8, 7, 4 kg) and the picture of 17 children. Any number the plan adds is labelled "practice values".
6. Do not introduce ideas outside the chapter: no tally marks, no frequency, no average, mean, median or mode, no line graph, pie chart or double bar graph, no half icons, no percentages, no scale with part icons. Use the book's words: data, table, pictograph, icon, scale, bar graph, vertical line, horizontal base, bar.
7. One video per book question, on its answer, plus a question card and a solution card; one video per teaching idea; the hook comes first and does not explain the chapter.
8. Answers the book does not print are worked out from the chapter's own ideas and shown as the plan's reading (section A6).
9. Text hygiene: no em or en dash; minus is the true minus sign; students never read "NCERT", "textbook", page numbers or exercise numbers; say "your maths book".
10. Instructions are short, age-appropriate, one at a time; feedback is specific; hint, retry and reset are always present.
11. One visual identity (the class newsroom) and one navigation layout across all tools.
12. Verify page numbers, figures and numbers against the book's page images before any build.

### F2. Concepts and activities with no separate tool, and why
- **Comparing with a Pictograph (Two-wheelers on the Road, p. 182):** reading, totals and differences use the same icons-times-scale step as Tool 2, whose cards include Deepti's last question; a second pictograph tool would repeat that interaction.
- **Using a Bar Graph: Totals and Predictions (Food Wastage, p. 186):** reading bars is practised in Tool 3 and the totals, the rule (1 kg feeds 3 children) and the prediction are calculation and reasoning; a tool would only repeat reading bars.
- **Discuss and suggest prompts** ("Can watching TV for a long time be harmful to the eyes?", "Suggest some ways to him.", "How is Sheela's recording different from Raman's recording?", "What do you think about food wastage?"): talk prompts, kept in the teaching text of the matching video.
- **Hands-on paper strips (p. 184):** cutting and tearing paper strips cannot be done on a screen; Tool 4 takes the measured lengths.
- **Chapter Revision:** a text summary with short checks (pipeline rule, see section B note).
