# maths6_ch2 - tool descriptions (8 tools)

Tool Name: Name It

Tool Description:

A drawing sheet shows a few labelled points. Above it is a toolbar with three drawing tools named Segment, Ray and Line, and below it sit a name box that takes letters and a Check button. Each card names the points to use, and the student first says whether the thing to be drawn has two ends, one end or none.

The student picks a tool and draws between the marked points. A segment is drawn with a dot at each end, a ray with a dot at its starting point and an arrowhead at the far end, and a line with an arrowhead at both ends, so the ends can be counted on the screen. Then the student types the name: the two end letters for a segment, the starting point first for a ray, and two points on it for a line. A ray named with its starting point second, such as PO for the ray OP, is redrawn starting at P and pointing the other way, beside the student's own figure. A segment drawn as a curved or bent route between A and B gets the straight segment laid beside it, so the shorter one can be seen.

The six cards are: join A and B by the shortest path and name the segment AB; draw the ray from O through P and name it OP, not PO; draw the line through L and M and name it line LM; three points A, B and C on one line, where the student lists every segment on it (AB, BC and AC) and a counter shows how many are found; a torch T shining through a lamp L, where the beam starts at T and goes on past L, so it is the ray TL and not LT; and last, draw through one point as many lines as possible, then through two points. The one-point sheet keeps taking new lines, but on the two-point sheet a second line falls exactly on the first and is marked as the same line. A solved card shows the object with its name and its ends marked.

Hints go in order: what does the picture have at its ends, two, one or none? Then: a segment has two ends, a ray has one and a line has none, and the starting point comes first in the name of a ray. Then: find the starting point of the ray, write its letter first and then a point on the ray. Last, the object is shown with its ends marked and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns to tell a segment, a ray and a line apart by their ends, to write the name of a ray in the right order, and that many lines pass through one point but only one line passes through two points.

Tool Name: Name the Angle

Tool Description:

A figure of rays drawn from labelled points fills the sheet. Under it are three letter boxes in a row, with the middle box marked as the place for the vertex, and a Check button. The student taps one arm and then a second arm, and a coloured arc appears between them to show which angle has been chosen.

Before tapping, the student predicts which angle a card is asking about. Then the name is typed as a point on one arm, the vertex, and a point on the other arm. For the angle with arms OA and OB, both AOB and BOA are accepted and light the same arc, so the student sees that reading the name backwards gives the same angle. A name with the vertex at the end, such as OAB, lights up the angle that really sits at A, which is a different angle. A single letter, such as P, lights up every angle at that vertex one after another, so the student sees that one letter cannot say which angle is meant.

The six cards are: one angle with arms OA and OB; the same angle written the other way round; three rays PA, PB and PC from one vertex P, where all three angles (APB, BPC and APC) are named and a tick list shows the ones found; a figure with vertex T and rays TP, TQ and TR, where an arc joins two of the arms and the student names the angle it shows; two angles with the same vertex, where the student tries one letter and sees why it is not enough; and four points A, B, C and D with no three on one line and the six lines through pairs of them drawn in. Here the student lists every angle, three at each point, and a counter runs up to 12. An angle written in the other order, such as CAB for BAC, is recognised as already found. A solved card shows the arc and the name with the vertex in the middle.

Hints go in order: which point do the two arms start from? Then: the vertex goes in the middle of the name. Then: write one point on an arm, then the vertex, then a point on the other arm. Last, the angle is shown with its arc and name and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that the vertex is always the middle letter, that one letter cannot name an angle when more angles share the vertex, and that three such points give 3 angles while four give 12.

(tool id M1.S5.T5.C26.2D1, concept M1.S5.T5.C26, topic: What Is an Angle?)

Tool Name: Which Angle Is Greater?

Tool Description:

Two labelled angles lie side by side on a sheet, one drawn on a movable tracing in blue and the other fixed in orange. A drag handle on the blue angle slides it anywhere and turns it about its vertex, a ghost copy stays behind where it started, and three buttons named Greater, Smaller and Equal wait at the bottom.

On every card the student first predicts which angle is greater, then tests it. The blue angle is dragged until its vertex sits on the other vertex and turned until one arm lies on one arm. The tool shows when the two match and colours the second arms, so the student can see whether the blue second arm falls outside the orange angle, inside it, or exactly on it. Only then are Greater, Smaller or Equal pressed. If the student presses one while the vertices are still apart, the two vertices are shown apart and the tool says the comparison does not work yet. Either arm can be laid on either arm and the answer is the same. When arms of different length lie together, the longer arms are trimmed on screen to the same length, to show that the angles have not changed.

The six cards are: two angles with long and short arms, where the longer arms do not belong to the greater angle; two angles that look equal although one has longer arms; two angles turned in different directions; three angles to put in order from smallest to largest by comparing two at a time and dragging them into three slots; two equal angles drawn differently; and last, two angles very close in size. For this card a transparent circle appears. Its centre is dropped on the vertex, and the points A and B where the arms cross it are marked. The circle is then moved to the other angle with one arm through A. A second arm that meets the circle beyond B means the greater angle, on B means equal, and short of B means smaller. A solved card shows the angles laid together with the outside arm marked.

Hints go in order: what two things must meet before the angles are compared? Then: put the vertices together and one arm on one arm, and look at the other arms. Then: whichever second arm lies outside the other belongs to the greater angle. Last, the angles are shown laid together with the greater one marked and a similar pair follows. Through doing, the student learns that the size of an angle is the turn between its arms and not their length, and that two angles are equal when they fit exactly on each other.

Tool Name: Fold a Right Angle

Tool Description:

A paper strip lies flat on the screen with a point O on its edge. The edge makes a straight angle AOB, with the two arms pointing opposite ways and a half-turn arc drawn over them. A crease line through O can be dragged by its end and turned, a Fold button swings one side over onto the other and leaves the far side as a faint ghost, and a corner checker, a small L that can be moved and turned, waits at the side.

The student first predicts whether the two arms will lie on top of each other, then drags the crease and presses Fold. A crease that misses the middle leaves one arm sticking out past the other, and the two angles are shown unequal. When the crease is dragged until the folded arm lies exactly on the other arm, the two angles fit on each other and are marked equal. The tool then names each one a right angle and writes that a straight angle holds two right angles. The student keeps that crease, then folds again along a second crease at right angles to the first, which gives four equal angles at O and shows each right angle as a quarter of a full turn.

The six cards are: fold a straight angle to get two equal angles; fold again to get four equal angles at O; test an L-shaped corner that has been turned to a slant by laying the checker on the folded right angle, to see that a right angle stays a right angle however it is turned; a dot grid with a point A and a point B, where the student counts the ways to join A to another dot so that the two segments make a straight angle (3 ways on the grid where A and B lie on one row); the same grid, where the student counts the ways to make a right angle (5 ways); and a classroom picture of windows, a door, a desk, the board and a book, where the checker is dragged onto each corner. A corner that sticks out past the checker is marked as not a right angle. The similar card that follows a solved grid card uses A and B on a slant, with 2 ways for a straight angle and 4 for a right angle.

Hints go in order: what do the two arms of a straight angle look like when folded together? Then: drag the crease until the two arms lie exactly on each other. Then: test a corner by laying it on the folded right angle. Last, the right angle is shown on the fold and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that folding is superimposition, that a right angle is half of a straight angle and a quarter turn, and that an L shape is a right angle only when it fits the checker exactly.

(tool id M2.S12.T12.C65.2D1, concept M2.S12.T12.C65, topic: Straight Angles and Right Angles)

Tool Name: Paper Protractor

Tool Description:

A paper semicircle lies on the screen with its centre O in the middle of the straight edge, 0 written at the bottom right corner and 180 at the bottom left. A Fold button sits below it, a box takes the measure of each new crease, and nine empty marks A to I wait along the curved edge.

The student first predicts what angle the half circle makes at O, and types 180 as half of the full turn of 360. A student who types 360 sees the whole circle drawn beside the paper, with the half marked. Pressing Fold folds the semicircle in half and a crease runs up from O. The student predicts and types its measure, 90, and the tool writes it at the top and notes that it is half of 180 and also a quarter of 360. Fold again and the crease halves the 90, giving 45, and the matching crease is labelled 135 as 180 minus 45. One more fold gives 22.5. Before pressing Fold, the student may drag the crease to a place of their own choice. A crease that is not through the middle shows two unequal parts side by side, and a label that is not half of the one before is shown with the angle the fold really makes.

The six cards are: fold the semicircle in half and mark 90; fold the quarter in half and mark 45; fold again and mark 22.5; mark 135 from the crease at 90 and the one at 45; mark every crease from 0 to 180 in steps of 22.5, which are A at 0, B at 22.5, C at 45, D at 67.5, E at 90, F at 112.5, G at 135, H at 157.5 and I at 180; and use the finished paper protractor to measure a given angle, for example one whose arm lies on the crease at 112.5. An angle that falls between two creases, such as 42 degrees, can only be placed between 22.5 and 45, so the paper protractor cannot read it exactly. Tapping a crease names it as a bisector: OE halves the straight angle AOI into 90 and 90, and OC halves the angle AOE into 45 and 45. The eight angles AOB, BOC and so on up to HOI are all shown equal.

Hints go in order: what angle is the semicircle at the centre? Then: each fold halves the angle that was marked before it. Then: half of 90 is 45, and half of 45 is 22.5. Last, the folds are shown with their measures and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that each fold halves an angle, that the marks come out 22.5 degrees apart, and that the line which halves an angle is its bisector.

Tool Name: Protractor Reader

Tool Description:

A protractor floats over an angle on the sheet. The student can drag it and turn it, its centre dot is marked, the 0 mark is highlighted, and both scales are numbered. Under the angle are a number box for the reading and a Check button.

The student first predicts whether the angle is acute or obtuse. Then the protractor is dragged until its centre dot sits on the vertex and turned until one arm passes through the 0 mark. The reading is the number where the other arm crosses the scale that starts at 0 on that arm, and it is typed into the box. If the centre dot is off the vertex, a short line joins the two to show the gap. If the reading comes from the wrong scale, the angle's size is shown beside the number with a clue, for example that an angle smaller than a right angle cannot read 130. A reading within one degree of the true size is accepted.

The six cards are: an acute angle of 47 degrees with its base arm on the left; an obtuse angle of 108 degrees with its base arm on the right; an angle that opens upward (60 degrees) and one that opens downward (120 degrees), where the protractor has to be turned to put 0 on the arm; two angles at one vertex whose arms pass the 20 and 55 marks on the same scale, where the angle between them is found by subtracting the two readings, 55 minus 20 giving 35, and then checked by turning the protractor to put one arm on 0; a student's reading of 130 on an angle that is plainly acute, where the mistake is found and mended to 50; and the three angles of a triangle, such as 50, 60 and 70 degrees, which are measured one by one and added in a box that shows 180. A solved card shows the protractor placed correctly with the reading marked.

Hints go in order: is the angle smaller or bigger than a right angle? Then: put the centre on the vertex and one arm on 0, and read the scale that starts at 0 there. Then: if the angle is acute, the reading is less than 90. Last, the protractor is shown placed correctly with the reading and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns the three checks of a good reading, centre on the vertex, one arm on 0 and the right scale, and learns to test a reading against how the angle looks.

(tool id M3.S24.T24.C122.2D1, concept M3.S24.T24.C122, topic: Using a Protractor Well)

Tool Name: Draw the Angle

Tool Description:

The sheet shows a vertex I with a base ray IN drawn out of it, and a card at the top asking for an angle such as 30 degrees. The student has a protractor that can be dragged and turned, a dot tool for marking, and a Join button, and a check that reads the finished angle sits beside them.

The student first predicts where on the scale the mark will fall. The protractor is placed with its centre on I and the base ray IN on the 0 mark. Counting from 0 along the scale that starts there, in tens and then ones, the student puts a dot at the target number and names it T. The protractor is then taken away and Join draws the second arm from I to T. The check measures the finished angle and shows its reading, and the card is accepted when the angle is within 2 degrees of the target. A mark placed on the other scale shows the angle that was really drawn and its measure, for example a mark at 60 where 120 was asked. A base ray that is not on the 0 mark shows the offset between the ray and the mark.

The six cards are: draw a 30 degree angle; draw 75 degrees; draw 120 degrees; draw an angle with the base ray on the right, so that the scale that starts at 0 on the right must be used; draw an angle equal to a given angle of 40 degrees, which the student measures first; and draw 140 degrees. A solved card shows the finished angle with its measure.

Hints go in order: which arm is already drawn, and where is the 0 mark? Then: mark the number on the scale that starts at 0 on your base arm. Then: for 120, count from the 0 mark to the 120 mark and put the dot there. Last, the mark is shown on the scale and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that drawing an angle is measuring in reverse, that the scale must start at 0 on the base arm, and that a drawn angle should be checked by measuring it again.

Tool Name: Sort the Angle

Tool Description:

One angle stands at the left of the screen. It has a fixed base arm and a second arm that the student drags around the vertex, with a live measure in degrees changing as the arm turns. At the right are five labelled bins: acute, right, obtuse, straight and reflex. An estimate box and a Check button sit under the angle.

Each card gives a target. The student first predicts the type and types an estimate, then turns the arm until the live measure reads the target. A small square mark appears at the corner when the arm reaches 90, a straight line is drawn at 180, and past 180 the arc swings round the outside up to 360. The student then drops the angle into a bin. An angle of exactly 90 placed in obtuse shows the right angle mark and the words that obtuse needs more than 90. A reflex angle read on its small side shows the small angle and the rule 360 minus the small angle, so that a small angle of 100 gives a reflex angle of 260.

The six cards are: an angle of 35 degrees, acute; 90 degrees, right; 120 degrees, obtuse; 180 degrees, straight; 260 degrees, reflex; and last, an angle whose measure is hidden. The student estimates it, then the measure is shown with how many degrees the estimate was out, and the student names the type. Benchmarks of 45, 90 and 180 stay faintly marked to help the estimate. A solved card shows the angle with its measure and type.

Hints go in order: is the angle smaller than a right angle? Then: under 90 is acute, 90 is right, 90 to 180 is obtuse, 180 is straight and over 180 is reflex. Then: compare the measure with 90 and 180 in turn. Last, the angle is shown with its type and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that the type of an angle comes from comparing its measure with 90, 180 and 360, that 90 is right and not obtuse, and that a reflex angle is found by taking the small angle from a full turn.

(tool id M4.S31.T31.C168.2D1, concept M4.S31.T31.C168, topic: Types of Angles by Their Measure)
