# Class 5 Mathematics, Chapter 4: We the Travellers - II. Learning experience plan

Source: Maths Mela, Textbook of Mathematics for Grade 5, Chapter 4 (`textbooks/class-5/eemm104.pdf`, 15 PDF pages = printed pages 42 to 56; the opener page carries no printed number, it is page 42 by sequence, and the next page prints 43).
Written to the brief "Class 5 Mathematics Learning Experience Designer" (sections A to F). Every number, question, table, map label and character below was read from the page images, not from the text layer (the text layer scrambles every column sum and place value chart). Every answer in this plan was recomputed with Python (scratchpad `verify_facts.py`), including a search of every possible swap in the opening puzzles, every pair in the "closest sum" question, every coin combination, every palindrome and every grid.
Page references use the printed page number. "LUS" means a "Let Us Solve" box, "LUT" a "Let Us Think" box, "LUD" a "Let Us Do" box.
The chapter continues Chapter 1 (We the Travellers I): the same idea of a journey, now with the arithmetic a traveller needs (fuel, distance, money, weight) and number patterns for the long hours on the road.

## SECTION A: Chapter Understanding

### A1. Chapter name
We the Travellers - II (Class 5, Maths Mela, Chapter 4).

### A2. Main learning objectives
Students will be able to:
1. Explain how interchanging one number from each of two groups changes both sums, and make two sums equal in the fewest moves (the opener, p. 42).
2. Add two numbers with place value, regrouping 10 ones as 1 ten and 10 tens as 1 hundred, and find easier ways to add where a number is near a ten or a hundred (p. 43).
3. Use the link between addition and subtraction to write the sentences that follow from one sentence, and to check an answer (p. 44).
4. Subtract with place value, regrouping 1 ten as 10 ones, and check by adding back (pp. 44 to 45).
5. Find, describe and use patterns in the sums of consecutive numbers: even or odd, the steady difference between successive sums, and pairs from the two ends that make the same total (pp. 45 to 46).
6. Add and subtract large numbers up to 5 digits in the same way as small numbers, first with a place value chart and then with the digits aligned and no labels (pp. 46 to 50).
7. Solve journey problems that add, subtract and compare amounts of money, weight, distance and runs, and estimate before calculating (pp. 48, 50, 51, 56).
8. Find the number to add to reach 100, 1,000 or 10,000, and subtract 9 or 99 quickly, using round numbers as stepping stones (pp. 51 to 52).
9. Recognise and list palindrome numbers (p. 53).
10. Fill a 3 by 3 grid with 1 to 9 so that every row and every column is increasing or decreasing as asked (p. 53).
11. Explain even and odd numbers with paired arrangements, and what happens to the sum of two even numbers, two odd numbers, and an odd with an even number (p. 54).
12. Reason through two puzzles with coins and a torch switch (p. 54 to 55).
13. Read a table to find the highest and lowest values and differences, and decide whether an amount is enough, short or extra (pp. 55 to 56).

### A5. Natural learning sequence
A balancing puzzle that makes the student play with sums (hook, free try, the idea of swapping) -> adding with place value -> near-round numbers (a separate strategy) -> subtraction and its link with addition -> a pattern interlude (consecutive sums, then the pair-the-ends shortcut) -> large numbers added and subtracted, with aligned digits -> journey problems -> number tricks with round numbers (nines, 9 and 99) -> number puzzles (palindromes, grids) -> even and odd, then their sums -> thinking puzzles (coins, then the torch) -> reading tables -> revision. This is the book's own order. Changes: (1) the book's two arithmetic sets in "Let Us Do" on p. 56 (nine sums and nine subtractions) sit with the large-number topic and its three word problems (Ambrish, factory, runs) sit with journey problems, where their ideas are learnt; (2) the relationship between addition and subtraction and the "More Fuel Arithmetic" subtraction share one topic and one video with two labelled sections, because the check "18 + 47 = 65" is the link; (2b) near-round numbers, the pair-the-ends shortcut, the sums of even and odd numbers and the two Let Us Think puzzles each have their own topic and video, as the content team asked (version 3, 9 Nov 2026); (3) the mountain table and the certificate table share one topic (reading a table).

### A6. Points the textbook leaves unclear (to confirm; the plan does not guess)
1. **Page numbering.** The first PDF page is the unnumbered opener (printed page 42 by sequence); the next page prints 43, the last prints 56.
2. **Map label 1885 km (p. 46).** The map prints "1885 km" for the Srinagar side, while the text and the book's own sum use 1,855 km (1,855 + 1,862 = 3,717). The plan uses the text and the sum: 1,855 km. Please confirm.
3. **Repeated letters in the Let Us Do sums (p. 56).** Both Q1 (add) and Q2 (subtract) have nine parts but the printed letters run a to f, then f to h (f appears twice). The plan letters the nine parts (a) to (i) in the order printed.
4. **A six-digit answer (p. 56, Q1).** 50,050 + 55,000 = 1,05,050 has six digits, one place beyond the TTh chart of this chapter. The plan writes it with the Indian comma pattern (1,05,050), says the extra 1 is written in a new place on the left, and does not name that place. Please confirm.
5. **"Least number of moves" (p. 42).** The plan reads a move as interchanging one number from one group with one number from the other group. Checking every possible swap gives: (a) 1 move, swap 2 and 3; (b) 1 move, swap 5 and 9, or 7 and 11; (c) 2 moves; (d) 2 moves. The book prints no answers.
6. **The 2-and-5 hint (p. 42).** Interchanging 2 and 5 in puzzle (a) makes the sums 22 and 18, which are further apart than 19 and 21. The plan reads the hint as a first try that shows what a swap does, and the working swap is 2 and 3.
7. **"Explain why this is happening" (p. 45, Q1) and the two other questions of the box** print no answers. The plan's reading: sums of 2 consecutive numbers are always odd (one even and one odd number); of 3 consecutive numbers they alternate odd and even (the sum is 3 times the middle number); of 4 consecutive numbers they are always even (two pairs of consecutive numbers, odd plus odd). The difference between successive sums is 2, 3 and 4 in the three boxes, the same throughout each box (the number of numbers added); for 5 and 6 consecutive numbers it is 5 and 6.
8. **"Notice how the sums are related to the numbers being added" (p. 46).** The book's arrows join the first and last number of a run, then the second and the second from last. The plan reads the pattern as: pairs from the two ends make the same total (1 + 5 = 2 + 4 = 6 in 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5), so a run of 3 or 5 numbers adds to the number of numbers times the middle number, and a run of 4 or 6 numbers adds to the number of pairs times the pair total.
9. **The great Indian road trip (p. 48).** The four distances sit on the map next to Delhi to Mumbai (1,600 km), Mumbai to Goa (590 km), Goa to Hyderabad (670 km) and Hyderabad to Puri (1,055 km). The label 590 km sits under the word MUMBAI; the plan reads it as the Mumbai to Goa leg. The total is 3,915 km whichever leg carries it.
10. **"Find 2 numbers whose sum is closest" (p. 48).** The plan checks all ten pairs. For 13,000 two pairs are close (8,455 + 4,840 = 13,295, 295 away; 6,220 + 7,095 = 13,315, 315 away); the answer is the first pair.
11. **"Estimate whether the school council has raised enough" (p. 51).** The book does not say how to estimate. The plan rounds each amount up to the next thousand (as in Chapter 1), adds, and compares with ₹70,500: 40,000 + 10,000 + 20,000 = 70,000 is less than 70,500, and the real amounts are even less, so yes, enough. The exact total is 68,878, so ₹1,622 is left.
12. **"Will this method work if the units digit is 0?" and "Do you think this method will always work?" (p. 52)** are discussion prompts with no printed answer. The plan reads: yes, it always works; with a 0 in the units place the last nine is still 9 and the extra 1 carries (180: 819 + 1 = 820); other methods are to count up to the next hundred (180 + 20 = 200, then 200 + 800 = 1,000) or to take 180 from 1,000.
13. **"Use the above solutions to find answers ... Do not calculate again" (p. 52).** The plan reads "the above solutions" as the quick method for 9 and 99 (take away 10 or 100, then add 1) and the link between addition and subtraction (if a − b = c then a − c = b). The blanks are 32 − 23 = 9, 56 − 47 = 9, 877 − 778 = 99, 666 − 567 = 99.
14. **The grids (p. 53).** The plan reads rows left to right and columns top to bottom, with "inc" meaning growing in that direction. Each grid has many answers (42 for the first three, 60 for the last two); any valid grid is accepted. The book prints no answers.
15. **Palindromes between 900 and 1,200 (p. 53)** include the four digit numbers 1,001 and 1,111. The ends (100, 200, 900, 1,200, 25,000, 27,000) are not palindromes.
16. **The paired arrangements (p. 54).** Counted on the page: 18 is two rows of 9; 23 is a row of 12 and a row of 11 (one counter has no partner).
17. **Jincy's coins (p. 54).** "What possible combination" has 11 answers; the plan lists all of them by a systematic search and shows two or three as the sample.
18. **Raghu's torch (p. 54).** The torch is OFF before the first press (the first press turns it ON). After an odd press it is ON, after an even press OFF.
19. **Mountain climbing (p. 55).** (b) "difference between the highest and lowest peaks as per the table" is 8,848 − 5,642 = 3,206 metres. (d) 2013 − 20 = 1993. The plan keeps the book's data; the borders in brackets are written with "and" instead of a dash.
20. **Certificates (p. 56).** The book says "if the number of certificates were sufficient"; the plan keeps the wording. Chittoor: 121 extra; Jaunpur: 222 short; Raigad: 889 extra.
21. **Real people.** Priyanka Mohite, Virat Kohli and Sachin Tendulkar are named by the book (a table and a runs question). The plan uses them as the book prints them and adds no fact about them; their numbers are the book's.
22. **The book does not number its solved examples.** The plan numbers the seven printed solved examples 1 to 7 in book order (lorry, minibus, North-South Corridor, Mahesh, ship, Talon-Sagres difference, Sukanta).
23. **Unprinted commas.** The book prints 1301 (p. 44) and 2700 km (p. 49) without a comma. The plan writes 1,301 and 2,700.
24. **Questions numbered 2 to 9 on p. 45** are eight bare subtractions; the plan treats them as one set lettered (a) to (h).
25. **The unit l.** The book writes litres as l; the plan writes "litres".
26. The QR code on the opener page is not readable from the supplied file; nothing is taken from it.

## SECTION C: Interactive Tool Specifications

Common to every tool (details in sections D and E): one activity at a time; Back and Next always in the same place; progress dots; Hint, Retry, Reset, and (where moving objects) Undo; specific feedback ("The ones make 13: write 3 and carry 1 ten"), never only right or wrong; the mathematical object (cards, chart, number line, grid, table) is the largest thing on screen. Practice values the plan adds are labelled "practice values" and differ from the book's numbers and from the worked example of the tool's own topic; every other number is the book's. Version 3 has 11 tools, numbered as in the content team's table (T1 to T11); the topic number is the topic's number in that table (1 to 17). One tool, one objective. A tool with two activities or modes (Tools 7, 8 and 10) shows one at a time on one shared scene, each with its own instruction and its own feedback, and the second opens after the first is done.

### Tool 1. Balance the Sums
- **Connected topic:** Making Sums Equal (topic 1: the opening topic, free first try right after the hook; the concept video follows it). Page 42.
- **Learning objective:** Students explain how interchanging one number from each group changes both sums, and make two sums equal in the fewest moves.
- **Why a tool:** the opener is a hands-on puzzle ("you could write each number on a small piece of paper"); moving a card and watching both sums change together is what makes the idea visible.
- **Environment:** the back seat of the car on the road trip: a fold-down tray with two paper trays, the number cards written on small pieces of paper.
- **Objects:** two trays of four number cards each, with a live sum under each tray; a gap bar that shows how far apart the sums are; a moves counter; a Fewest moves badge that lights when the student has used the least number of swaps.
- **Controls:** tap one card in each tray, then press Swap (or drag a card onto a card of the other tray); Undo; Check.
- **What the student does:** The hook's picture: puzzle (a) groups 1, 2, 7, 9 and 3, 4, 5, 9 with the sums 19 and 21; instruction "Make the two sums equal." ## SECTION D: Shared Visual Language

- **Overall illustration style:** warm, colourful, polished educational-game illustrations; 3D or isometric where the scene holds objects, soft shadows, clear shapes, clean uncluttered compositions; gentle animation. The numbers, the place value chart, the number line or the grid is always the largest, clearest thing on screen.
- **Chapter theme and environment:** "The Milestone Road Trip". One long road across India with a roadside stop for every tool: the back seat of the car (cards), a fuel station (adding), a road milestone (near-round numbers), a minibus fuel gauge (subtracting), stepping stones with a running total (consecutive numbers), a sequence strip of milestones (pair the ends), a toll plaza board (aligned digits), a long road with round-number signposts (hops), a railway waiting hall floor (grid), a bus stand bench (pairs), a travel information board (tables). The travel is the book's own: fuel, road, rail and sea routes, a map of India, a truck and a ship. Nothing is added that does not help the mathematics.
- **Characters:** only the book's: Nazrana and her friends, Mahesh and his family, Mary, Sukanta, Piku, Namita, Nitin, Jincy, Raghu, Ambrish. They appear to set a situation, never to teach. Priyanka Mohite, Virat Kohli and Sachin Tendulkar are real people named in two questions; they are shown only as text.
- **Colour and visual direction:** warm sand and road grey for the background, milestone cream, fuel-station green and red for objects. One colour per role: the number being worked is orange, a carry or regroup mark is green, a card to look at again is soft amber (never red), a correct result is green.
- **Main visual objects:** number cards on small slips of paper, place value charts with labelled columns, fuel drops, bars and tanks as place value tokens, number lines with hops, milestones, a 3 by 3 tile grid, round counters in pairs, tables.
- **Animation style:** only meaningful movement: cards swap, ten drops merge into one bar when trading, a car hops along the line, the lit window of milestones slides, pairs of counters close up. No decoration that moves.
- **UI layout and navigation:** one activity per screen; instruction strip at the top (one sentence, large); the working area in the middle; Hint, Retry, Reset (and Undo) in a fixed row at the bottom; Back and Next fixed at the bottom corners; progress dots under the instruction. Same placement in all eleven tools.
- **Common interaction patterns:** tap to select, drag to move, a stepper for counts, a Check button that gives specific feedback, a three-step hint ladder with a worked step at the end, Retry for the step, Reset for the tool.

## SECTION E: Learning and Platform Rules

1. Videos explain; tools let the student explore, apply and get feedback. No classroom teacher is given a teaching role anywhere.
2. Every tool has one distinct mathematical purpose. Tools 2, 4 and 7 all work with place value but differ: Tool 2 is the written addition with the regroup of 10 as 1 of the next place, Tool 4 is the written subtraction with the regroup of 1 as 10 of the place on the right (adding back is only a check), Tool 7 is lining up large numbers with no labels, in separate Addition and Subtraction activities. Tool 3 adds with a nearby round number, Tool 8 uses round numbers as stepping stones in two labelled modes, Tools 5 and 6 are the sums of consecutive numbers step by step and by pairing the ends, Tool 10 is even and odd by pairing and the sums of even and odd numbers, and Tool 11 is reading a table.
3. Each tool has three phases, in order: Recall and get ready, Play and apply, NCERT challenge.
4. A tool card that quotes the book uses the book's own questions, with their original wording, numbers and conditions. The plan does not change them and does not invent a challenge where the book has none.
5. Preserve the book's mathematics: its puzzles, its grids, its tables, its distances. Any number the plan adds is labelled "practice values".
6. Do not introduce ideas outside the chapter: no multiplication by a long number, no division, no fractions or decimals, no negative numbers, no "average", no place named beyond the ten thousands, no algebra.
7. One video per book question, on its answer, plus a question card and a solution card; one video per teaching idea; the hook comes first and does not explain the chapter.
8. Answers the book does not print are worked out from the chapter's own ideas and shown as the plan's reading (section A6).
9. Text hygiene: no em or en dash; minus is the true minus sign; students never read "NCERT", "textbook", page numbers or exercise numbers; say "your maths book".
10. Instructions are short, age-appropriate, one at a time; feedback is specific; hint, retry and reset are always present.
11. One visual identity (the milestone road trip) and one navigation layout across all tools.
12. Verify page numbers, figures and numbers against the book's page images before any build.

### F2. Concepts and activities with no separate tool, and why
- **Journey Problems (topic 8; pp. 48, 50, 51, 56):** concept explanation and visual, then the exact question flow of the book (6 questions). A separate tool would largely duplicate the operation practice of Tools 2, 4 and 7 and the estimation of the video: these problems apply the same arithmetic to a story and there is no new object to manipulate.
- **Palindrome Numbers (topic 10; p. 53):** the idea is that the first and last digits match; a short explanation with a two way number plate, then the book's puzzle questions (list the palindromes in three ranges). A tool would add only the typing of numbers, and no unrelated rules are added.
- **Let Us Think: Coins (topic 14) and Let Us Think: A Torch (topic 15; pp. 54, 55):** two unrelated puzzles with their own explanation and picture; they must not be merged. Neither needs an interactive tool: a puzzle video with a think-and-tell pause is enough, and a coin purse or a switch tool would be added only to raise the count.
- **Addition and subtraction are linked (inside topic 4; p. 44):** the idea is a relation between three numbers that one picture in the video shows. Its two question sets cannot move out of the Fuel Gauge Subtractor's concept, so they appear as sentence cards of Tool 4 only; the check by adding is an optional step in Tool 4, and a separate tool would repeat it.
- **Even and Odd Numbers (topic 12; p. 54):** its practice (circle the even numbers, add 2 to 18 and 23) has no tool, because a tool can sit in only one concept and Pairs and Leftovers sits in the next topic; Activity 1 of that tool recaps this topic with practice values.
- **Chapter Revision (topic 17):** revision reuses the existing videos, question practice and tools; there is no separate revision tool. Because a tool can sit in only one concept, the revision topic cannot hold a tool a second time; it carries a journey map with one signpost per major idea instead, as a text summary with short checks (pipeline rule, see section B note).
