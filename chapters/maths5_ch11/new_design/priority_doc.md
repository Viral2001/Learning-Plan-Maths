## SECTION A: Chapter Understanding

### A1. Chapter name
Grandmother's Quilt (Class 5, Maths Mela, Chapter 11). The chapter is about perimeter (the border of a shape) and area (the region a shape covers).

### A2. Main learning objectives
Students will be able to:
1. Find the perimeter of a shape as the length of its whole border, by adding its sides, and by number of equal sides times side length.
2. Say which shapes tile (cover a surface with no gaps and no overlaps) and which do not, and name the region a set of tiles covers as the area.
3. Count tiles to find an area in the units of the tile used, and see that the same region needs different numbers of different tiles.
4. Compare two areas by covering and by counting unit squares on a square grid, and use the unit square (1 unit square, 1 square cm) as the measure of area.
5. Explain that area and perimeter are different measures: shapes with the same area can have different perimeters, and shapes with the same perimeter can have different areas, so a bigger area does not always mean a bigger perimeter.
6. Find the area of a rectangle as length × breadth and its perimeter as 2 × length + 2 × breadth, and the area and perimeter of a square as length × length and 4 × length.
7. Use the rules in real situations: a missing side from the area and one side, a side marked in parts, objects measured with a scale.
8. Find the perimeter of a figure of square tiles by counting its outer edges, and use it to play the perimeter 24 game.

### A5. Natural learning sequence
The quilt and its border (a free try with the lace) -> perimeter -> tiles that cover without gaps -> area as the region covered, counted in tile units -> comparing areas and the unit square -> area and perimeter are different measures -> area of a rectangle, perimeter of a rectangle, the square -> using the rules -> the tile game -> revision. This is the book's own order. The only changes: the two Let Us Do questions about perimeter (draw rectangles of perimeter 26 cm and 18 cm) are practised with the perimeter of a rectangle, where the idea they need (length + breadth is half the perimeter) is learnt; the mats question (Q4 of the first Let Us Do) and Trisha's question sit with "area and perimeter are different measures", where the idea of the answer is learnt.

### A6. Points the textbook leaves unclear (to confirm; the plan does not guess)
1. **The quilt has no numbers on it.** The plan counts the picture: 15 patches along the long side and 10 along the short side (150 patches). Each patch side is taken as 1 unit (the laces are in units). The border is 15 + 10 + 15 + 10 = 50 units, so the green lace (50 units) covers it exactly; the red lace (40 units) and the blue lace (25 units) are too short.
2. **"Two different coloured laces: how much of each kind?"** does not say how the border is shared between the two laces. The plan gives a sample: one lace for the two long sides (15 + 15 = 30 units) and the other for the two short sides (10 + 10 = 20 units), for example red 30 units and blue 20 units (both reels are long enough). Any two lengths that make 50 units and fit the reels are right (for example blue 25 units and 25 units of red). The tool accepts any such split. To be confirmed.
3. **LUD p. 142 Q1, the shape with 5 cm.** The picture is a bow-tie (two triangles meeting at a point: a top line, a bottom line and two crossing lines) although the text says all sides are equal. The plan reads it as a shape with 4 equal sides of 5 cm, perimeter 4 × 5 = 20 cm. The pentagon with 4 cm sides has 5 × 4 = 20 cm. The plan letters the two shapes (a) pentagon, (b) the 5 cm shape.
4. **LUD p. 142 Q2, "Draw two rectangles each having the following perimeters (a) 26 cm (b) 18 cm"** is read as two rectangles for each perimeter. The answers are open (many pairs of length and breadth); the plan gives one sample pair for each and says the student's answer may differ.
5. **"Which of the above objects covered the region completely?" (p. 143)** depends on the student's own table. The plan answers with the idea (all four objects are rectangles, and rectangles tile) and says that whether one covers the table exactly depends on the table.
6. **The counts on the rug and the tables are not printed.** The plan counts the pictures: the rug 15 by 6 = 90 patches; Table 1 has 5 by 2 squares each cut along a diagonal = 20 triangles; Table 3 has 4 by 2 = 8 squares; Table 4 has 3 by 4 = 12 rectangles; Table 2 has 8 circles and leaves gaps.
7. **The desk (p. 144).** The book prints no answers. The plan reads the grid squares as unit squares: the desk is 9 by 6 = 54 squares; the blue tile is one square (54 blue squares); the green triangle is half a square, so 108 green triangles; the red triangle has two sides of 3 squares, so 2 red triangles make a 3 by 3 square of 9 squares and the desk holds 6 such squares: 12 red triangles. The book's blanks are "(a) Green triangles, (b) Red triangles, (c) Blue squares" in that order.
8. **The mats (LUD p. 145 to 147, Q4).** The size of the patch is not stated. The drawn patch is exactly as large as one 1 cm square of the grids on the same pages, so the plan takes a patch as 1 cm by 1 cm. Mat (a) is 5 by 2 = 10 patches (10 square cm, perimeter 14 cm), mat (b) is 4 by 3 = 12 patches (12 square cm, perimeter 14 cm). The two mats need different numbers of patches and have the same perimeter.
9. **Palm and leaves (LUD p. 146 Q2 and Q3)** use the student's own palm and leaves; there is no single answer, and the book does not say how to count a part square. The plan gives the method (count whole squares, then put the part squares together to make about whole squares) and a sample with practice values, and says the answer may differ.
10. **The gardens (LUD p. 145 Q1).** The pale column between and beside the two gardens carries no label; the plan treats it as a path that is part of neither garden. Garden A is 2 by 5 = 10 square cm and garden B is 4 by 3 = 12 square cm. The book writes the unit as "cm square" here and as "square cm" in its rules; the plan writes "square cm" everywhere (same meaning).
11. **LUE p. 147 Q1 and Q2.** Counted cell by cell: Q1 shapes (a), (b), (c), (d), (f) each have area 12 squares and shape (e) has 8; their perimeters are 16, 18, 14, 14, 26 and (e) 18. Q2: all five shapes have area 7; perimeters 12, 12, 16, 16, 16, so two groups have the same perimeter: (a) with (b), and (c) with (d) and (e). The plan shows both groups.
12. **The patchwork figure (p. 150)** labels "Length" under the 4-patch side and "Breadth" beside the 6-patch side, but the text says "6 is the length of one side and 4 is the length of the non-equal side" and writes 6 cm × 4 cm. The product is the same. The plan follows the text (length 6, breadth 4) and does not rely on which side carries the word.
13. **The square on p. 151** is introduced as a question ("What will happen if all the sides of a rectangle are equal, that is, the case of square? Let us think about a square whose sides are 5 units long") with the working printed. The plan makes it a worked example question: area and perimeter of a square of side 5.
14. **LUD p. 152 Q2.** Each shape has two blanks (length and breadth, ten blanks in all); the plan asks for length, breadth, area and perimeter of each of the five shapes (a) to (e). Counted: (a) 6 by 6, (b) 4 by 7, (c) 12 by 4, (d) 3 by 3, (e) 6 by 5.
15. **LUD p. 153 Q3 (the table) and p. 151 Q1 (the classroom floor)** need measurements of real objects; the table has two empty rows (5 and 6) for the student's own objects. The plan gives a method and a sample with practice values, and says the answer may differ.
16. **LUD p. 153 Q6 (the figure with 32 cm, 6 cm, 12 cm).** The figure is not drawn to scale and has dotted lines with no numbers. The plan reads one rectangle 32 cm wide whose height is 6 cm and 12 cm in two parts: height 6 + 12 = 18 cm, area 32 × 18 = 576 square cm (checked by the two parts: 32 × 6 + 32 × 12 = 576).
17. **Let Us Play (p. 154).** The rules do not say what happens when the perimeter goes past 24, or whether a tile must touch the figure along a whole side. The plan follows the pictures (tiles touch edge to edge, the tiles are not moved) and lets the perimeter come back down by adding a tile in a notch. The perimeter must be exactly 24 to win. To be confirmed.
18. The book assumes perimeter is known ("Recall that..."). The plan teaches it in one short concept anyway, using only the book's idea.
19. The LUD p. 144 (the desk) has no question number and one question with three parts; LUD p. 145 and the other boxes restart the numbering Q1, Q2, ... in each box. The plan keeps each question's own number in its label.
20. The QR code on the opener page cannot be read from the supplied file; nothing is taken from it. The "Note for Teachers" boxes (pp. 145 and 151) are kept as coverage ids; their ideas are used in the concepts, and no teacher is given a role.

## SECTION C: Interactive Tool Specifications

Common to every tool (details in sections D and E): one activity at a time; Back and Next always in the same place; progress dots; Hint, Retry, Reset, and (where objects move) Undo; specific feedback ("The red lace ends after 40 units; the border is longer"), never only right or wrong; the mathematical object is the largest thing on screen. Practice values the plan adds are labelled "practice values"; every other number is the book's.

### Tool 1. Quilt Lace
- **Connected topic:** Grandmother's Quilt (the opening topic, free first try right after the hook; the concept video follows it). Page 142.
- **Learning objective:** Students lay lace along the border of a quilt, count the units all the way round, and decide which lace is long enough, so that the length of the whole border is felt before it is named.
- **Why a tool:** the border has to be walked edge by edge to see what "all the way round" counts, and running out of lace is felt, not told.
- **Environment:** Grandmother's sunny room: the quilt spread on the floor, three lace reels on a stool beside it.
- **Objects:** the quilt of 15 patches by 10 patches (each patch side is 1 unit, as in the book's picture) with a pink rim; three reels with the book's lengths: red 40 units, green 50 units, blue 25 units, each lace marked in units; a counter "units laid"; scissors; a second reel slot for two-lace mode.
- **Controls:** tap a reel to pick it; drag the end of the lace along the border (it snaps to patch edges, one unit per patch side, and turns at the corners); Cut; Undo; a "Two laces" switch.
- **What the student does:** The hook's picture, Grandmother's quilt and the three reels; one line: "Tap the reel you think will go all the way round the border." (1) lay the red lace along the border: it runs out after 40 units; (2) try the blue: it runs out after 25; (3) try the green: it fits round exactly, and the counter shows the units on each side; (4) practice values: a small quilt of 6 patches by 4 patches: tap every outer edge and count the border; (5) count the patches along the border and count the unit lengths along the border: the corner patches show why the two counts differ. The book's questions unchanged as an open challenge: "Tick the lace option that would cover the entire border of the quilt." and "She decides to use two different coloured laces. How much lace of each kind will be needed to cover the entire border?" The student cuts two laces that together go round exactly; any split that makes the whole border and fits the reels is accepted.
- **Feedback and hints:** "The red lace has 40 units. The border is longer, 10 units still to cover." Hints: where does the border start and end; how many units along one side; add the four sides; bottom-out lays the first side.
- **Retry and reset:** Retry picks up the lace of this step; Reset returns the quilt and the reels.

### Tool 2. Tile the Table
- **Connected topic:** Covering a Surface with Tiles. Pages 143 to 144.
- **Learning objective:** Students find which shapes cover a surface without gaps or overlaps, and count the tiles that cover a region as its area in that tile's units.
- **Why a tool:** dropping tiles shows gaps appearing, and covering one region with different tiles shows that the count changes while the region stays the same.
- **Environment:** Grandmother's room table corner: four table tops in a row (Tables 1 to 4) and Preetha's desk, with a shelf of loose tiles.
- **Objects:** Table 1, a 5 by 2 table top that takes triangles (each square cut along a diagonal); Table 2, a table top for circles (4 by 2, 8 circles); Table 3, a 4 by 2 top for squares; Table 4, a 3 by 4 top for rectangles; the desk, a 9 by 6 grid of unit squares, with a blue square tile, a green triangle tile (half a square) and a red triangle tile (a right triangle with two sides of 3 squares); a tile shelf; a gaps glow; a tile counter.
- **Controls:** tap a tile on the shelf, then tap places on the table to put it down (a "fill the rest" button repeats the tile); a tile counter shows how many tiles are used; Undo.
- **What the student does:** The rug of Grandmother made of square patches (15 across, 6 down); one line: "Which shapes can cover a table with no gaps and no overlaps?" (1) cover Table 1 with triangles and read the count, 20; (2) cover Table 3 with squares, 8; (3) cover Table 4 with rectangles, 12; (4) put circles on Table 2: gaps glow, 8 circles; (5) practice values: a tray of 6 by 4 covered by 24 squares, then each square cut along a diagonal into 48 triangles. The book's items unchanged: the sentences "They found that ___, ___ and ___ shapes cover the top of the table without gaps and overlaps. ___ shape leaves gaps. ___ triangles cover Table 1. ___ squares cover Table 3. ___ rectangles cover Table 4." and the area sentences; and Preetha's desk: "how many of the following shapes will cover the desk: green triangles, red triangles, blue squares".
- **Feedback and hints:** "The circles leave gaps, so they do not tile." "20 triangles cover Table 1, so its area is 20 triangle units." Hints: are there gaps; are there overlaps; count the tiles; bottom-out fills the table.
- **Retry and reset:** Retry clears the table in front; Reset returns all the tables.

### Tool 3. Grid Area
- **Connected topic:** Comparing Shapes. Pages 144 to 146.
- **Learning objective:** Students compare areas by laying shapes on a square grid and counting unit squares, and see why a common unit square is needed.
- **Why a tool:** overlaying shapes shows when one fits inside another, and the grid takes over when it does not; the count on the grid is the measure.
- **Environment:** a patchwork board (a square grid mat) on the table with paper cut-outs beside it.
- **Objects:** cut-outs A (3 by 4), B (2 by 4) and C (2 by 5) of unit squares; the gardens of the book (garden A 2 by 5, garden B 4 by 3, with the 1 cm path); a square grid with a "1 unit square" key; a counter; a trace pad on which a student outlines any shape (the palm, a leaf) and taps the squares inside.
- **Controls:** drag a cut-out onto another to overlay it; drag a cut-out onto the grid; tap a square to count it (it turns orange and the counter goes up); a "show unit squares" switch; on the trace pad, draw with a finger and tap whole squares and part squares.
- **What the student does:** The picture of the desk and its different tiles; one line: "Which of these three rectangles has the largest area?" (1) overlay B on A: B fits inside A; (2) overlay B on C: B fits inside C; (3) overlay A and C: neither fits inside the other; (4) put A and C on the grid and count: 12 and 10; (5) practice values: a rectangle of 7 by 2 and one of 4 by 4: count 14 and 16; (6) practice values: a right triangle with two sides of 4 squares: count 6 whole squares and 4 half squares. The book's items unchanged: "Which of the above rectangles has the largest area?" with A, B, C on the grid; "Compare the areas of the two gardens given below on the square grid"; then the palm and the leaves on the trace pad.
- **Feedback and hints:** "B fits inside A, so A has the larger area." "A has 12 unit squares and C has 10, so A is larger." Hints: does one shape fit inside the other; what could you count that is the same for both; count the squares in each row; bottom-out counts the first shape.
- **Retry and reset:** Retry returns the shapes of this step; Reset clears the board.

### Tool 4. Shape Builder
- **Connected topic:** Area and Perimeter Are Different. Pages 147 to 150.
- **Learning objective:** Students build shapes on a square grid and read the area and perimeter side by side, to see that one can stay the same while the other changes.
- **Why a tool:** the two numbers move differently as squares are placed; seeing them together is the whole idea, and drawing many shapes on paper is slow.
- **Environment:** Grandmother's patchwork board: a big grid with a box of unit patches.
- **Objects:** a grid of 15 columns by 12 rows of unit squares; a box of patches; an "area" counter (squares) and a "perimeter" counter (outer edges, with the outline drawn thick); a shelf that keeps up to 3 saved shapes with their two numbers; the book's shapes preloaded in challenges.
- **Controls:** tap a square to place or remove a patch; drag across squares to place several; "Save shape" to the shelf; "Show outline" switch; Clear.
- **What the student does:** A 2 by 3 rug of patches with area 6 and perimeter 10 shown; one line: "Count the patches inside, then the edges round the outside." (1) practice values: build three rectangles of 16 squares, 4 by 4, 8 by 2 and 16 by 1: perimeters 16, 20 and 34; (2) build three rectangles of perimeter 20: 5 by 5, 7 by 3 and 9 by 1: areas 25, 21 and 9; (3) build a shape that is not a rectangle with 16 squares and read its perimeter; (4) find two shapes where the area goes up and the perimeter goes down: 1 by 8 (area 8, perimeter 18) and 3 by 3 (area 9, perimeter 12). The book's items unchanged: Trisha's two rectangles (2 by 3 and 3 by 4) and her claim "I increased the area of my rectangle, and the perimeter increased. Do you think this is always true?"; "Tick the shapes with the same area. Find the perimeters of these shapes. What do you notice?"; "Tick the shapes with the same perimeter. Find the areas of these shapes. What do you notice?"; "Draw different shapes having the same area as the given shape. Write the perimeter of each shape. What do you notice?"
- **Feedback and hints:** "Both shapes have 16 squares, but the perimeters are 16 and 20." Hints: count the squares; count the outside edges; try a longer, thinner shape; bottom-out builds the first shape.
- **Retry and reset:** Retry clears the grid of this step; Reset clears the grid and the shelf.

### Tool 5. Rectangle Maker
- **Connected topic:** Area and Perimeter of Rectangles and Squares. Pages 150 to 152.
- **Learning objective:** Students find the area of a rectangle as rows times patches in a row (length × breadth) and its perimeter by adding the four sides (2 × length + 2 × breadth), and see that a square is a rectangle with equal sides.
- **Why a tool:** changing the length and the breadth and watching the rows fill and the border sum update turns the rules into something seen.
- **Environment:** Grandmother's rug frame, where patches are laid in rows and columns.
- **Objects:** a rectangle whose length and breadth are set from 1 to 12; unit patches that fill the rectangle row by row; side labels in cm; an "area" read-out showing rows of patches and the product; a "perimeter" read-out showing the walk round the border as a sum; a Square badge when length and breadth are equal; the grid figures of the book.
- **Controls:** a stepper for the length and a stepper for the breadth; "Fill rows"; "Walk the border"; "Show the rule"; answer boxes that take numbers.
- **What the student does:** 5 rows of 4 patches (practice values) with the count 20 shown; one line: "Rows of patches: how many patches in all?" (1) practice values: make a rectangle 8 by 5: 5 rows of 8 patches, area 40, perimeter 8 + 5 + 8 + 5 = 26; (2) change the breadth to 6 and watch both numbers; (3) make a square 6 by 6: area 36, perimeter 4 × 6 = 24; (4) make a rectangle of perimeter 20 with length 7: breadth 3. The book's items unchanged: "Draw two rectangles each having the following perimeters. (a) 26 cm (b) 18 cm"; "Find the area and perimeter of the following shapes" (the five shapes (a) to (e) of the grid); and the worked ones, 6 cm × 4 cm and the square of side 5.
- **Feedback and hints:** "5 rows of 8 patches make 40 patches, so the area is 40 square cm." Hints: how many patches in one row; how many rows; multiply; bottom-out fills the rows.
- **Retry and reset:** Retry returns the rectangle of this step; Reset returns the first rectangle.

### Tool 6. Perimeter 24 Game
- **Connected topic:** Let Us Play. Page 154.
- **Learning objective:** Students find the perimeter of a figure of square tiles by counting its outer edges, and choose where to add tiles so that the perimeter becomes exactly 24.
- **Why a tool:** the book's game needs tiles and a die; the tool gives every pair a die, a pile of tiles and a counter, and checks the count.
- **Environment:** Grandmother's floor: square tiles on a mat and a die.
- **Objects:** a pile of unit tiles; a die; the growing figure with every outer edge marked 1, as in the book; a perimeter counter; two player tags (two players on one screen taking turns, or one player against the tool); a "perimeter 24" banner.
- **Controls:** Roll; drag tiles of this turn so that they touch the figure along whole edges; tiles are not moved once placed; "Count the edges" to tap each outer edge; Undo this turn; Next player.
- **What the student does:** A figure of 3 tiles in a row (practice values) with its 8 outer edges marked; one line: "Count the outer edges. That is the perimeter." (1) roll and place the tiles in any shape, count the perimeter; (2) add one tile that touches the figure along one edge and see the perimeter go up by 2; (3) add one tile in a corner notch and see the perimeter stay the same; (4) practice values: play to a perimeter of 16. The book's game unchanged: "Find the perimeter of the tiles" (the figure of 4 tiles), then "Do not move the tiles. The second player can take turn and add tiles to the same tiled figure." and "Take turns and add tiles to the same figure till the perimeter becomes 24. The one who makes the perimeter 24 wins the game."
- **Feedback and hints:** "This figure has 10 outer edges, so its perimeter is 10." Hints: which edges are on the outside; count them one by one; does the new tile touch one edge or more; bottom-out places a tile that makes 24.
- **Retry and reset:** Retry undoes this turn; Reset clears the figure.

## SECTION D: Shared Visual Language

- **Overall illustration style:** warm, colourful, polished educational-game illustrations; 3D or isometric where the scene holds objects, soft shadows, clear shapes, clean uncluttered compositions; gentle animation. The shape being measured (the quilt, the table top, the grid, the rectangle) is always the largest, clearest thing on screen.
- **Chapter theme and environment:** "Grandmother's Patchwork Room". Every shape in the book (quilt, rug, tables, desk, grids, mats, tiles) sits on the floor, a table or a board of one sunny, friendly room where Grandmother stitches patchwork. Each tool is a station there: the quilt on the floor with the lace reels, the table tops, the grid board, the shape board, the rug frame, the tile mat. The room is the one environment of the chapter; nothing is added that does not help the mathematics.
- **Characters:** only the book's: Preetha, Adrit, Trisha and their grandmother. They appear to set a situation, never to teach.
- **Colour and visual direction:** warm cream and wood tones for the room; the quilt colours of the book's picture (green with dots, red with a white weave, yellow with spirals, a pink rim); the rug in red and yellow checks; lace reels red, green and blue. One colour per role: the thing being counted (the patches inside for area, the edges outside for perimeter) is orange, the rest is pale cream, correct is green, "look again" is soft amber (never red).
- **Main visual objects:** a square grid with clear 1 unit squares, rectangles of patches in rows, outlined borders with each edge unit visible, tiles, number labels in a large clear font.
- **Animation style:** only meaningful movement: lace slides along the border edge by edge, tiles drop into place, a marker walks round the border, rows of patches fill one row at a time. No decoration that moves.
- **UI layout and navigation:** one activity per screen; instruction strip at the top (one sentence, large); the working area in the middle; Hint, Retry, Reset (and Undo) in a fixed row at the bottom; Back and Next fixed at the bottom corners; progress dots under the instruction. Same placement in all six tools.
- **Common interaction patterns:** tap to select, drag to move, a stepper for lengths, a Check button that gives specific feedback, a three-step hint ladder with a worked step at the end, Retry for the step, Reset for the tool.

## SECTION E: Learning and Platform Rules

1. Videos explain; tools let the student explore, apply and get feedback. No classroom teacher is given a teaching role anywhere.
2. Every tool has one distinct mathematical purpose; no interaction is repeated across tools without a learning reason. Tools 2 and 3 both count tiles but Tool 2 asks which shapes tile and what the tile count means, and Tool 3 compares areas on a unit grid; Tool 4 reads area and perimeter of free shapes, Tool 5 builds rectangles with the rules, Tool 6 is a turn-taking game on the perimeter of tiles.
3. A tool is made from its concept description: the idea, the screen, what the student does, the practice cards, feedback and hints. It is not scripted in phases.
4. A tool card that quotes the book uses the book's own questions, with their original wording, numbers and conditions. The plan does not change them and does not invent a challenge where the book has none.
5. Preserve the book's mathematics: its quilt, tables, desk, grids, mats and tiles. Any number the plan adds is labelled "practice values".
6. Do not introduce ideas outside the chapter: no area of a triangle rule, no area of other shapes, no decimals, no fractions of a unit beyond a half square, no volume.
7. One video per book question, on its answer, plus a question card and a solution card; one video per teaching idea; the hook comes first and does not explain the chapter.
8. Answers the book does not print are worked out from the chapter's own ideas and shown as the plan's reading (section A6).
9. Text hygiene: no em or en dash; minus is the true minus sign; students never read "NCERT", "textbook", page numbers or exercise numbers; say "your maths book".
10. Instructions are short, age-appropriate, one at a time; feedback is specific; hint, retry and reset are always present.
11. One visual identity (the patchwork room) and one navigation layout across all tools.
12. Verify page numbers, figures and numbers against the book's page images before any build.
