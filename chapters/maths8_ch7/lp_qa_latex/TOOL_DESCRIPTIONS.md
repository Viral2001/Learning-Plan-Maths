# maths8_ch7 - tool descriptions (9 tools)

Tool Name: Scale Figure Stretcher

Tool Description:

A 3 by 5 figure sits on a unit grid, with its original outline left behind it as a faint ghost. Beneath it are two sliders, one for the width factor and one for the height factor, each running from 0.5 to 3 in steps of 0.1, a switch that locks the two sliders together, and a control that adds the same length to both sides. Readouts show the two factors, the width to height ratio before and after, and a Same shape indicator above the figure.

Before touching anything the student predicts whether making the figure twice as wide, with the height left alone, still gives the same shape. The indicator stays hidden until that guess is made. Then the width slider goes to 2. The figure stretches sideways against the ghost, the factors read 2 and 1, the ratio moves from 3 : 5 to 6 : 5, and the indicator goes off the instant the factors part. Moving the height slider to 2 brings the ratio back to 3 : 5 and the indicator back on. With the lock on, the indicator is still worked out from the two factors and never from the lock. A figure set to 60 by 40, like image A, reaches 30 by 20 (image C) at 0.5 and 0.5, so shrinking keeps the shape too, 90 by 60 (image D) at 1.5 and 1.5, and the squashed 60 by 60 (image E) at 1 and 1.5.

Then the student tries adding instead of multiplying. Adding 2 to each side of a 3 by 7 figure gives 5 by 9, and cross multiplying 3 : 7 and 5 : 9 gives 27 and 35, which differ. Adding 4 to each side of a 6 by 6 square gives 10 by 10 and the shape survives, but the same move on a 3 by 5 figure gives 7 by 9, so the square is a special case and not a rule. On a 10 by 5 figure, factors 2 and 2.1 switch the indicator off, with the gap and the tolerance of 0.05 printed, so nearly equal is seen not to be equal.

The cards ask for the new width of a 4 by 6 figure scaled by 2 in both directions, whether 2 across and 3 down keeps the shape, the height that goes with a 4 by 7 figure enlarged until its width is 12 (21), and the repair of a 4 by 7 figure stretched to 12 by 19, where a height of 21 puts the shape back. At the end the student writes the condition in a sentence and the tool tests it on two new factor pairs, one matching and one not. Hints ask for new divided by old on the width, then on the height, then whether the two answers agree, and last the matching figure is drawn over the stretched one. Through doing, the student learns that a figure keeps its shape exactly when both sides are multiplied by the same factor, above or below 1, and that adding the same length to both sides changes the ratio.

(tool id M1.S2.T2.C4.2D1, concept M1.S2.T2.C4, topic: Same Factor, Same Shape)

Tool Name: Ratio Mixer

Tool Description:

Two glasses stand side by side on a counter, each built from stacked unit blocks, concentrate in one colour and water in another, with a solid colour swatch above each glass showing its shade. Both start at 2 concentrate to 3 water, so the swatches match. Under each glass are plus and minus buttons for the concentrate blocks (0 to 12) and the water blocks (1 to 12). Shared controls are an Add one to both parts button, a Multiply both parts button (2 to 6, within the 12 block limit), a Show simplest form switch, and a Compare with target button that puts a third swatch above the pair.

The student first predicts: if one spoon of concentrate and one of water go into the right glass, does it still taste the same? Then Add one to both parts is pressed. A block slides onto each stack, the right swatch shifts to a stronger shade, the two swatches are held side by side with the gap marked, and the tool writes the new ratio, 3 to 4, beside 2 to 3. Multiply both parts by 2 is different: the stacks grow to 4 and 6, yet the swatch does not move at all, and the tool holds it still for a moment. The student then builds other glasses, such as 6 to 9 or 8 to 12, and finds that every glass whose simplest form is 2 to 3 shows the same swatch whatever its size. That is the strong filter coffee of 20 mL decoction to 30 mL milk. The swatch comes from the simplest form and never from the total, and Compare with target uses exact cross multiplication, so two mixtures that are close but not equal are reported as different.

The guided cards include 4 to 6 against 2 to 3 (the same, although the first glass holds more), one block added to each part of a 5 to 5 glass (still the same, the only kind where adding keeps the taste), a glass of 4 water blocks and no concentrate, which is plain water, and a glass of 3 concentrate and no water, which the tool refuses because there is no mixture to show. Later cards ask for the water in a glass that must match 2 to 3 with 6 concentrate blocks (9), and for a glass of 20 blocks in all that tastes like 2 to 3 (8 and 12). Hints ask which part changed and whether the other part changed by the same factor, then to compare the two simplest forms, then show both side by side. Through doing, the student learns that a mixture is fixed by the ratio of its parts and not by its size, that multiplying both parts by one number keeps it, and that adding the same amount to both parts changes it.

(tool id M2.S5.T5.C17.2D1, concept M2.S5.T5.C17, topic: Ratios and Their Simplest Form)

Tool Name: Ratio Table Filler

Tool Description:

A table of two rows runs across the screen with the base ratio pinned at the left. It opens on the base 4 to 6, with the first column filled, 10 in the top row of the second column and an empty box beneath it. A curved arrow joins the two columns and carries a blank label slot. Buttons let the student add a column, check the table, and switch on the drift readout.

The student first predicts what must happen to the bottom row when the top row jumps from 4 to 10. A number is then typed into the empty box. The tool checks the whole column and not only the cell: 10 over 15 is in the ratio 4 to 6, so the column lights up in the main accent. Next the student writes on the arrow how the column was made. Here the multiplier is 5/2, because 4 does not divide 10, and it stays a stacked fraction. A right number with a wrong label is accepted as a cell, but the label is replaced by the true operation, so an answer reached by luck is caught. To see a broken table the student adds a column 8 and 12 and then builds the next one by adding 4 to both rows. The column 12 and 16 turns to the warning colour, its ratio slides from the base value to its own value, and the gap is printed as a number. The cross products of the base and the new column are drawn as two diagonals, and they differ.

Columns far apart have no arrow, so the student works out the multiplier alone: with a second column of 6 and 15 and a later column with 14 on top, the multiplier is 7/3 and the blank is 35. The base can be set to 14 : 21 to fill 28 : 42 (twice each term), 6 : 9 (3/7 of each term) and 2 : 3 (divide both by 7), or to 6 : 10 for the lemonade, which reaches 18 : 30 with a factor of 3. The practice cards give a base of 2 to 3 with 8 on top (12), the column 6 and 15 against a base of 2 to 5, a table on 3 to 4 in which the column 12 and 15 does not belong, and a distant column with 40 on top after a second column of 15 and 24 (64). Hints ask what the top row was multiplied by, then to apply the same factor to the bottom, then the filled column is shown. Through doing, the student learns that a column belongs in a ratio table only when both entries are multiplied or divided by the same number, that the number may be a fraction, and that adding to both rows breaks the ratio.

(tool id M2.S9.T9.C33.2D1, concept M2.S9.T9.C33, topic: Finding a Missing Term)

Tool Name: Double Number Line

Tool Description:

A pair of number lines is drawn one above the other, kilograms on the top and rupees below, both starting at 0, with their ticks locked into vertical columns by a base pair of 3 kg and 90 rupees. A dashed column marks the base pair, and a marker on each line is joined to its partner by a vertical connector, so a pair is one object. Controls let the student drop a labelled tick, change the base pair, jump to the unit tick, show or hide the multiplier, and type a multiplier to be graded.

The student first marks where 7 kilograms will fall before dragging anything. Then the kilograms marker is dragged to 7 and the rupees marker follows to 210. Two identical curved arrows write times 7/3, one over the top line and one under the bottom line, which shows that one number moves both lines. A marker cannot be moved alone. Dragging back to half the base pair gives 1.5 kg and 45 rupees with times 1/2, so fractions work the same way. The jump to the unit tick takes the kilograms marker to 1 and the rupees marker to 30, found by dividing and not by a separate rule. Pulling a marker past the end of the line makes both lines stretch together, and the tool says the scale changed.

Wrong ideas are drawn and not just refused. With 4 kg for 72 rupees, a student who marks 7 kg by adding 3 to both numbers proposes 7 and 75. The proposed dots are joined by a ghost segment that slants off the locked column, and the true pair, 7 kg and 126 rupees, is placed beside it. A base pair with a zero in it is refused, with two different scales drawn faintly to show that 0 and 0 fix none.

The same lines hold the mid-day meal when the base pair is set to 120 students and 15 kg of rice: dragging the students marker to 80 sends the rice marker to 10, with times 2/3 on both lines, which is 120 : 15 :: 80 : 10. The cards give 4 kg for 84 rupees (12 kg costs 252), 8 kg of wheat for 208 rupees (5 kg costs 130), and 4 kg of sugar for 84 rupees (273 rupees buys 13 kg). The student names the multiplier before checking, and it is graded apart from the partner value. Hints ask what the new amount is a multiple of, then what the other line must be multiplied by, then the multiplier is printed on both lines. Through doing, the student learns that moving to a new value on one line multiplies the other by the same factor, and that finding one unit is the same move with a factor below 1.

(tool id M3.S13.T13.C51.2D1, concept M3.S13.T13.C51, topic: The Rule of Three)

Tool Name: Additive Vs Multiplicative Judge

Tool Description:

A situation card is pinned across the top of the screen, with a table of pairs beneath it and two verdict buttons, Proportional and Not proportional. The table has a ratio column and a difference column, so the student can see which of the two stays fixed. The card opens on Neelima and her mother: when Neelima is 3, her mother is 30.

The student reads the situation and commits a verdict before any test unlocks. No answer is given yet, because the test the student then chooses is checked as well. Nine years later Neelima is 12 and her mother is 39. The table shows 3 : 30, which is 1 : 10, and 12 : 39, which is 4 : 13. The ratio column changes while the difference column holds at 27. The student runs the doubling test, which doubles one quantity and works out the other from the situation. Doubling Neelima's age from 3 to 6 does not give a mother of 60, because she is 33. The two results are drawn as bars from one baseline, with the shortfall of 27 spanned by a labelled bracket. A single pair never settles anything, and the tool asks for a second pair before it gives a verdict.

The next situations are put through the same test. Lemonade at 6 glasses to 10 spoons becomes 18 glasses and 30 spoons, a factor of 3, and doubling to 12 glasses gives 20 spoons, so the ratio stays 3 : 5 and the verdict is proportional. Adding 12 to both gives 22 spoons, shown failing. The mid-day meal, 120 students for 15 kg, becomes 80 students for 10 kg. For the motorcycle trip at 50 km per hour for 2 hours, the student tries 50 : 2 :: 75 : ? and gets 3 hours, which is more time at a higher speed and cannot be right. Doubling the speed to 100 km per hour should halve the time to 1 hour. The distance of 100 km stays fixed, so the time at 75 km per hour is 100 divided by 75, which is 4/3 hours or 1 hour 20 minutes. Petrol at 96 rupees a litre is also in the set, as a proportional case.

At the end the student types the test to use every time, and the tool applies it to a new situation. A test such as 'both numbers grow' passes Neelima's ages and is named too weak. Hints ask what happens to the second quantity when the first is doubled, then to write two pairs and compare ratios and differences, then the pairs of the case are laid out. Through doing, the student learns to check the relationship before using the Rule of Three, because adding the same amount to both terms changes the ratio and some quantities change together without being proportional.

(tool id M3.S17.T17.C72.2D1, concept M3.S17.T17.C72, topic: Not Every Change Is Proportional)

Tool Name: Unit Rate Finder

Tool Description:

Each offer is drawn as a long tape bar whose length is its quantity, with the quantity and price written on it. The first screen shows 5 pens for 60 rupees above 8 pens for 88 rupees, both unsplit and with no rates shown. Under the bars are a Compare button, a quantity box that applies to both offers, a switch between cost per item and items per rupee, and a box where the student says which unit was chosen and why.

The student first guesses which offer is the better buy, then drags along each bar to split it into equal parts, five for the first and eight for the second. Unequal parts are impossible. The segments appear from left to right, the first one lights up as the cost of one pen, and the rate writes itself under the bar: 12 rupees and 11 rupees. The student types each rate, and an equivalent fraction is accepted. Compare unlocks only after both rates are in, and then the second offer takes the accent although it costs more in total. Now the student sets the quantity to 40 for both, and the bars restretch to the same width while the ranking holds, 480 against 440. Switching to items per rupee flips which number is larger, and the tool says the ranking has not moved. Rates stay exact fractions and are rounded only for display: 4 soaps for 90 rupees and 6 for 132 are 22.5 and 22, and 22 wins, never a tie made by rounding.

The cards include 4 pens for 60 and 6 pens for 90, both 15 rupees a pen, which the tool reports as equal value and does not force into a winner; two prices of 50 and 80 rupees with no sizes, which cannot be compared; 5 books for 100 against 4 books for 92 (20 and 23, so the first wins in either unit); and 7 for 105 against 12 for 168 (15 and 14), then the cost of 84 items at the better rate, 1176 rupees. Up to three offers can be loaded, so the shampoo sizes can be compared too: the sachet of 6 mL for 2 rupees, the small bottle of 180 mL for 154 and the large bottle of 1000 mL for 540 come to about 0.33, 0.86 and 0.54 rupees for one millilitre, which opens the question of what the cheapest choice costs in plastic waste. Hints ask what one item costs in each offer, then to divide the price by the quantity, then the split bars are shown. Through doing, the student learns that offers of different sizes are compared by the amount for one unit and never by their totals.

(tool id M3.S21.T21.C83.2D1, concept M3.S21.T21.C83, topic: Comparing by Unit Price)

Tool Name: Ratio Sharer

Tool Description:

One long bar stretches across the screen with its total, 1200 rupees, written above it and the ratio 2 to 3 at its far end. Nothing is cut yet. Under the bar are a stepper for the number of equal parts (1 to 24), a draggable group divider, a box for each share, a Wrong route switch, and a Hide the total button for reverse problems.

The student first predicts how many equal parts are needed. Setting the stepper to 5 cuts the bar into five identical segments, and the part value, 240 rupees, appears beneath. Dragging the divider after the second segment tints two groups, with 2 parts and 3 parts counted above the bar. The student types the shares, 480 and 720, and the add-back line shows they come to 1200. A part count that is not 2 plus 3 leaves the groups unable to match the ratio, and the tool says so. Turning on Wrong route draws a second bar cut into 2 pieces, one for each name in the ratio, of 600 rupees each. Two of those pieces and three of those pieces are 1200 and 1800, and the run bursts past the end of the bar, with the overshoot of 1800 printed. Changing the total to 1000 and the ratio to 3 to 4 makes seven parts that do not divide evenly. The part value stays the fraction 1000/7, and the shares 3000/7 and 4000/7 still add to 1000. A ratio of 1 to 2 to 3 on 1200 gives six parts of 200 through the same rule.

Other totals can be set: 12 counters in 3 to 1 (four groups of 3, giving 9 and 3), 42 counters in 4 to 3 (seven groups of 6, giving 24 and 18), and the food cart profit of 4000 in 3 to 1 (groups of 1000, giving 3000 and 1000). Reverse cards leave the total as a question mark with one share ringed: with the ratio 4 to 7 and the smaller share 640 rupees, the part value 160 comes first and then the total 1760, and a first share of 480 in the ratio 3 to 5 gives 1280. Other cards give 1400 in 2 to 5 (400 and 1000), the middle share of 1080 in 1 to 2 to 3 (360), and a student who shares 1500 in 2 to 3 by dividing by 2 and gets shares that come to 3750. Hints ask how many equal parts the ratio makes, then what one part is worth, then the groups are tinted. Through doing, the student learns that sharing in the ratio m to n needs m plus n equal parts, that one part is the total divided by that sum, and that each share is its own number of parts times one part.

(tool id M4.S24.T24.C91.2D1, concept M4.S24.T24.C91, topic: Sharing in a Ratio)

Tool Name: Temperature Twins

Tool Description:

A Celsius thermometer on the left and a Fahrenheit thermometer on the right stand side by side, with a slider for the Celsius reading, a Double button that doubles it, and a table beneath that keeps each pair of readings together with their ratio.

The student begins with a prediction: if 0 degrees Celsius is 32 degrees Fahrenheit, is 50 degrees Celsius equal to 64 degrees Fahrenheit? Then the slider moves. At 25 degrees Celsius the Fahrenheit thermometer reads 77, and the pair goes into the table. At 0 degrees Celsius it already reads 32, and the ratio cell cannot be filled because the Celsius reading is zero, which is the first sign that the two scales do not start together. At 50 degrees Celsius it reads 122, far from the guess of 64. Pressing Double on 10 degrees Celsius sends the Celsius thermometer to 20 and the Fahrenheit one to 68, shown beside the doubled guess of 100. In the table the Fahrenheit to Celsius ratio is 5 at 10 degrees, 3.4 at 20, 3.08 at 25 and 2.44 at 50. A ratio that keeps changing shows that the scales are not in a fixed ratio.

To go the other way the student types a Fahrenheit reading, and the working appears in order: subtract 32 first, then multiply by 5/9. For 77 this is 45 times 5/9, which is 25, and for body temperature, 98.6, it is 66.6 times 5/9, which is 37. The Celsius thermometer moves to the answer so it can be checked against the working.

The six cards are: set 25 degrees Celsius and read the Fahrenheit value; set 0 and read it; double 10 degrees Celsius and say whether the Fahrenheit value doubles; check whether 50 degrees Celsius is 64 degrees Fahrenheit; find the Celsius reading for 77 degrees Fahrenheit; and find it for 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit. Hints go in order: what does each thermometer read when the slider moves? Then: multiply by 9/5 and add 32 to go to Fahrenheit, and subtract 32 first to come back. Then: for 10 degrees Celsius, 9/5 times 10 plus 32 is 50, and for 20 degrees it is 68, not 100. Last, the doubled reading of 68 is shown next to the guess of 100. Through doing, the student learns that Fahrenheit is 9/5 of the Celsius reading plus 32, so doubling one reading does not double the other, and that temperature conversion is not a simple ratio.

(tool id M4.S28.T28.C113.2D1, concept M4.S28.T28.C113, topic: Converting Units)

Tool Name: Unit Bridge

Tool Description:

A reference card of conversions lies at the left of the screen: length (1 metre is 3.281 feet), area (1 square metre is 10.764 square feet, 1 acre is 43,560 square feet, 1 hectare is 10,000 square metres, 1 hectare is 2.471 acres) and volume (1 mL is 1 cc, 1 litre is 1,000 mL). In the middle is a bridge with the old unit on the left bank and the new unit on the right, an amount box, and a second bridge for the Rule of Three that stays locked at first.

The student reads the amount and its unit, for example 2 hectares, and picks the line on the card that links the two units, here 1 hectare is 2.471 acres. That ratio is placed on the bridge, so the unit being crossed is always visible. Dragging the amount across multiplies it by the number of new units in one old unit, and 2 hectares arrive as 4.942 acres. If the conversion is used the wrong way round, 2 hectares lands at about 0.81 acres, far from the expected size, and the tool points out that a hectare is bigger than an acre, so the number of acres must be larger.

The six cards are: convert 2 hectares to acres; convert 5 acres to square feet (217,800); convert a 100 m by 50 m plot to hectares (5,000 square metres, which is 0.5 hectare); find the area of a 200 ft by 500 ft plot in acres; find the manure for that plot if 1 acre needs 10 tonnes; and find the cost of 2400 square feet if 1 acre costs 1,500,000 rupees. For the plot, 200 times 500 is 100,000 square feet, and dividing by 43,560 gives about 2.30 acres. Only then does the second bridge open, because it opens only when both ratios of a proportion use the same unit. The manure is 1 : 10 :: 2.30 : ?, about 23 tonnes. For the cost, the acre is first written as 43,560 square feet, and then 43,560 : 1,500,000 :: 2,400 : ? gives about 82,645 rupees.

Hints go in order: which two units does this card connect? Then: pick the conversion that links them and decide whether to multiply or divide by it. Then: for the plot, 200 times 500 is 100,000 square feet, and 1 acre is 43,560 square feet, so the plot is about 2.30 acres. Last, the reference ratio is placed on the bridge and the converted amount is shown on the new unit. Through doing, the student learns that a unit conversion is a proportion, that quantities must be brought to one unit before the Rule of Three is used, and that a size check catches a conversion applied the wrong way.

(tool id M4.S28.T28.C114.2D1, concept M4.S28.T28.C114, topic: Converting Units)
