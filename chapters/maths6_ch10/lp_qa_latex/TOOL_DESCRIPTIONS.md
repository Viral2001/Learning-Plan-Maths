# maths6_ch10 - tool descriptions (7 tools)

Tool Name: Lift Mover

Tool Description:

The student sees a tall cut-away of Bela's Building of Fun with the floor numbers written down the side, +6 at the top, the Welcome Hall at 0 and the basements below, and a lift standing on the starting floor named by the card. The shaft is drawn one level deeper than the last shop, down to -6, so that every card has room to land. Beside the lift are two large buttons, '+' and '-'. Each press moves the lift exactly one floor, and a press counter keeps two tallies, how many '+' presses and how many '-' presses, with the net movement beside them. Along the bottom a line reads Starting Floor + Movement = Target Floor, with blanks waiting to be filled.

Before touching a button, the student types the floor they expect to reach. Then they press. The lift rides one floor per press, the counter changes, and the line fills in as the presses add up, for example (+3) + (-5) = -2. A '-' press from floor 0 carries the lift below the ground and the new floor turns red, so the student sees that the lift is not stuck at zero and that a '-' press is a number with a direction, not a press that does nothing. If the prediction was wrong, the tool replays the ride floor by floor from the start so the student can see where the count slipped.

The six cards are: start at 0 and press '+' four times (+4); start at +3 and press '-' five times (-2); start at -2 and press '+' six times (+4); start at -4 and press '-' twice (-6); reach floor -3 from floor +2, where the student must decide that five '-' presses are needed; and go from +5 down to -1 and then up to +3. On the last card the counter shows the two legs, -6 and then +4, and the student combines them into one movement, -2, so that (+5) + (-2) = +3. Whenever the presses undo each other, for instance three '+' presses followed by three '-' presses, the lift returns to its floor, the movement reads 0, and the tool names -3 as the inverse of +3.

Hints come in order. First: which button moves the lift up, and which moves it down? Then: every '+' press is +1 and every '-' press is -1, and the movement is the total. Then: count the presses of each button, then add them to the starting floor. Last, the lift's path is shown floor by floor and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a button press is a signed number, that several presses combine into one movement, and that the target floor is the starting floor plus that movement, read off the lift and not from the signs alone.

(tool id M1.S4.T4.C13.2D1, concept M1.S4.T4.C13, topic: Adding Button Presses)

Tool Name: Floor Line

Tool Description:

A single vertical line of floors fills the middle of the screen, +6 at the top, 0 in the middle and the basements below it, each floor a clearly numbered tick. The line runs down to -7 so that every card fits. Two draggable markers, Start and Target, sit on the line. Between them the tool draws a movement arrow, green and pointing up for a climb and red and pointing down for a descent, with its length written on it. Above the line is a small panel with the two floor numbers and an empty box between them for the sign '<' or '>', and below it a line that reads Target - Start = Movement.

In the movement cards the student drags Start and Target to the floors named on the card, counts the steps along the arrow and types the movement with its sign. The tool then fills in Target - Start = Movement. For Start +2 and Target -3 the arrow points down for five floors, so the movement is -5. A wrong movement shows the arrow's real length and direction. A student who types 5 sees that the arrow points down and needs a minus sign, and a student who works out Start - Target and gets +5 sees that the arrow goes the other way.

In the comparison cards the student drags the two markers to the floors, decides which marker is higher in the building, and chooses '<' or '>' for the box. For -3 and -5 the marker at -3 is higher, so -3 > -5, even though 5 is the bigger digit. A wrong choice highlights the higher floor, and the student sees that -5 sits deeper in the building. For -7 and 0 the student sees that every negative floor lies below the ground floor, so -7 < 0. The six cards are: Start +2 and Target -3 (-5); Start -4 and Target +1 (+5); Start -2 and Target -5 (-3); compare -3 and -5; compare -7 and 0; and find the movement from -6 to +4 (+10).

Hints come in order. First: which floor is higher in the building, and which is lower? Then: the movement is the target floor minus the starting floor, and its sign shows the direction. Then: count the steps from Start to Target and give them a plus if you go up and a minus if you go down. Last, the arrow shows the movement and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a higher floor is a greater number, that of two negative floors the one nearer to 0 is the greater, and that moving between two floors has both a size and a direction.

(tool id M1.S7.T7.C36.2D1, concept M1.S7.T7.C36, topic: Comparing Floors)

Tool Name: Number Line Walker

Tool Description:

A wide horizontal number line from -10 to +10 stretches across the screen, with 0 marked in a stronger colour and a small walker standing on it. Two round handles, Start and Target, can be dragged along the line and snap to whole numbers. Under the line are a box for the student's predicted movement and the empty sum Start + Movement = Target. Small labels at the two ends remind the student that right is the positive direction and left is the negative direction, just as up and down were in the lift.

Each card names two numbers. The student first types the movement they expect, then drags Start and Target to the numbers. The walker then goes from Start to Target one step at a time, with the steps counted on screen. A walk to the right draws a green arrow and the movement has a plus sign. A walk to the left draws a red arrow and the movement has a minus sign. The sum fills in, for example 9 + (-6) = 3 for the walk from 9 to 3, and once the card is solved the matching subtraction 3 - 9 = -6 is written beneath it. A wrong movement shows the walker's real path and its length, so a student who typed 6 for the walk from 9 to 3 sees that the walker went left and the movement is -6.

The cards are: from 3 to 8 (+5); from 9 to 3 (-6); from 3 to -2 (-5), where the walker crosses 0; from -4 to -9 (-5); and from -7 to 2 (+9), where the walker crosses 0 going right. The last card asks the student to place -3, 0, -8 and 4 on the line in order, smallest on the left. Four number chips are dropped on the line, and the tool then reads them from left to right, -8, -3, 0, 4, and writes -8 < -3 < 0 < 4.

Hints come in order. First: is the target to the left or to the right of the start? Then: right is positive and left is negative, so the movement takes the sign of the direction. Then: count the steps from the start to the target. Last, the walker's path is shown with the movement and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that the number line carries on to the left of 0, that smaller numbers lie to the left of bigger ones, and that the movement is the number that makes Start + Movement = Target.

(tool id M2.S18.T18.C78.2D1, concept M2.S18.T18.C78, topic: Back to the Number Line)

Tool Name: Token Board

Tool Description:

The student faces a flat board with two token trays beside it. The green tray holds tokens worth +1 and the red tray holds tokens worth -1, and neither tray runs out. Under the board are a Cancel Pairs button, an answer box for the total and a readout that counts how many green and how many red tokens are on the board. Each card names a sum, for example (+5) + (-3).

The student predicts the total first, then drags tokens onto the board, five green and three red for the first card, and the tokens settle into two groups. Every green token that has a red partner is joined to it by a dashed link, so the zero pairs can be seen before anything is removed. Each press of Cancel Pairs removes one green and one red token together, with a small flash to show that the pair is worth zero. The student keeps cancelling until one colour is gone, reads what is left, two green tokens, and types +2. A wrong total shows the tokens left on the board, so the student can count them and read their colour. If only one colour is on the board, Cancel Pairs says there is nothing to cancel.

The six cards are: (+5) + (-3), giving +2; (+4) + (-7), giving -3, where red tokens are left over; (-6) + (+6), where every token cancels and the board ends empty at 0; (-5) + (-2), where there is no zero pair at all and the answer is the seven red tokens, -7; and (+8) + (-3) + (-4), where the seven red tokens cancel against seven of the eight green ones and one green is left, +1. The last card works backwards: show -3 using exactly five tokens, which the student builds as four red and one green and checks by cancelling the pair. A solved card shades the zero pairs that went and writes the sum.

Hints come in order. First: what do a green token and a red token make together? Then: pair each green token with a red token, because pairs make zero. Then: count the tokens that have no partner and read their colour. Last, the zero pairs are shaded and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a green and a red token cancel to zero, that taking out zero pairs does not change the value, and that the sum is what is left, with its sign given by the colour.

(tool id M3.S24.T24.C109.2D1, concept M3.S24.T24.C109, topic: Adding with Tokens)

Tool Name: Token Taker

Tool Description:

A board already holds the green and red tokens of the first number in a subtraction, such as five green tokens for (+5) - (+6). On its right is an empty Take Away area, and below it an Add Zero Pair button that puts one green and one red token on the board together. A total readout shows the value of the tokens on the board. The Take Away area shows how many tokens of which colour the subtraction asks for.

The student predicts the result, then drags the tokens named by the subtraction into the Take Away area. For (+5) - (+6) the area asks for six green tokens but the board has only five. It shows that one green token is missing and the Add Zero Pair button glows. When the student presses it, a green and a red token appear, the readout still says +5, and a note says that a zero pair does not change the value. Now six green tokens can be taken. One red token is left, so (+5) - (+6) = -1. The solved card shows the pairs added and the tokens taken. If the student adds more pairs than are needed, the answer stays the same, which shows again that a zero pair changes nothing.

The six cards are: (+5) - (+4), where there are enough tokens and +1 is left; (-7) - (-5), where five of the seven red tokens are taken and -2 is left, and the solved card sets it beside (-7) + (+5), which is also -2; (+5) - (+6), as above; (+4) - (-6), where the board has no red token at all, so six zero pairs are needed and ten green tokens are left, +10, shown beside (+4) + (+6); (-3) - (+2), where two zero pairs are added and five red tokens are left, -5; and (-2) - (-6), where four zero pairs are added and four green tokens are left, +4.

Hints come in order. First: are there enough tokens of the kind you must take away? Then: if not, add zero pairs, because they do not change the total. Then: add the number of zero pairs you need, and then take the tokens away. Last, the zero pairs and the taken tokens are shown and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that subtraction takes tokens away, that a zero pair can be put in whenever more tokens are needed, and that taking away negative tokens leaves a result larger than before.

(tool id M3.S28.T28.C121.2D1, concept M3.S28.T28.C121, topic: Subtracting with Tokens)

Tool Name: Border Grid

Tool Description:

The student works on a square of eight number cells around a greyed centre cell. Four sum readouts sit outside the square, one for the top row, the bottom row, the left column and the right column. The centre belongs to none of them and is marked as not counted. Beside the grid a badge shows the target border sum, which reads '?' on the first two cards. Tapping an empty cell opens a number picker, and a number placed in a cell appears at once in the readouts of the sides it belongs to.

The first two cards come with every cell filled. The first grid is 4, -1, -3 across the top, -3 and 1 at the sides of the blank centre, and -1, -1, 2 along the bottom. The second is 5, -3, -5 / 0, blank, -5 / -8, -2, 7. The student works out what each side adds to and types it in. A wrong sum is corrected with the real sum shown. The first grid gives 0 on all four sides and the second gives -3 on all four, so the student names the border sum, 0 and then -3, and sees that the four sides always agree.

The next three cards give a grid with some cells filled and a border sum of +4, then -2, then -4. The student taps an empty cell and sets its number. Tapping a cell lights up the sides it belongs to, so the student sees that a middle cell is in one side and a corner is in two. A side that does not match the border sum turns red and shows what it really adds to, and a side that matches turns green. When a side holds 4 and -1 and the border sum is 0, the blank must be -3, because 4 + (-1) = 3. On the last of these cards the student fills the grid for -4, presses Keep, and then finds a second filling. The two grids sit side by side with the cells that differ outlined, and the tool points out that they always differ in a corner, since a corner is shared by two sides. The sixth card lets the student fill all eight cells to make a grid of their own and read its border sum, and then hide a few cells to make a puzzle for a friend.

Hints come in order. First: which sides does the blank cell belong to? Then: each side must add to the border sum. Then: the border sum minus the two known numbers on that side gives the blank. Last, the correct number is shown and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that all four sides share one border sum, how to find a missing number from it, and why some grids can be filled in more than one way.

(tool id M4.S36.T36.C175.2D1, concept M4.S36.T36.C175, topic: Hollow Integer Grids)

Tool Name: Integer Snakes and Ladders

Tool Description:

A long board runs from -50 at one end through 0 in the middle to +50 at the other, drawn as a winding path of squares with a few ladders and snakes marked on it. Two pawns stand on 0. Beside the board is a pair of dice, a green die showing +1 to +6 and a red die showing -1 to -6, a Roll button, and three result buttons: the sum, the first number minus the second, and the second number minus the first.

On a turn the student presses Roll and the two numbers appear, for example +4 and -5. Each result button then shows the expression it stands for. The sum is (+4) + (-5) = -1, the first minus the second is (+4) - (-5) = +9, and the second minus the first is (-5) - (+4) = -9. The student chooses one, and the pawn walks that many squares: towards +50 for a positive result and towards -50 for a negative one. The chosen result is written as an expression so the move can be checked. If the pawn stops on the foot of a ladder or the head of a snake, it slides to the new square, which is named on screen. The two players take turns, a friend can play the second pawn, and the first pawn to reach +50 or -50 wins. A finished game shows the number of moves taken.

Three set-roll cards come first. On +4 and -5 the student chooses the move that goes furthest from 0, which is +9 or -9. On +3 and -2 the student chooses the move that stays near 0, the sum +1, and sees that the other two results are +5 and -5. On +6 and -1 the student types all three results, +5, +7 and -7, before the buttons confirm them. The fourth card is a game played until a pawn lands on a ladder or a snake, and the fifth is a full game to +50 or -50.

Hints come in order. First: which two numbers did you roll? Then: you may add them or subtract them, in either order. Then: a positive result moves you towards +50 and a negative result towards -50. Last, the three results are shown and a similar roll follows. Through doing, the student learns that one roll gives three possible results, that subtracting a negative number gives a larger positive move, that the two differences are opposites of each other, and that choosing a result is a decision about which way and how far to go.

(tool id M4.S44.T44.C225.2D1, concept M4.S44.T44.C225, topic: Integers: Snakes and Ladders)
