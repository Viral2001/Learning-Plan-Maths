# maths8_ch11 - tool descriptions (10 tools)

Tool Name: Fractal Stepper: Grow the Carpet

Tool Description:

A large square stands in the middle of the screen. Buttons for Step 0, Step 1, Step 2 and Step 3 run along the top, with a Tap Mode switch beside them. A counter panel on the right shows three live numbers: the remaining squares, the holes, and the part of the starting area that is left. At Step 0 it reads 1 square, 0 holes and the whole area.

Every card begins with a prediction. The student types the squares and holes expected at the next step before touching the picture. In Tap Mode the student then makes the step by hand. Tapping a square cuts it into a 3 by 3 grid and removes the centre one, so one square becomes 8 smaller squares and 1 new hole. At Step 2 the student taps the centres of the 8 squares one at a time and watches the holes climb to 9. At Step 3 there are 64 squares to cut, so the student presses Next step instead and the same rule runs on every square at once, giving 512 squares and 73 holes. New holes are drawn in one colour and holes left over from earlier steps in another, so the student can see where each hole came from. At the end the student writes the rule for the squares and for the holes in words.

The six cards ask for the squares and holes at Step 1 (8 and 1), the squares at Step 2 (64), the holes at Step 2 (9), the squares at Step 3 using the rule multiply by 8 (512), the holes at Step 3 by adding the 64 squares of Step 2 to the 9 old holes (73), and the fraction of the starting area left after Step 2. A count that adds 8 instead of multiplying, such as 16 squares at Step 2, is laid next to the real picture and counted square by square. A hole count that forgets the older holes, such as 64 holes at Step 3, lights the earlier holes in the second colour so the missing 9 can be seen. An area answer is checked by shading 8 of every 9 parts at each step, which gives 8/9 and then 64/81.

Hints come in order. First: what happens to one square when the rule is applied to it? Then: every remaining square becomes 8 smaller squares and 1 new hole. Then: multiply the squares of the last step by 8, and add those same squares to the old holes. Last, the counts are shown on the picture and the next card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a fractal grows by one rule repeated on every piece: the squares multiply by 8, the holes grow by the squares of the step before, and each step keeps only 8 of every 9 parts of the area.

(tool id M1.S2.T2.C5.2D1, concept M1.S2.T2.C5, topic: The Sierpinski Carpet)

Tool Name: Triangle Stepper: Grow the Gasket

Tool Description:

An equilateral triangle of area 1 square unit stands on the canvas. Buttons for Step 0 to Step 4 sit above it and a Midpoint tool sits beside it. The counter panel shows the remaining triangles, the holes and the area left, and at Step 0 it reads 1 triangle, 0 holes and area 1.

Each card starts with a prediction typed into a box. Then the student acts. With the Midpoint tool, tapping a triangle marks the midpoints of its three sides, joins them, and shades out the middle triangle, so one triangle becomes 3 remaining triangles and 1 hole. The student does this by hand at Step 1 and Step 2, then presses Next step to run the same rule on every triangle for Step 3 and Step 4. The counters climb through 1, 3, 9, 27, 81 triangles and 0, 1, 4, 13, 40 holes. New holes and old holes are drawn in different colours, and the area left is drawn as a shaded fraction bar under the triangle.

The six cards ask for the triangles and holes at Step 1 (3 and 1), Step 2 (9 and 4), Step 3 (27 and 13), and Step 4 by the rule alone (81 and 40). Then the student gives the area left after Step 2 and after Step 3 (9/16 and 27/64). The last card sets the triangle beside the carpet and asks which has less area left after Step 2, the carpet with 64/81 or the triangle with 9/16. A count that adds 3 each time, such as 6 triangles at Step 2, is shown beside the real picture triangle by triangle. A hole count that forgets the older holes, such as 3 holes at Step 2, lights the hole from Step 1 in a second colour. An area answer is checked by shading 3 of every 4 parts at each step and writing the fraction that is left.

The hints go in order. First: what happens to one triangle when its midpoints are joined and the middle one is removed? Then: every remaining triangle becomes 3 smaller triangles and makes 1 new hole. Then: multiply the triangles of the last step by 3, and add those same triangles to the old holes. Last, the counts are shown on the picture and the next card follows. Through doing, the student learns that the triangles are multiplied by 3, the holes grow by the triangles of the step before, and the area is multiplied by 3/4 each time, so the triangle loses area faster than the carpet.

(tool id M1.S4.T4.C10.2D1, concept M1.S4.T4.C10, topic: The Sierpinski Triangle)

Tool Name: Bump Builder: Grow the Snowflake

Tool Description:

On the left of the screen is an equilateral triangle, with a box above it for the starting side length, set to 1 unit, and a Bump tool beside it. Buttons for Step 0 to Step 3 run along the top. A counter panel shows three live values: the number of sides, the length of each side, and the perimeter. At Step 0 with side 1 it reads 3 sides, length 1 and perimeter 3.

The student predicts the next row of the counter and then acts. With the Bump tool, tapping one side cuts it into three equal parts, raises a small equilateral triangle on the middle part, and removes that middle part. The one side is now 4 sides, each a third as long as the old one. After bumping a side or two by hand, the student presses Next step to bump every side. Step 1 gives 12 sides of length 1/3 and perimeter 4. Step 2 gives 48 sides of length 1/9 and perimeter 16/3. Step 3 gives 192 sides of length 1/27 and perimeter 64/9. The old side and its four new sides can be laid along the same line so the student can compare them.

The six cards are: Step 1 with starting side 1 (12 sides, 1/3 each, perimeter 4); Step 2 (48 sides, 1/9 each, perimeter 16/3); Step 3 sides and perimeter (192 and 64/9); the number the perimeter is multiplied by from Step 1 to Step 2 (4/3); starting side 9, perimeter at Steps 0, 1 and 2 (27, 36 and 48); and the sides at Step 4 (768). A count that adds 3 sides each time, such as 6 sides at Step 1, is shown next to the picture side by side. A perimeter that forgets that every side is a third as long, such as 12 at Step 1, is checked on one side: the four new sides together measure 4/3, not 4. Every perimeter answer is checked by showing the number of sides times the length of one side.

Hints come in order. First: what happens to one side when the bump rule is applied to it? Then: one side becomes 4 sides, each one third as long. Then: multiply the sides by 4, divide the side length by 3, and multiply the two to get the perimeter. Last, the counts are shown on the picture and the next card follows. Through doing, the student learns that the sides are multiplied by 4 and the perimeter by 4/3 at every step.

(tool id M1.S7.T7.C17.2D1, concept M1.S7.T7.C17, topic: The Koch Snowflake and Fractals in Art)

Tool Name: Solid Parts Counter: Prisms and Pyramids

Tool Description:

A solid turns slowly in the middle of the screen. A switch above it chooses Prism or Pyramid, and a slider sets n, the number of sides of the base polygon, from 3 to 12. A counter panel on the right lists faces, edges and vertices, and a small table under it records every solid the student has counted, one row for each choice of solid and n.

Before reading the counter, the student predicts the three counts. Then the student counts on the solid itself. Tapping a face, an edge or a vertex marks it in colour and adds one to the tally, and tapping it again takes it back, so nothing is counted twice. A triangular prism gives 2 triangles and 3 rectangles, so 5 faces, 9 edges and 6 vertices. Switching to Pyramid with n = 4 gives a square pyramid with 5 faces, 8 edges and 5 vertices. Then the student drags the slider and watches which counts change and by how much. For the prism, each extra side adds 1 face, 3 edges and 2 vertices. For the pyramid it adds 1 face, 2 edges and 1 vertex. After a few rows, the table asks the student to write each count in terms of n.

The seven cards ask for the triangular prism, the square pyramid, the pentagonal prism (7 faces, 15 edges, 10 vertices), the hexagonal pyramid (7, 12 and 7), and a prism on a 12 sided polygon (14, 36 and 24), where the student uses the rule before checking. In the sixth card a prism and a pyramid both have a 9 sided base. The student finds which has more edges (27 against 18) and works out faces plus vertices minus edges for each, which is 2 both times. The last card gives a pyramid with 24 edges and asks for the sides of its base, 12, which the student confirms by setting n to 12. A prism count given for a pyramid is shown with the two solids side by side and the extra edges and vertices highlighted. A count that misses the base faces highlights the two bases of the prism or the one base of the pyramid. A rule that does not fit a second value of n is tested on the solid with that n.

Hints come in order. First: which polygon is the base, and how many sides does it have? Then: count the side faces first, one for every side of the base. Then: for a prism add the two bases, for a pyramid add one base, and count the edges and vertices on the base polygon and then on the rest of the solid. Last, the counts are shown on the solid with their rule in n and the next card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a prism on n sides has n + 2 faces, 3n edges and 2n vertices, a pyramid has n + 1 faces, 2n edges and n + 1 vertices, and every count depends on the sides of the base.

(tool id M2.S12.T12.C32.2D1, concept M2.S12.T12.C32, topic: Faces, Edges and Vertices of Prisms and Pyramids)

Tool Name: Net Folder: Will It Make a Cube?

Tool Description:

The student faces an empty square grid with a tray of six unit squares beside it. A Fold button sits under the grid, a gallery strip along the bottom has 11 empty slots, and a switch changes the tool to Cuboid mode. The student drags the six squares onto the grid, edge to edge, to make a flat shape.

Every card begins with a prediction: will this fold into a cube, yes or no? Then the student presses Fold and a 3D animation wraps the squares around the one picked as the bottom. A working net closes into a cube and can be saved to the gallery. If two squares land on the same place, those two faces turn the same colour and the open face of the cube is outlined, so the student sees the clash and the gap together. The gallery refuses a net that is only a turn or a flip of one already saved, and shows the new net turned or flipped onto the saved one.

The first card gives the cross shaped net: a column of four squares A, B, C and D, with a square L and a square R on either side of B. With B as the bottom, the student folds it and names the face opposite the bottom square, which is D, with A opposite C and L opposite R. The second card asks for a row of four with both extra squares on the same side. The student predicts, then folds and sees both extras land on the lid while the bottom stays open. The third card is the staircase of 2, 2 and 2 squares, which folds. The fourth asks for three different working nets that are not crosses, and the fifth for all 11. The tool is done when the six cards are finished and at least 6 nets are saved.

The sixth card switches to Cuboid mode, with boxes for length, breadth and height set to 4, 2 and 1. Six rectangles appear, in three equal pairs: 4 by 2, 4 by 1 and 2 by 1. The student arranges them into a net, and a joined pair of edges with different lengths is highlighted. The total area of the net is read out, 28 square cm, and Fold checks the net.

Hints go in order. First: which square will be the bottom of the cube? Then: a row of four squares wraps around to make four side faces, so where must the top and the bottom go? Then: in a row of four, put one extra square above the row and one below it. Last, a working net is shown folding and the next card follows. Through doing, the student learns that six joined squares fold into a cube only when no two land on the same face, that there are 11 nets, and that a cuboid net needs three pairs of equal rectangles.

(tool id M2.S14.T14.C40.2D1, concept M2.S14.T14.C40, topic: Nets of a Cube and a Cuboid)

Tool Name: Net Roller: Unroll a Cylinder and a Cone

Tool Description:

A cylinder stands on a table in the middle of the screen. Under it are an Unroll slider and a switch for Cylinder and Cone, with a switch to use pi as 22/7. In Cylinder mode two sliders set the base radius and the height. A read-out beside the solid gives the two sides of the rectangle that its curved surface becomes, and when the solid is open the two circles of the net sit above and below that rectangle.

The student first predicts the shape of the net and its measurements, then drags the Unroll slider. The curved wall peels away from the two circles and flattens into a rectangle. A coloured thread runs once round the base circle and then lies along the long side of the rectangle, so the long side is seen to be the distance once round, the circumference, and the short side is the height. For radius 14 and height 20, the read-out gives 88 by 20. For radius 3.5 and height 5 it gives 22 by 5. In Cone mode, sliders set the base radius and the slant length, and the height is shown but not set, because the radius and the slant length already fix it. Unrolling opens the curved surface into a sector with its corner at the apex and a radius equal to the slant length. The read-out gives the sector as a fraction of the full circle of that radius and as an angle. Rolling the sector back closes the seam into the cone.

The seven cards are: cylinder of radius 14 and height 20 (88 by 20); radius 3.5 and height 5 (22 by 5); a rectangle 66 long and 4 tall, where the student types a radius and sees whether the rectangle wraps exactly once round (10.5); cone of base radius 7 and slant 14 (one half); base radius 3 and slant 9 (one third); base radius 4 and slant 5 (four fifths, 288 degrees); and a cone of base radius 6 that unrolls into a quarter circle, where the student finds the slant length (24). A long side taken as the diameter, such as 28 for the first card, is wrapped round the base and falls short of going all the way round. A wrong sector fraction is rolled up and the gap or overlap at the seam is highlighted. A slant length shorter than the radius is rejected, because the slant length is the longest side of the cone.

Hints go in order. First: which part of the solid is unrolled, and which part stays as circles? Then: the curved edge of the net is as long as the circle it was wrapped round. Then: for the cylinder the long side is 2 times pi times the radius, and for the cone the sector fraction is the base radius divided by the slant length. Last, the net is shown with its measurements and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a cylinder is two circles and a rectangle of circumference by height, and that a cone opens into a sector whose arc is as long as the circumference of its base.

(tool id M2.S17.T17.C48.2D1, concept M2.S17.T17.C48, topic: Nets of Other Solids)

Tool Name: Ant Path Unfolder

Tool Description:

A box floats in the middle of the screen with a small ant and a laddu, and each can be dragged onto any face or edge. An Unfold menu on the right lays the faces of the box flat in different ways. On the flat layout, a ruler line joins the ant to the laddu and shows its length. A Fold Back button wraps the layout, and the line, back onto the box.

The student begins with a prediction: should the ant walk straight up and across, or cut diagonally? Then the student picks an unfolding. When the ant and the laddu land on one flat piece, the straight line between them is drawn and measured. A line that stays inside the net is kept. A line that leaves the net turns red and the gap is highlighted, because the ant cannot walk over empty paper. Fold Back shows the same path as a bent line on the box with the same length. As the student tries more unfoldings, a longer line is shown beside the shortest valid one found so far with both lengths.

The six cards use these boxes. First, a cube of side 4 cm with the ant at the centre of a side face and the laddu at the centre of the top face. With the top face laid beside the side face the line is 2 + 2 = 4 cm. Second, a cube of side 6 cm with the ant at the centre of the front face and the laddu at the middle of the edge between the top and the right face. With the right face beside the front face the laddu is 6 across and 3 up, so the line is the square root of 45, about 6.71 cm, while the bent route of 3 up and 6 across is 9 cm. Third and fourth, a box 8 by 4 by 4 with the ant at the centre of the end face and the laddu on the bottom edge of a long face, 2 cm from the end. The student finds an unfolding where the line leaves the net, then the one with the end face beside the long face, where it stays inside as the long side of a right triangle with sides 4 and 2, about 4.47 cm. Fifth, a box 30 by 12 by 12, with the ant 1 cm from the top of one end and the laddu 1 cm from the bottom of the other end, both on the middle line. The route down, along and up is 11 + 30 + 1 = 42 cm, while a slanted unfolding across five faces gives a right triangle with sides 24 and 32, so 40 cm. Last, the student folds back the shortest line and names the faces it crosses.

Hints come in order. First: which faces must the ant walk across? Then: lay those faces flat side by side so the ant and the laddu are on one flat piece. Then: draw the straight line and use a right triangle to find its length. Last, the shortest valid unfolding is shown with its length and the next card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a path on the box has the same length on its net, that the shortest path is a straight line that stays inside a suitable net, and that every unfolding must be compared.

(tool id M2.S19.T19.C55.2D1, concept M2.S19.T19.C55, topic: Shortest Paths on a Cuboid)

Tool Name: Projection Tilter: Sticks and Cards

Tool Description:

A tabletop is drawn in a slanting view with a thin stick lying on it, and a switch above the table chooses Stick or Card. The student drags one end of the stick up. From each end a thin dashed line drops straight down to the table, and the two feet are joined by a bold line on the table, which is the projection. A read-out lists the stick length, the height difference between its ends, and the projection length.

Before every tilt the student predicts whether the projection will be longer, shorter or equal to the stick. Then the student drags and watches the numbers change. With a stick 17 cm long and one end 8 cm higher, the projection reads 15 cm, and the tool draws the right triangle made by the stick, the projection and the height difference, with the stick as its longest side. Lowering the raised end makes the triangle flatten until the projection is as long as the stick, which happens only when the height difference is 0 and the stick is parallel to the table. A rod 25 cm long with one end 7 cm higher gives 24 cm, and a stick 10 cm long with a projection of 6 cm has ends 8 cm apart in height.

Switching to Card, a flat card hangs above the table, the student tilts it by dragging an edge or a corner, and its projection is drawn on the table as a shadow shape. A square card of side 10 cm, tilted about one side so that the opposite side is 6 cm higher, projects to a rectangle 10 by 8. A square card with diagonals of 16 cm, tilted about one diagonal until the other diagonal projects to 12 cm, projects to a rhombus with diagonals 16 and 12, whose side is 10 cm. In the last card the student tries every tilt of a parallelogram card to get a trapezium. The tool marks the pairs of parallel sides that stay parallel in the shadow and shows that it is always a parallelogram, or a line when the card stands on its edge.

The seven cards are the 17 cm stick, the 25 cm rod, the 10 cm stick with a 6 cm projection, the stick tilted until its projection equals its length, the two square cards, and the parallelogram. A projection drawn longer than the stick is rejected and the right triangle is shown with the stick as the longest side. A card projection with sides that are not parallel is rejected and the matching pairs of parallel sides are highlighted. Hints come in order. First: where do the perpendiculars from the two ends of the stick meet the table? Then: the stick, its projection and the height difference make a right triangle, so which side is the longest? Then: the square of the longest side equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides. Last, the right triangle is shown with the projection length and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a projection is never longer than the line, and that a tilted parallelogram always projects to a parallelogram or a line.

(tool id M3.S21.T21.C63.2D1, concept M3.S21.T21.C63, topic: Projections of Points, Lines and Shapes)

Tool Name: Three Views Viewer

Tool Description:

A solid sits on a turntable in the middle of the screen. Three panels surround it: the front view on the wall behind it, the top view on the floor below it, and the side view on the wall at its side. A picker offers the cube, cuboid, parallelepiped, cylinder, cone, prism, pyramid and everyday objects such as a funnel, and a Cube Builder switches the floor to a grid for stacks of unit cubes.

The student picks a solid and first draws a prediction of its three views on small sketch pads, one for each panel. Then the live views are revealed. A wrong prediction has the true view laid over the student's drawing, so the difference can be seen. The student drags the solid round to see how each view changes and which stay the same. A cylinder standing on its base gives a rectangle from the front, a circle from the top and a rectangle from the side. A cone gives a triangle, a circle and a triangle. A square pyramid gives a triangle from the front and the side, and from the top a square with lines from its corners to the centre.

In the Cube Builder, tapping the floor adds a cube and tapping a cube adds one on top. Three cubes in a row with a fourth on top of the left one show an L of 4 squares from the front. From the top they show a row of 3 squares, because the extra cube hides the one under it. From the side they show a column of 2 squares. A stack whose view is wrong highlights the column of cubes that differs. When two different solids share a view, they are shown side by side with their other views unlike.

The six cards are: the cylinder, drawn first and then checked; the cone, with front and top views predicted; the square pyramid, with all three views; finding another solid with the same top view as the cone, such as the cylinder, whose front view is a rectangle and not a triangle; building a stack of 4 cubes whose front view is an L; and matching a funnel with its front, top and side views from the choices. Hints go in order. First: which plane is in front of the solid, which is below it, and which is beside it? Then: look straight at the solid from that plane, and ask what outline it leaves. Then: for a stack of cubes, count the tallest column seen along each line of sight. Last, the correct view is drawn and the next card follows. Through doing, the student learns that one view cannot tell solids apart, and that the front, top and side views together describe a solid.

(tool id M3.S22.T22.C66.2D1, concept M3.S22.T22.C66, topic: Front, Top and Side Views)

Tool Name: Isometric Grid Builder

Tool Description:

An isometric dot grid fills most of the screen. On the left is an axis key with three coloured arrows: the vertical arrow is height, and the two slanting arrows are length and depth. The tools are a line pen that can only travel along those three directions, a cube stamp that snaps to the grid, and a hidden line eraser. Beside the grid a small 3D picture of the target solid turns slowly.

Before drawing, the student predicts the units needed along each direction by typing them in. For a 1 by 1 by 1 cube these are 1 unit of length, 1 of depth and 1 of height. With the pen, dragging along grid dots draws one unit at a time and counts the units as it goes. An edge dragged off all three directions is rejected and the nearest direction is shown. The student can draw edge by edge, or stamp the cubes one by one, which leaves the lines of earlier cubes showing through later ones. A line that would be hidden behind another cube flashes until it is erased, and only the visible edges remain. A solid cube shows 9 visible edges, 3 along each direction.

The six cards ask for a 1 by 1 by 1 cube, a 2 by 2 by 2 cube, where each of the 9 visible edges is 2 units long, and a row of four cubes along the depth axis. Then come the L shaped tetris piece and the T shaped tetris piece, each made of four cubes, and finally four cubes glued into a shape that is not flat. A drawing with the wrong number of units on an edge is placed next to the 3D solid, and the edge that does not match is highlighted in both. The card is done when every visible edge is correct and no hidden line is left.

Hints come in order. First: which direction on the grid is height, which is length and which is depth? Then: count the units along one direction at a time. Then: draw the front vertical edges first, then the top, then the side. Last, the finished drawing is shown and the next card follows. Through doing, the student learns that on isometric paper vertical lines are height and the two slanting families are length and depth, that a unit is the same length in all three directions, and that a solid made of cubes can be drawn just by counting units.

(tool id M3.S28.T28.C84.2D1, concept M3.S28.T28.C84, topic: Isometric Projection and Drawing)
