# maths8_ch6 - tool descriptions (10 tools)

Tool Name: Distributive Area

Tool Description:

A rectangle stands on a unit grid with a vertical cut line running through it and a drag handle on the cut. It opens 3 high and 7 wide, cut into widths 3 and 4. The two parts take two different accent colours, the height is written outside on the left, braces under the figure carry the two part widths and the whole width, and a total readout is pinned beneath. Before any area appears, the student predicts whether cutting the width into 3 and 4 changes the total area.

Then the student types the area of each part in square units, 9 and 12. A product such as 3 times 4 is accepted as well as 12. The two pieces slide back into one rectangle only when both labels are right and add up to the whole. If not, the merge is refused and the running sum is shown against the whole. On a 7 by 10 rectangle cut at 3 and 7, labels of 21 and 42 give 63 against 70, so the second label must be 49. The student then drags the cut. At 2 and 5 the parts become 6 and 15, both areas recompute, and the total readout stays at 21 and pulses without changing. A cut dragged to the very edge leaves a part 0 wide with area 0, and a 4 by 9 rectangle still totals 36. A second cut gives three parts: a 5 by 12 rectangle cut into widths 2, 4 and 6 gives 10 plus 20 plus 30, which is 60, and the tool says only the number of pieces has changed.

The wrong reading is drawn as a region, not stated. For a rectangle 6 high and 11 wide cut at 4 and 7, writing 6 times 4 plus 7 draws the 6 by 4 block with a one-unit-high strip of 7 squares beside it. Everything that line loses is hatched and labelled 35 square units, so the student can point at it. Cutting a strip one unit wide off the end also shows why raising one number by 1 adds the other number. Finally a letters switch removes the grid squares and every numeral, and the sides read a and b plus c, so nothing can be counted and the rule has to be used. Asked what stays the same, an answer that only names these numbers is turned back with the question: what happens for any cut?

Hints go from asking what the cut line adds to or takes from the region, to showing the two part areas beside the whole, to working the sum line by line. Through doing, the student learns that multiplying a sum means multiplying every part of it and adding, because moving the cut changes the parts but never the whole.

Tool Name: Binomial Tile Multiplier

Tool Description:

Two sides, each a sum of two terms, are written along the top and the left of a rectangle on grid paper. It opens on x plus 3 across and x plus 2 down, with nothing inside it and three empty trays beneath, one for squares, one for strips and one for units. Before any tile is laid, the student predicts how many separate pieces the rectangle will break into. Many say two, and the two cuts already drawn across the figure answer that.

Filling the rectangle lays the tiles one region at a time, largest first. One big x by x square sits in the corner, three upright strips run down one column, two flat strips run along one row, and a block of 6 units sits in the last corner. Every tile is drawn at its true size, so an x tile is five unit lengths long. The student names each region from its two sides: x times x, 3 times x, x times 2, and 3 times 2. Then the student drags every tile into its tray. A unit tile dropped into the strip tray shakes and springs back with the reason that a tray holds one kind. That refusal is what like terms means. Combining a tray collapses its tiles into one term whose coefficient is the count, and the three trays read x squared, 5x and 6.

Signs and zeros are handled on the same picture. The student can set the coefficient and constant of either side anywhere from minus 9 to 9. For x plus 7 times x minus 3, the 3 negative strips appear in a warning colour and cancel three of the seven positive strips pair by pair, leaving 4x, while the units region comes out as minus 21. For x plus 4 times x plus 0, the rectangle keeps all four regions, two of them empty outlines, and gives x squared plus 4x. A student who writes x squared plus 30 for x plus 6 times x plus 5 sees the two strip regions, 6x and 5x, left out as holes totalling 11x. Switching the tiles off leaves only the two sides and answer boxes, so the expansion has to be written, not counted. Later cards ask for the x coefficient of 2x plus 3 times x plus 5, which is 13, and the constant term of x plus 3 times x minus 4, which is minus 12.

Hints begin by asking which term of one side meets which term of the other, then light the region that has no label yet, then show the tiles being counted. Through doing, the student learns that two terms times two terms always give four products, and that only tiles of the same size can be collected.

Tool Name: Neighbour Digit Adder

Tool Description:

A number is written in digit boxes across the screen, and directly beneath it sits a copy of the same digits that can slide to the left. Below that are a carry row, a column-by-column sum row, a switch for 11, 101 and 99, and a box for the product.

The student starts with 23 times 11. Sliding the copy one place left shows that 11 is 10 plus 1, so the number times 10 and the number are added. Each column holds a digit and its right-hand neighbour. As the student adds a column, the two digits being added light up in the number and in the copy. The units column keeps its own digit and the tens column adds 2 and 3, so the middle digit of 253 is made by two neighbours. The student works from the right, writing each column's digit and placing any carry. For 68 times 11, the tens column adds 6 and 8 to make 14, so the student writes 4 and carries 1, and the hundreds column gives 7, so the product is 748. A column sum written without its carry makes the carry box flash. 365 times 11 needs a carry in two columns and gives 4015, and 2718 times 11 is written in one line as 29898. Every product is checked against the number times 10 plus the number.

Then the switch moves to 101 and the copy slides two places, so each digit meets the digit two places to its left. For 523 times 101 the student finds that the hundreds column adds 5 and 3, and the product is 52823. On 99 there is nothing to add: the screen shows a hundred times the number and the number taken away, so 47 times 99 is 4700 minus 47, which is 4653, and 623 times 99 is 62300 minus 623, which is 61677. The seven cards are 23, 68, 365 and 2718 times 11, 523 times 101, and 47 and 623 times 99, and the set is done when all seven are finished with the carry placed correctly in at least two.

Hints go from asking which two numbers are added when a number is multiplied by 10 plus 1, to writing the number with a copy one place left and adding from the right, to the worked 68 times 11, and last the columns are added on screen and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that these shortcuts are the distributive property in disguise, with each digit of the answer being a digit plus its neighbour.

(tool id M2.S8.T8.C34.2D1, concept M2.S8.T8.C34, topic: Fast Multiplication)

Tool Name: Square Of Sum Tiles

Tool Description:

A square waits on a unit grid with side 3 plus 4, whole, with no cuts and no labels. Before touching it, the student predicts whether the square of a plus b equals a squared plus b squared.

The student drags a split point along the top edge, and the matching cut travels down the left edge at the same moment, so the figure always stays a square. Four tiles appear: an a by a square and a b by b square in the main colour, and two a by b rectangles on opposite corners in a second colour. The student types the area of each tile, 9, 12, 12 and 16 here. Writing ab or ba is accepted, and so is the number. Assemble works only when all four labels are right. A student who labels the middle as ab instead of 2ab has both rectangles lit, so there are two rectangles where one was counted. The two rectangles are then tapped and pinned into a corner reminder that stays on screen as the picture changes.

A switch draws the two-term version, a squared plus b squared, to true scale over the same square. At 3 plus 4 it covers 25 of the 49 squares, and the band it leaves uncovered is measured and read out as 24, which is 2 times 3 times 4. The student drags one part to 0, and the two rectangles and one square become empty outlines, so the identity falls to a squared. Dragging a square of side 14 to the middle gives parts of 7 and 7, makes all four tiles equal, and shows the wrong version claiming exactly 98 of 196, half. A square of side 8 plus 3 misses 48 square units, and a letters switch lets the student see that the two forms differ by 2ab, so they agree only when a or b is 0. In practice the student also finds the whole area of 5 plus 3 (64) and 9 plus 4 (169), judges a line that writes the square of 3 plus 6 as 9 plus 36, and puts four squares in order of the area the wrong version misses.

Hints start by lighting the tiles that are missing, then ask the area of one rectangle, then show 2ab worked out, then the picture. Through doing, the student learns that the square of a plus b is a squared plus 2ab plus b squared, and that the middle term is doubled because the picture holds the same rectangle twice.

(tool id M3.S10.T10.C46.2D1, concept M3.S10.T10.C46, topic: The Square of a Sum)

Tool Name: Square Of Difference Carver

Tool Description:

A big square of side a fills the screen with two strips marked on it, an a by b strip along the right edge and another along the top, and the b by b corner where the two strips overlap is shaded. Number fields set a and b, and a toggle for the wrong picture and a button to add the corner back sit under the square.

The student starts with a equal to 60 and b equal to 5. Dragging the first strip off the square prints its area, 300, and dragging the second prints another 300. Subtracting the two strip areas gives 3600 minus 300 minus 300, which is 3000, but the square of side 55 left in the corner measures 3025, so the student looks for the missing 25. The shaded 5 by 5 corner was taken off twice, once with each strip, so the student presses the add-back button to put it in once, and 3025 appears. An answer written without the added corner makes the b by b corner light up. Next the student finds how much less 55 squared is than 60 squared (575, which is 600 minus 25), and then uses the same carving on 99 squared as 100 minus 1 squared (10000 minus 200 plus 1, which is 9801), on 58 squared as 60 minus 2 squared, which is 3364, and on 197 squared as 200 minus 3 squared, which is 38809. A letters card follows: 5x minus 2 all squared, with a equal to 5x and b equal to 2, so each strip is 10x and the result is 25x squared minus 20x plus 4.

The wrong picture, a squared minus b squared, is shown as an L-shaped region that does not fit the square left behind. The final card sets a equal to 10 and b equal to 3. The student sees the real square of side 7 and area 49, set beside the L of area 91, and answers why they are not the same.

Hints go from asking which big square the smaller one sits in and which two strips are cut away, to taking both strips and looking at the corner cut twice, to the worked 99 squared, and last the carving with every area written in and a similar card. Through doing, the student learns that a minus b squared is a squared minus 2ab plus b squared, because the small corner has to be added back once.

(tool id M3.S12.T12.C57.2D1, concept M3.S12.T12.C57, topic: The Square of a Difference)

Tool Name: Difference Of Squares Slide

Tool Description:

A square of side a has a b by b square cut out of one corner, and what is left is an L shape. Number fields set a and b. A cutting line sits across the L and can be dragged, and one strip can be turned and slid. The sides of the new rectangle are printed live as it forms.

The student starts with a equal to 10 and b equal to 4. The L has area 100 minus 16, which is 84. The student drags the cutting line until the L falls into two strips of the same width, which is a minus b, here 6. A cut that makes unequal strips shows the two lengths that differ. Then the student turns one strip and slides it beside the other, and the rectangle prints its sides as a plus b and a minus b, which are 14 and 6. The area of the L and the area of the rectangle are shown side by side, 84 and 84, so the student sees the same piece of paper rearranged. The second card repeats the move with a equal to 12 and b equal to 5, giving a rectangle 17 by 7 with area 119, equal to 144 minus 25.

Then the tool is used to find products. For 97 times 103, the student finds the pair a equal to 100 and b equal to 3, so the product is 10000 minus 9, which is 9991. 48 times 52 is 50 squared minus 2 squared, which is 2496, and 63 times 57 is 60 squared minus 3 squared, which is 3591. Two cards run the other way. Given a rectangle 16 by 6, the student works out that a plus b is 16 and a minus b is 6, so a is 11 and b is 5. A 30 by 4 rectangle gives a equal to 17 and b equal to 13.

Hints begin by asking what shape is left when the small square is cut from the corner of the big one, then suggest cutting the L into two strips of the same width and sliding one beside the other. The third hint is the worked a equal to 10, b equal to 4 case: the rectangle is 14 by 6, and 14 times 6 is 84, which is 100 minus 16. The last hint shows the strips joined and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns why a squared minus b squared equals a plus b times a minus b, and how to use it to multiply numbers near a round number.

(tool id M3.S14.T14.C65.2D1, concept M3.S14.T14.C65, topic: Patterns and the Difference of Squares)

Tool Name: Identity Generaliser

Tool Description:

A claim box stands on the right and a list of checked cases on the left, with an area square beneath and a test table below the claim. It opens with four pairs of numbers that all fit the rule the class suspects, such as 0 and 7, where the square of the sum is 49 and the sum of the squares is also 49. The student first says what they think holds for every pair, then types it in letters, for example a plus b squared equals a squared plus b squared. The tool reads it as typed and never repairs it. A statement it cannot parse is returned with the position where reading failed.

Pressing Test fills the table one row at a time, evaluating both sides at each substitution: a pair with a zero in it, then a negative pair such as a equal to minus 3 and b equal to 2, then a fractional pair. A row where one number is zero is tagged 2ab equal to 0, so an agreeing row there is visibly worth nothing. The first row that disagrees holds and the rest stop. For the wrong rule, a equal to 1 and b equal to 1 gives 4 against 2. The area square then redraws at that pair, with the two a by b rectangles hollow and the gap of 2 printed under them. The student can add a pair of their own and attack their own claim, and the failing values are named.

Then the student writes the claim a squared plus 2ab plus b squared, in any letters, such as p and q. Every row now agrees, including the negative and the fraction, and the statement is promoted to an identity card with the student's name and the word survived, never proved. A true statement that is not the expected one also survives, and the tool says it is a different true statement. The student also finds the smallest pair of whole numbers that breaks the wrong rule, 1 and 1, and sees why a list of three agreeing cases does not make a rule. In practice the student evaluates the square of the sum at 2 and 3 (25), 3 and 7 (100) and 12 and 5 (289), and finds the middle term at 5 and 6 (60).

Hints ask which values make the middle term vanish, then suggest trying a pair with no zero in it, and then show the two sides of the failing row. Through doing, the student learns that a few agreeing cases suggest a rule but do not establish it, that one failing pair ends a claim, and that letters state the rule for every number at once.

(tool id M3.S14.T14.C67.2D1, concept M3.S14.T14.C67, topic: Patterns and the Difference of Squares)

Tool Name: Identity Match Sort

Tool Description:

Four bins stand in a row, labelled with the written form first and the plain name second: a plus b all squared, a minus b all squared, a plus b times a minus b, and a fourth bin for none of these. The test that admits a card is printed under each bin. Expression cards lie face up on a table beneath. Before moving anything, the student looks at the whole set and predicts which bin will get the fewest cards.

The student drags a card to a bin and writes the feature that made them choose it. For x squared minus 9, the feature is two terms, both squares, joined by a minus, so it goes under a plus b times a minus b. For x squared plus 6x plus 9, it is three terms where the last term is 3 times 3 and the middle term 6x is twice x times 3, so it goes under the square of a sum. A correct card locks flat and the bin count ticks up. A wrong card travels back to the table with a one-line note naming the feature that decided it, so the reason arrives with the card. Tapping a card turns it over to show its expansion, so the claim can be checked: x plus 3 all squared does give x squared plus 6x plus 9.

The set always holds near misses. x squared plus 9 goes in the none bin, because a plus sign between two squares fits no identity and no whole-number product matches it. x squared plus 5x plus 9 is returned with the note that 5x is not twice the product of the roots, and x squared minus 5 is sent back from the difference bin, because 5 lies between 4 and 9 and is not the square of a whole number. The none bin always holds a card, so the sort can never be finished by elimination. Practice cards include x squared minus 25, x squared minus 4x plus 4, x squared minus 10x plus 25 and x squared minus 14x plus 49. Some ask the whole number squared in the last term: 9 for x squared minus 81, and 11 for x squared minus 121. The student can also flip the middle sign of a matched card, for example x squared plus 6x plus 9 into x squared minus 6x plus 9, and watch it move to the square of a difference. Writing a card of their own is judged by the same rule as the supplied ones. A feature that does not decide the bin, such as only having a squared term, is named as not enough.

Hints ask how many terms the expression has, then whether the outer terms are squares, then what sign decides the form. Through doing, the student learns to choose an identity by reading the structure of an expression, not by spotting a squared term.

(tool id M4.S17.T17.C80.2D1, concept M4.S17.T17.C80, topic: Mind the Mistake, Mend the Mistake)

Tool Name: Four Rectangles Square

Tool Description:

Four identical rectangles, each m by n, are turned round a small square in the middle to make one big square of side m plus n. Two sliders set m and n with n always at least m, and two readouts sit beside the figure, one headed by taking away and one headed by the side of the small square.

The student starts at m equal to 2 and n equal to 5. Before touching the readouts, the student predicts whether the middle square's area comes from adding or from taking away. Under the first readout the big square is 7 by 7, which is 49, the four rectangles are 4 times 10, which is 40, and the area left is 9. Under the second readout the side of the small square is read directly as 5 minus 2, which is 3, and 3 squared is also 9. The two answers appear side by side and match for every m and n the student tries. A student who reads the side of the small square as m plus n instead of n minus m sees the four rectangles light up, so the side cannot be that long.

The next cards use m equal to 3 and n equal to 8, where 121 minus 96 is 25 and the side 5 squared is also 25, and m equal to 4 and n equal to 6, where 100 minus 96 is 4 and the side 2 squared is also 4. A reversed card gives m equal to 3 and an area of 16 for the small square, and the student finds n by reading the side as 4 and adding 3 to get 7. Then the student slides n down to m, so m equals n equals 5. The small square shrinks to a point and both areas read 0, since the four rectangles use up all 100 of the big square. The last card asks the student to expand both expressions, m plus n all squared minus 4mn and n minus m all squared, and to see that each becomes m squared minus 2mn plus n squared, then to check this with m equal to 1 and n equal to 6, where both give 25.

Hints ask which shape is left in the middle when four equal rectangles surround it, then suggest finding its area two ways, taking away or reading the side, then work m equal to 2 and n equal to 5 as 49 minus 40 equals 9 and the side 5 minus 2 equals 3, so 3 squared equals 9. The last hint shows both ways with the same answer and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that one area can be found in two different ways, and that the two expressions are the same after expanding.

Tool Name: Pattern Regrouper

Tool Description:

A growing pattern of circles fills the screen, with a step slider under it, a lasso for circling groups of pieces, a box that shows the expression each grouping gives, and a Simplify button. Step 1 has 3 circles, Step 2 has 8 and Step 3 has 15. Before moving the slider, the student predicts Step 4.

The student moves the slider and watches the pattern grow, then lassos the circles into groups and reads the expression the grouping produces. For Step 4, the student circles the pieces two ways, as a 5 by 5 square with one circle missing and as a 4 by 6 rectangle, and the expressions 5 times 5 minus 1 and 4 times 6 both give 24. A grouping that misses a circle or counts one twice shows that circle flashing. For Step k, two groupings give k plus 1 all squared minus 1 and k times k plus 2. Simplify reduces both, one line at a time, to k squared plus 2k. The student can type any expression, and the slider checks it against the real count at every step, so an expression that fits Steps 1 and 2 but fails at Step 3 is caught. Step 15 is not counted by hand: putting 15 into k squared plus 2k gives 255.

A second pattern is built from square tiles: Steps 1, 2 and 3 have 8, 12 and 16 tiles, forming a square frame round an empty square. The student finds Step 4 (20) and Step 10 (44), then writes Step n two ways, as the big square less the hole, n plus 2 all squared minus n squared, and as four sides, 4n plus 4. Both simplify to 4n plus 4, and the tool puts the two simplified lines side by side. The set is done when all six cards are finished and at least two groupings have simplified to the same expression.

Hints ask how the pattern changes from one step to the next, then suggest grouping the pieces into a square, rows or a rectangle and counting each group. The third is the worked line, k plus 1 all squared minus 1 equals k squared plus 2k plus 1 minus 1, which is k squared plus 2k. The last shows two groupings with the same simplified expression. Through doing, the student learns that a pattern can be counted in more than one way, and that every correct way simplifies to the same expression.

(tool id M4.S20.T20.C101.2D1, concept M4.S20.T20.C101, topic: This Way or That Way)
