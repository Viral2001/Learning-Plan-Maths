## 1. Hard rules (gates enforce most; fix content, never a gate)
- Class 5 students, 10 to 11 years old: short plain sentences. Characters and settings are the book's: Daljeet Kaur (milk processing unit, butter packets and pouches), Priya, Gollappa, Nida, Kanti, John, Mili and her father, Anjali (Sudha Electricals), Mala, Ananya, Asma, Pankaj, Lado, Kira, Asher, the king and his three ministers; the village dairy: pouches, packets, milk cans, Gir cows, ghee jars, the compost pit, a cinema, a library. The plan's own WE use the same dairy things. Do not invent facts or datasets: every real-world number that is not a plain WE number comes from the book or from section 4. The plan's own WE numbers are practice values.
- **No em dash and no en dash** in any string or comment (the book's dashes become commas, colons or "to": "0 to 20"). Minus is U+2212 `−`. Use `×` for times (never the letter x) and `÷`. Hyphen only inside words.
- **Numbers.** Write numbers of four digits or more with a comma (`1,072`, `71,750`); currency as `₹3,600` (symbol glued to the number). Never put letters next to numbers except units with a space (`35 kg`, `4 km`, `13 litres`).
- **No place value abbreviations in steps or answers.** Write "thousands, hundreds, tens, ones" in words (a lone capital letter such as H, T or O converts to a maths variable). Inside a QUESTION the book's column names may appear once, inside one bracket, as in `(columns Problem, Th, H, T, O)`. No lone capital letter standing for a thing anywhere (name shapes and children in words: "the rectangle", "Nida").
- Never write "NCERT", "textbook", "page N", "Fig.", "Figure N" or exercise numbers in anything the student reads (names, teach lines, steps, questions, tool text). Say "your maths book". Exception: the `textbook=[...]` argument of `A()` quotes the book verbatim (stored as a `textbook_text` block), still no dashes. Do not write the words "partial products", "commutative", "distributive", "algorithm" for students: say "small products", "the order does not change the product", "split a number".
- **Arithmetic must be true in every string.** The equation gate `eqcheck_plain` reads every chain `a op b op c = d` of whole numbers (commas allowed): every such chain in any string must be TRUE, so write `35 × 12 = 30 × 12 + 5 × 12 = 360 + 60 = 420`, never an approximate or half-finished chain. A chain that stops before `=` is not read. Do not write a chain whose two sides name different numbers of different things. Do not put a bare capital word after a number inside a chain (`6 Tens`) unless the chain is true as a number. Recompute every fact with Python before you finish.
- 100% coverage: every id in `content.NCERT_ITEMS` (section 3) is attached to a concept: a `text` or `talk` id through `A(..., ncert=[...])`, a question id as an `FIO` item of the DOER named in section 3.
- **One video per concept.** A teaching concept `A()` has exactly ONE `WE` in `examples` (that WE is the concept video) with the PLAN's own numbers: never the numbers of a book question (the self-check script compares every `a × b` of your WE with every book question). The hook concept has `video_focus=["HOOK"]`, `enrichment=True`, no WE. DOER concepts carry a recap, their question items and at most one tool, no WE.
- **Questions.** Every book question is a `FIO(cov, label, question, steps, answer, kind=...)` in the DOER of its topic, in the book's order; `core/qt.py` moves each into its own concept of the "Questions: ..." topic that follows. `question` is the book's wording (copy it from section 3; keep it plain: blanks are `___`). `steps` = the numbered working, one operation per line, values before combining, a check as the last step; `answer` = short final answer. Open-ended items get a labelled "Sample answer" built by reasoning and say "Your answer may differ". Add `q_figure` (the figure column of section 3: ONLY what the question gives, never the answer or a hint) when the book has a drawing for the question, and `s_figure` (one plain sentence for the solution card; it may show the answer) when the working is clearer with a picture (arrays, boxes, place value tables, the number chart).
- **The answer is at most 220 characters** (the displayed answer is cut at 220 characters of its LaTeX form, and a longer one fails `solution_content`): for a question with many parts give only the final results, for example `(a): 312; (b): 747; (c): 1,876; (d): 1,961`, and keep the working in `steps`. Aim for 160 characters at most in plain text, because the LaTeX form is longer.
- **Labels (`book`) decide the Questions topic** (`core/question_naming.py`): a label starting `Let Us Do` goes to "Questions: Let us do"; a label starting `Example N` goes to "Questions: Example" (use exactly the numbers given in section 3); any other label goes to a topic named by the text before its first colon. Use exactly the labels given in section 3.
- Names: DOER name starts with `Practice: ` or `Pull it together and do it: ` (the framing topic's free-try DOER is `Pouch Packer: Explore`, `role="try_it"`).
- An idea topic has 2 to 4 primary concepts INCLUDING its DOER (last). The intro T1 is `topic_type="framing"` with `""` objective, all concepts `enrichment=True`: C1 hook (`video_focus=["HOOK"]`) and C3 a DOER with `role="try_it"` and the free-try tool (the framing topic holds exactly ONE video, the hook, and no book question). The concept video and the book questions of the opener live in the idea topic T1B. The summary topic T16 is `topic_type="summary"`, one concept, no tool, no video_focus (there is no recap video).
- Objectives: one per non-enrichment concept (teaching concepts and DOERs of idea topics), starts "Students ...", checkable (a verb you could test: find, show, write, multiply, split, halve, check, estimate, explain, compare), sharing a key term with the concept (a word of its name or key_terms), and NEVER naming a tool. Topic objective on each idea topic. Bloom words: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze.
- Misconception ids come from `content.MISCONCEPTIONS` (section 6); attach the relevant ones to teach concepts, DOERs and tools by id.
- `teach` lines: the first line is a prediction question ("Predict first: ..."), then the reveal, then the rule in one line. `minutes` 4 to 8.
- Tool specs (`T(...)`): section 5. Exactly 5 to 7 challenges, exactly 4 hints.
- Do not use words outside the chapter (`book_rules.json` banned_words): no division algorithm, remainder, decimal, fraction, percent, factor, multiple as a topic, prime, exponent, square (as a power), LCM. (The words "multiplier" and "multiplicand" are the book's own and are fine; "multiple" appears in the book's "nearest multiple" and "not a multiple of 4".)

Teaching concept seeds (the WE is the plan's own; these are suggestions: change them if you like, but check each against the book numbers with the self-check script; every chain must be true):
- T1B.C1 equal groups: 36 pouches packed in equal groups: 2 × 18, 3 × 12, 4 × 9, 6 × 6 (and swapped); number of groups × group size = total. Rule line: a total can be packed in many equal groups; number of groups × group size = total.
- T2.C1 order: 7 rows of 12 pouches and 12 rows of 7: 7 × 12 = 84 = 12 × 7 (7 × 12 = 70 + 14); the picture turned is the same pouches; and anything times 0 is 0. Rule: changing the order does not change the product.
- T3.C1 by 10 and 100: 37 × 10 = 370 (each digit moves one place left, a zero fills the ones) and 37 × 100 = 3,700 (two places).
- T3.C2 tens, hundreds, thousands: 60 × 70: 6 × 10 × 7 × 10 = 6 × 7 × 10 × 10 = 42 × 100 = 4,200; and by 1,000: 9 × 4,000 = 36,000 in teach lines.
- T4.C1 split: 14 × 6 = 10 × 6 + 4 × 6 = 60 + 24 = 84. Rule: split one number, multiply each part, add the parts.
- T5.C1 halve and double: 8 × 13: cut the 8 rows in the middle into two blocks of 4 rows and put them side by side: 4 × 26 = 104 = 8 × 13. Rule: halve one number and double the other and the product stays.
- T5.C2 with 5 and 25: 25 × 44 = 100 × 11 = 1,100 (halve 44 twice and double 25 twice); 5 × 46 = 10 × 23 = 230 in teach lines. Why 5 and 25: they are half of 10 and a quarter of 100.
- T6.C1 nearest multiple: 6 × 19 = 6 × 20 − 6 = 120 − 6 = 114; and 13 × 31 = 13 × 30 + 13 = 390 + 13 = 403 in teach lines. Rule: round, multiply, then take away or add the extra strip.
- T7.C1 choose the easy way: 15 × 48 = 30 × 24 = 720 (halve 48... or 15 × 48 = 10 × 48 + 5 × 48 = 480 + 240 = 720); look at the numbers first: a 5, 25 or 50 means halve and double; a number just below or above a ten means nearest multiple; otherwise split.
- T8.C1 box: 23 × 14 = (20 + 3) × (10 + 4): cells 200, 30, 80, 12: column sums 280 and 42: 322.
- T8.C2 lines: 26 × 13: Kanti's four lines 18 (3 × 6), 60 (3 × 20), 60 (10 × 6), 200 (10 × 20) = 338; John's two lines 78 (3 × 26) and 260 (10 × 26) = 338.
- T9.C1 place by place: 235 × 4 = 4 × 5 + 4 × 30 + 4 × 200 = 20 + 120 + 800 = 940; "4 × 3 tens = 12 tens = 120".
- T9.C2 carry: 176 × 24 = 4,224: first row 4 × 176 = 704 (4 × 6 = 24, write 4, carry 2; 4 × 7 = 28 + 2 = 30, write 0, carry 3; 4 × 1 = 4 + 3 = 7), second row 2 tens × 176 = 3,520, add 704 + 3,520 = 4,224. Rule: the carry is added after multiplying the next place, not before.
- T10.C1 check: a child solved 24 × 31 as 24 × 1 = 24, 24 × 3 = 72, so 24 + 72 = 96; an estimate 24 × 30 = 720 shows it is wrong: the 3 is 3 tens, so 24 × 30 = 720 and 24 + 720 = 744.
- T11.C1 same product: is 15 × 12 the same as 30 × 6? (halve 12 ... double 15), 15 × 10 + 15 × 2? 16 × 12 − 12? check by changing numbers, not by calculating: choose numbers not in the book.
- T11.C2 grow: given 7 × 14 = 98: 7 × 15 = 98 + 7 = 105 (one more column of 7); 6 × 14 = 98 − 14 = 84 (one row of 14 less).
- T12.C1 estimate: 52 × 48 is about 50 × 50 = 2,500 (exact 2,496).
- T12.C2 digit puzzle: find the two-digit numbers (two boxes) times 6 that give three equal digits: 37 × 6 = 222 and 74 × 6 = 444 (look at the ones digit first: 6 times the ones digit must end in the repeated digit; the answer is a multiple of 111). Check any seed with Python.
- T13.C1 growth: a coin chest that doubles each day: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 coins after 5 doublings (practice values); and 1 coin times 3 each day: 3, 9, 27, 81, 243 (practice values). The one with the smaller start can overtake.
- T14.C1 change: 18 × 24 = 432: 9 × 24 = 216 (half), 36 × 24 = 864 (double), 9 × 12 = 108 (a quarter), 9 × 48 = 432 (same).
- T14.C2 patterns: 65 × 65 = 4,225 (6 × 7 = 42, then 25); 11 × 36 = 396 (3, 3 + 6 = 9, 6).
- T15.C1 plan: the cooperative buys 24 cans at ₹35 each and pays ₹120 to carry them (practice values): 24 × 35 = 840, 840 + 120 = 960; read, decide what to multiply and what to add, estimate, then check.

## 6. Misconception ids (defined in content.py; attach by id)
- `M_ONE_WAY`: A total can be packed in equal groups in only one way, so 60 pouches can only be 30 groups of 2.
- `M_ORDER_CHANGES`: Changing the order of the numbers changes the product, so 9 × 0 is not the same as 0 × 9.
- `M_ZERO_TIMES`: A number times 0 is the number itself, so 9 × 0 = 9.
- `M_ZERO_COUNT`: To multiply tens or hundreds you multiply the digits and forget the zeros, so 30 × 20 = 6.
- `M_ADD_ZERO`: Multiplying by 10 just means writing a zero at the end, with no idea that every digit moves one place to the left.
- `M_HALVE_BOTH`: To halve and double, halve both numbers, so 14 × 6 becomes 7 × 3.
- `M_DOUBLE_BOTH`: Doubling both numbers keeps the product the same, so 6 × 8 = 12 × 16.
- `M_NO_ADJUST`: Rounding is enough: 4 × 19 = 4 × 20 = 80, with nothing taken away.
- `M_WRONG_STRIP`: To get 17 × 24 from 17 × 23 you add 23, the number that was already multiplied.
- `M_SPLIT_ONE`: When a number is split into parts, only one of the parts needs to be multiplied.
- `M_MISSING_ZERO`: The line for the tens digit needs no zero at the end, so 70 × 193 is written as 7 × 193.
- `M_CARRY_FIRST`: A carry is added to the digit before it is multiplied: for 4 × 68, add 3 to 6 and then multiply.
- `M_PLACE_SLIP`: The 2 in 203 stands for 2 tens, so 203 is 20 + 3.
- `M_ESTIMATE_EXACT`: An estimate has to be exactly equal to the product.
- `M_START_MOST`: The choice that starts with the most coins always ends with the most coins.
- `M_MULT_ALL`: In a word problem you multiply every pair of numbers you see.
