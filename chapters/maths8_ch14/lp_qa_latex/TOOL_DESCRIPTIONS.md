# maths8_ch14 - tool descriptions (9 tools)

Tool Name: Rectangle Stretcher: Area or Perimeter

Tool Description:

A square grid fills the screen with a blue rectangle sitting on its bottom left corner. Its right edge and top edge carry drag handles that snap to grid lines. Four separate readouts show the length, the width, the area and the perimeter, and each one updates the moment the student drags. Beside the blue rectangle sits a pale ghost rectangle, the one to beat, with its own four readouts, so two rectangles can always be compared side by side.

Every card starts with a prediction. In the first card the ghost is 7 by 4 and the student's rectangle is 8 by 3, and the student says which one needs more rangoli powder before any square is counted. Pressing Check shades the unit squares in both and counts them, 28 and 24. A wrong guess shows both counts side by side. The student then drags the edges and sees that the area and the perimeter change by different amounts, and sometimes in different directions.

A Hold perimeter switch locks the perimeter at 20. With it on, pulling one edge longer pushes the other shorter, and the student stretches the rectangle from 5 by 5, which covers 25 squares, down to a thin 9 by 1 strip that covers only 9. As the strip gets thinner the area readout turns red, so the student watches the area fall while the boundary stays the same length. The second and third cards ask for the largest and the smallest area at perimeter 20. The fourth card sets the ghost at 5 by 4, perimeter 18 and area 20, and asks for a rectangle with a larger perimeter but a smaller area. A 10 by 1 strip does it, with perimeter 22 and area 10, and when the student finds such a pair the tool marks it as a longer boundary with less area. In the fifth card the area is 21 and one side is 7, and the student drags the other side until the area reads 21; the tool checks by multiplying back, 7 times 3. In the sixth card the tool draws the diagonal of a 7 by 4 rectangle and the student counts the squares in one triangle, 14, which is half of 28.

Hints go in order. First: what does area count, and what does perimeter measure? Then: count the unit squares inside, then walk around the edge and add the sides. Then: for a 10 by 1 strip the perimeter is 22 but the area is only 10, so compare it with 5 by 4. Last, the pair is shown with both counts and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that area and perimeter measure different things, that one can grow while the other shrinks, and that the length of the boundary can never stand in for the number of squares inside.

(tool id M1.S2.T2.C6.2D1, concept M1.S2.T2.C6, topic: Rectangles: Area Is Not Perimeter)

Tool Name: Triangle Height Finder: Pick a Base

Tool Description:

A triangle sits on a grid with a draggable dot at each corner. Tapping a side makes it the base and highlights it, and a dashed line drops from the opposite corner to the base with a small square marking the right angle. Readouts show the base, the height and half of base times height, and an Enclosing rectangle switch draws the rectangle with the same base and the same height round the triangle.

On every card the student first predicts which segment is the height, then taps to see it drawn. In the first card the base is 8 and the height is 5, and the student reads the area, 20 square units, noticing that it is half of the 8 by 5 rectangle that appears when the switch is on. The second card has base 5, slanting side 6 and height 4, and asks which number goes in the area. If the student uses the slanting side, the tool shows the larger wrong area, half of 5 times 6 which is 15, beside the true one, half of 5 times 4 which is 10. The third card is a right triangle with sides 6, 8 and 10. The student takes the side 10 as the base and finds that the height drawn to it is 4.8, and that half of 10 times 4.8 is 24, the same as half of 6 times 8.

Then the student drags the top corner sideways past the end of the base. The foot of the perpendicular slips off the base, so the tool extends the base line with a dotted line and keeps the height square to it, now outside the triangle. The fourth card asks for this with base 6 and height 4, and the area reads 12. In the fifth card a triangle with base 10 and height 6 has area 30; the student picks a second base, 12, the two products appear side by side, and the missing height comes out as 5. The sixth card asks the student to build a triangle of area 24 on a base of 8 by dragging the corner until the height reads 6.

Hints go in order. First: which side is the base, and which corner is opposite to it? Then: the height meets the base line at a right angle, so extend the base line if it has to. Then: take half of 8 times 5 and compare it with the area readout. Last, the area is written as 20 square units and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that any side can be the base, that the height is the perpendicular distance and never the slanting side, that it can fall outside the triangle, and that every choice of base gives the same area.

(tool id M1.S5.T5.C21.2D1, concept M1.S5.T5.C21, topic: The Area of a Triangle)

Tool Name: Median Splitter: Equal Areas

Tool Description:

The student sees a triangle ABC on a grid with BC along the bottom and a point D that slides along it. The line AD splits the triangle into a left part and a right part, shaded in two colours, and two bars beside the figure show the area of each part. A marker shows the midpoint of BC, and a Rectangle button switches the screen to a rectangle with both diagonals drawn and the four triangles shaded.

In the first card BC is 10 and the height is 6, and the student predicts where D must sit before touching it. Sliding D, the student watches one bar rise as the other falls. When D reaches the midpoint, 5 from B, the bars level out and turn green at 15 each. In the second card D is put 4 from B and the bars read 12 and 18. The third card asks for D so that the left part is a quarter of the triangle, which needs BD to be 2.5 and gives 7.5 out of 30. A wrong placement shows both areas written as half of a base times the same height, so the student sees that only the base differs. Where the two parts are equal in area but not alike in shape, the tool puts them side by side with their bases and the shared height, to correct the idea that equal areas need matching pieces.

In the fourth card the student switches to a rectangle 8 by 6 whose diagonals meet at O and reads the area of each of the four triangles, 12 each. The two thin triangles and the two fat ones are not congruent, but tapping any of them shows its base on half of a diagonal and the same altitude as its neighbour. In the fifth card D stays at the midpoint and the student slides the top corner along a line parallel to BC. The shapes of the two parts keep changing, yet the bars stay level, because the height does not change. In the sixth card BC is 12 and the height is 5, and the student finds BD so that the left part has area 10, which is 4.

Hints go in order. First: which two bases do the two parts stand on, and what height do they share? Then: equal heights mean the areas are equal exactly when the bases are equal. Then: with BC = 10 and height 6, the left part is half of BD times 6, so set it equal to 15. Last, D is shown at 5 from B, each part is half of 5 times 6, which is 15, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a line from a corner to the midpoint of the opposite side halves the area, that equal areas do not need equal shapes, and that the same reasoning gives four equal-area triangles in a rectangle.

(tool id M1.S8.T8.C30.2D1, concept M1.S8.T8.C30, topic: Using the Triangle Formula)

Tool Name: Triangle Slider: Parallel Lines

Tool Description:

Base BC is fixed near the bottom of a grid, and a straight line l runs parallel to it above. A draggable top vertex A sits on l, so the triangle ABC changes shape as A moves. Live readouts give the base, the height, the area and the perimeter, a height bar shows the distance from l to BC, and a Reflect button waits beside the figure.

In the first card the base is 6 and the height is 4. The student predicts whether the area will change as A slides, then drags A to three different places and records the area at each, 12 every time. If the student predicted a change, the height bar is highlighted as unchanged to show why. Meanwhile the perimeter readout keeps changing and leaves a trail of its values on the screen. The second card asks where on l the perimeter is the largest the student can find. Dragging A farther along l keeps raising the perimeter, so the student finds that there is no largest, only a lowest value, which the trail makes easy to see.

In the third card the student uses Reflect. The tool draws the mirror image C' of C in l, and the student draws the straight path from B to C'. A is placed where that path crosses l. The reflected path is drawn in a second colour and its length is shown. AC and AC' are equal, so the bent path B to A to C has the same length as B to A to C', and with A on the straight line this is the shortest it can be. The perimeter trail shows its lowest value here. The fourth card asks the student to check that AB equals AC at this point, which puts A on the perpendicular bisector of BC. In the fifth card the height is changed to 3 and the student predicts the new area, 9, before sliding. The sixth card gives AX = 3, BC = 5 and AC = 4. The student writes the area as half of 5 times 3, which is 7.5, writes it again with base AC as half of 4 times BY, and solves for BY, which is 3.75.

Hints go in order. First: which two measurements does the area of a triangle depend on? Then: is the distance from A to the base changing as A slides along l? Then: reflect C in l; the straight line from B to the image crosses l at the best A. Last, the best A is shown on the perpendicular bisector of BC and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that triangles on one base with the top vertex on a parallel line share one area but not one perimeter, that the smallest perimeter comes from reflection, and that one area written with two bases gives a missing altitude.

(tool id M1.S8.T8.C33.2D1, concept M1.S8.T8.C33, topic: Using the Triangle Formula)

Tool Name: Polygon Splitter: Parts or Cut Out

Tool Description:

Before any line is drawn, the student meets a polygon on a grid with its corners on grid points and two route buttons beneath it, Split and Enclose. Choosing the route is the first decision on every card. With Split, the student taps two corners to draw a line, and each part is shaded and labelled with its area as soon as it is marked. With Enclose, the smallest rectangle round the figure appears, and the corner pieces left over can be lifted off one at a time, each carrying its own area.

The first card is a quadrilateral with a diagonal of 14 and heights of 4 on one side and 2 on the other. Splitting along the diagonal gives two triangles, half of 14 times 4 which is 28 and half of 14 times 2 which is 14, so the area is 42 square units. The second card is a house shaped pentagon, a 6 by 4 rectangle with a triangle of height 3 on its side of 6, so 24 plus 9 gives 33. The third card is a figure inside a 10 by 6 rectangle with two corner triangles removed, each with legs 4 and 3; the rectangle is 60 and the corners are 6 each, so the area is 48. The fourth card is an 8 by 5 rectangle with a 2 by 2 notch cut out of one edge, which is 40 minus 4, or 36. The fifth card is a quadrilateral with a diagonal of 12 and heights of 5 and 3, so 30 plus 18 gives 48.

A split that leaves a gap or an overlap shades the squares that are wrong, so the student can correct the lines. Each part carries its own area, so the final sum is built from visible pieces. In the sixth card the figure sits inside a 12 by 9 rectangle with two corner triangles removed, each with legs 6 and 3. The student must use both routes on it: Enclose gives 108 minus 18, which is 90, and Split into triangles and rectangles must give the same total. When the two routes agree, the matching totals are shown side by side and the student says in a sentence why they agree.

Hints go in order. First: which route would you try first, splitting or cutting out of a rectangle? Then: split into pieces whose areas you can find, or enclose the figure and take away the corners. Then: for the quadrilateral with diagonal 14, find the two triangle areas from the diagonal and its heights. Last, the two triangles are written out as 28 and 14, the area is 42 square units, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that the area of any polygon on a grid comes from triangles and rectangles, either added together or taken away from an enclosing rectangle, and that both routes give the same area.

(tool id M2.S11.T11.C43.2D1, concept M2.S11.T11.C43, topic: Area of Any Polygon)

Tool Name: Parallelogram Cutter: Cut and Slide

Tool Description:

A parallelogram rests on a grid with its lower side marked as the base. A dashed cut line can be dragged along the base and always stays square to it, and a right angle marker shows where it meets the base. Readouts give the base, the height, the slanting side and the area, and a Change base button turns the figure so another side becomes the base.

In the first card the base is 7 and the height is 4. The student predicts whether a rectangle made from the same pieces will have the same area, then drags the cut line to the top corner. A right triangle comes away from the end. The student drags this triangle to the other end of the parallelogram, and when it fits exactly, the rectangle outline lights up with its area, 28. If the student tries to tilt the cut away from the right angle, the cut is refused and the right angle marker is shown. If the student reads the area from the slanting side instead of the height, the two areas are shown disagreeing, with the slanting side giving too much. The second card has base 5 and height 3, and the student reads the area, 15, and answers whether the slanting side was used anywhere. It was not.

In the third card the student builds three different parallelograms on the same base and height by sliding the top side along, and compares them. The slant changes, but the rectangle made by cut and slide is always the same, so the areas match. The fourth card puts a rectangle and a parallelogram, both with sides 5 and 4, side by side on the same base. The rectangle covers 20 squares, and the parallelogram, whose height is less than 4, covers fewer.

In the fifth card the sides are 12 and 7.6 and the height on the side of 12 is 6, so the area is 72. The student uses it to find the height on the other side, 72 divided by 7.6, which is about 9.47. In the sixth card the student presses Change base, uses the other side as the base, cuts again with its own height, and gets the same area.

Hints go in order. First: what shape do you get if the slanting ends become upright? Then: cut along a height so the piece is a right triangle. Then: the rectangle has the same base and the same height, so multiply them. Last, the cut and slide is shown with the area and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a parallelogram can be turned into a rectangle of equal area by cutting off a right triangle and sliding it across, that area is base times height for either side taken as the base, and that the slanting side is never the height.

(tool id M2.S14.T14.C52.2D1, concept M2.S14.T14.C52, topic: Parallelogram: Cut and Slide)

Tool Name: Rhombus Dissector: Diagonals to Rectangle

Tool Description:

A rhombus is drawn in the middle of the screen with its two diagonals as dashed lines crossing at right angles. Each diagonal has a handle at one end. Dragging a handle lengthens or shortens that diagonal while the two diagonals keep crossing at their midpoints, so the rhombus changes shape but stays a rhombus. Readouts show d1, d2, the side and the area. Below the figure are a Cut button, an empty rectangle outline, and a switch that draws the rectangle round the rhombus.

In the first card the diagonals are 12 and 8. The student predicts what the pieces will make, then presses Cut. The rhombus separates into four right triangles with legs 6 and 4. The student drags them into the outline. Pieces that do not fit leave a gap, and the gap is shaded. When all four fit, the rectangle lights up with its two sides marked, 12 for a whole diagonal and 4 for half of the other, and the area reads 12 times 4, which is 48. Then the student switches on the rectangle round the rhombus, 12 by 8, and sees that it is twice as large, 96. This is why the full product of the diagonals is not the area; half of it is.

The second card has diagonals 10 and 6, and the student finds the area, 30. The third has diagonals 16 and 30; the rectangle is 30 by 8, or 16 by 15, and the area is 240. In the fourth card one diagonal is 12 and the area is 60, and the student drags the other handle until the area reads 60, which makes the other diagonal 10 and the rectangle 12 by 5. The fifth card has diagonals 6 and 8. The student finds the area, 24, and also the side, because each small triangle has legs 3 and 4, so the side is 5. In the sixth card the student makes a different rhombus with area 48, for example diagonals 16 and 6, and checks that the product of the diagonals is 96.

Hints go in order. First: what shapes do the two diagonals cut the rhombus into? Then: fit the four triangles into a rectangle; one side is a whole diagonal, the other is half of the other diagonal. Then: for diagonals 12 and 8 the rectangle is 12 by 4, so multiply those two sides. Last, the area is written as 12 times 4, which is 48 square units and is half of 12 times 8, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that the diagonals of a rhombus cut it into four triangles that rearrange into a rectangle, so the area is half of one diagonal times the other, never the whole product.

(tool id M2.S17.T17.C65.2D1, concept M2.S17.T17.C65, topic: Rhombus: Half the Product of Diagonals)

Tool Name: Trapezium Joiner: Two Copies

Tool Description:

A single trapezium stands on the work surface with its parallel sides marked a and b, its height h shown as a dashed perpendicular, and editable boxes for a, b and h. A Copy button sits beside it, and once a copy exists it carries a rotate handle. Readouts show the base, the height and the area of whatever figure the two trapeziums make.

The student first predicts what shape two copies will make when joined. Pressing Copy places a second trapezium. The student turns it half way round with the handle and drags it to a slanting side, and when the slanting sides meet, the copy snaps into place. The angles at the joint add to 180 degrees, so the edges run straight, with the short side of one continuing the long side of the other, and the long base is labelled a + b. A copy joined without turning leaves a six sided figure, and the tool hints to rotate it. The student reads the base of the new parallelogram, compares it with a plus b, and halves its area to get the trapezium. A result that forgets the half is shown next to the parallelogram's area.

In the first card the parallel sides are 7 and 11 and the height is 6, so the parallelogram has base 18 and area 108, and the trapezium has area 54. In the second card the sides are 24 and 36 and the height is 14; the student predicts the parallelogram base, 60, first, then finds 840 for the parallelogram and 420 for the trapezium. In the third card the student changes only the height and watches the area change in step with it. The fourth card asks for a trapezium with area 144, so a plus b times h must be 288, for example sides 10 and 14 with height 12. In the fifth card the trapezium has both slants leaning the same way, and the student joins two copies and finds that the result is still a parallelogram with base a + b and height h. In the sixth card the student breaks the 7 and 11 trapezium instead into a rectangle and two triangles by dropping two heights, 7 times 6 is 42 plus two triangles of 12 in all, and compares the 54 with the first method.

Hints go in order. First: what shape has opposite sides parallel and an easy area? Then: turn the copy so its long side lines up with the short side of the first. Then: the base becomes a + b and the height stays h, so the parallelogram is a + b times h. Last, the joined parallelogram and its half are shown and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that two copies of any trapezium make a parallelogram, so the area of a trapezium is half of its height times the sum of the parallel sides.

(tool id M2.S20.T20.C73.2D1, concept M2.S20.T20.C73, topic: Trapezium: Two Copies Make a Parallelogram)

Tool Name: Square Unit Scaler: Side and Area

Tool Description:

One large unit square fills the middle of the screen, with a pair chooser above it offering metre and centimetre, foot and inch, inch and centimetre, and kilometre and metre. Two readouts show the length factor and the area factor, and a converter row below turns a given area from one unit into the other.

Each card begins with a prediction typed into the area factor box before the square is tiled. Then the student chooses a pair, and the side of the big square splits into equal parts. For foot and inch it splits into 12 parts along each side, and the whole square fills with small squares in 12 rows of 12. The tool counts by rows, so the area factor appears as rows times columns, 12 times 12, which is 144. A student who types the length factor 12 as the area factor sees a tiling in which 12 squares cover only one row, so it visibly does not fit. Metre and centimetre give 100 times 100, which is 10000. For kilometre and metre the tool shows a zoomed corner of the tiling with the count written beside it, 1000 rows of 1000, which is 1000000 square metres.

The inch and centimetre pair is the surprise, because 2.54 is not a whole number. The tiling shows 2 by 2 whole centimetre squares, two thin strips each 2 by 0.54, and a tiny corner piece 0.54 by 0.54. These add up to 4 plus 2.16 plus 0.2916, which is 6.4516 square centimetres, so the student sees why 1 square inch is not 2.54 square centimetres. The first four cards ask for 1 square foot in square inches (144), 1 square metre in square centimetres (10000), 1 square inch in square centimetres with the reason it is not 2.54 (6.4516), and 1 square kilometre in square metres (1000000). Then the converter row is used. The fifth card turns 12.9032 square centimetres into square inches by dividing by 6.4516, which gives 2. The sixth card turns a room of 20 square metres into square centimetres by multiplying by 10000, which gives 200000. A correct conversion shows the factor used and the converted area.

Hints go in order. First: how many small squares fit along one side, and how many rows are there? Then: the area factor is the length factor times itself, because the squares fill rows and columns. Then: for feet and inches the length factor is 12, so multiply 12 by 12. Last, 1 square foot is shown as 12 times 12, which is 144 square inches, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a square unit converts by the length factor multiplied by itself, and can explain why the area factor is the length factor used twice.

(tool id M3.S23.T23.C86.2D1, concept M3.S23.T23.C86, topic: Areas in Real Life)
