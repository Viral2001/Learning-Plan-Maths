# maths6_ch8 - tool descriptions (8 tools)

Tool Name: Compass Circle Maker

Tool Description:

A blank drawing sheet opens with a ruler laid along the bottom edge, a compass standing on the sheet, a centre point that can be dragged, and a pencil on the free leg of the compass. Two read-outs sit at the side, one for the radius and one for the width across the circle. A task card at the top gives the case, for example a circle of radius 3 cm.

The student first predicts the answer to the card, then opens the compass by dragging the pencil leg along the ruler until the opening matches the length needed. The tool shows the opening against the zero mark of the ruler. The student drops the centre point where the circle should sit, which fixes the tip, and sweeps the pencil round. The circle is drawn as a trail behind the pencil while the tip stays still. The student can drop points on the finished circle, and each one shows a thin line back to the centre with the same length as the opening every time. The radius read-out shows the opening and the width read-out shows twice that. Then the student types the radius or the width that the card asks for.

If the opening is set from the end of the ruler instead of its zero mark, the tool shows the true opening beside the one the student typed, so the slip is seen. If the width across is typed in as the radius, the radius read-out shows half of that width, with the radius line drawn from the centre and a matching line on the other side to make up the width. A solved card shows the centre, the radius line and the finished circle.

The six cards ask: radius 3 cm, how wide is the circle across (6 cm); a circle 10 cm across, what radius is set (5 cm); two circles of radius 2 cm that touch, how far apart are the centres (4 cm), found by dragging the second centre until they just touch; two circles that share a centre with radii 2 cm and 4 cm, how wide is the gap between them (2 cm); two circles of radius 3 cm with centres 4 cm apart, which overlap, and is the centre of one inside the other (no, because 4 cm is more than the radius 3 cm, even though the circles overlap); and radius 2.5 cm, how wide across (5 cm). Hints come in order: what does the compass opening stand for? Then: the opening is the radius, and the width across is two radii. Then: read the radius or the width in the card and halve or double it. Last, the radius line and the width across are shown, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a circle is all the points at one fixed distance from its centre, that this distance is the compass opening, the radius, that the width across is two radii, that touching circles have centres two radii apart and that circles sharing a centre are concentric.

(tool id M1.S2.T2.C6.2D1, concept M1.S2.T2.C6, topic: Circles with a Compass)

Tool Name: Wave Builder

Tool Description:

A long central line lies across the middle of the screen with a ruler under it and its two ends labelled A and B. Above the line is a stepper for the number of waves and a radius dial. Two read-outs show the width of each wave along the line and the radius being used. A card gives the length of the central line, for example 12 cm.

The student predicts the radius from the card, then sets the stepper to the number of waves. The tool marks the line into that many equal parts and shows each flat side. Then the student turns the radius dial and the half circles are drawn at once: the first above the line, the second below it, the third above again, so the wave alternates. Each half circle is drawn from a compass tip shown at the middle of its flat side. The student watches whether the half circles join end to end along the line, and adjusts the dial until they do.

A radius that is too small leaves a gap along the line, and one that is too big runs past its end, and the tool shows both. Using the width of the wave as the radius makes the half circles overlap, with the extra length marked. A solved card shows the half circles joined along the central line.

The six cards are: a line of 12 cm with 3 waves (radius 2 cm); a line of 8 cm with 2 waves (radius 2 cm, the wavy line with one half circle above and one below); a line of 10 cm with 5 waves (radius 1 cm); a line of 9 cm with 3 waves (radius 1.5 cm); a radius of 1.5 cm on a line of 12 cm, where the student sets the radius first and counts how many waves fit (4); and a line of 6 cm with 1 wave (radius 3 cm). Hints come in order: how many half circles must fit along the line? Then: each half circle spans the line length divided by the number of waves, and the radius is half of that. Then: divide the line length in the card by the waves, then halve it. Last, the span and the radius are shown, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that the flat side of a half circle is its diameter and the radius is half of it, and that equal half circles on a line each take an equal share of its length, with the compass tip at the middle of each share.

(tool id M1.S4.T4.C12.2D1, concept M1.S4.T4.C12, topic: Waves from Half Circles)

Tool Name: Shape Property Checker

Tool Description:

A single figure sits on a small turntable in the middle of the screen. Beside it are a tilt control, a ruler and a protractor that snap to any side or corner the student touches, and a checklist with three rows: opposite sides equal, all four sides equal, and all four angles 90 degrees. Under the checklist is a name box with square, rectangle and neither.

The student first predicts the name of the figure from a card, then measures. Touching a side with the ruler writes its length on the figure and into the checklist, and touching a corner with the protractor writes its angle. Rows of the checklist tick as soon as the measured values agree. The tilt control turns the figure through any angle, and the student sees that the measured sides and angles do not change when the figure is tilted. Then the student picks the name that the checklist allows. When square is picked, a second tick asks whether it is also a rectangle.

Naming a figure from its looks alone is stopped, and the checklist rows not yet measured are shown. Saying that a square is not a rectangle shows that the square passes both rectangle checks, equal opposite sides and four right angles, so it is a special rectangle. A solved card shows the measured sides and angles and the ticked checklist.

The six cards are: four sides of 4 cm and four angles of 90 degrees (a square, which is also a rectangle); sides of 6 cm and 3 cm with four right angles (a rectangle, not a square); four sides of 5 cm with angles of 60 and 120 degrees, a slanted diamond (neither); a square of side 4 cm tilted by 45 degrees (still a square); opposite sides of 5 cm and 3 cm with angles of 70 and 110 degrees (neither); and sides of 5 cm and 5 cm with four right angles (a square). Hints come in order: what must be true for a rectangle? Then: a rectangle has four angles of 90 degrees and equal opposite sides, and a square also has all sides equal. Then: measure the angles first, then compare the sides in the card. Last, the checklist is shown filled in, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns to tell squares and rectangles apart by measuring, that equal sides alone do not give right angles, that right angles alone do not give equal sides, and that tilting a figure changes nothing.

(tool id M2.S10.T10.C30.2D1, concept M2.S10.T10.C30, topic: What Makes a Square or a Rectangle)

Tool Name: Construction Stepper

Tool Description:

The student faces an empty drawing sheet with a tray of three instruments on one side, a ruler, a protractor and a compass, and a list of numbered steps on the other side, with only the first step lit. A card at the top names the figure, for example a square of side 5 cm. A measuring read-out lists every side and angle drawn so far, and a Check button waits at the bottom.

The student predicts the length of the fourth side, then works down the list. Step one is the first side, drawn with the ruler from the zero mark. Step two is a perpendicular at one end of it, and step three is another at the other end, each drawn with the protractor. Step four marks the given length on each perpendicular, and here the student chooses the ruler or the compass, keeping the compass opening at the length and putting the tip at each end in turn. Step five joins the last two corners. Each step unlocks only when the one before it is done, and the student picks the instrument for the step before drawing.

Pressing Check measures every side and angle. Measuring from the end of the ruler instead of the zero mark gives a side that is too short, and Check shows the actual length. Marking an equal length on only one perpendicular leaves the figure open, and the tool shows the gap. On the rectangle cards the lengths marked on the perpendiculars differ from the first side, so the student sees that only opposite sides match. A solved card shows every step in order with the final sides and angles.

The six cards are: a square of side 5 cm, the fourth side (5 cm); a rectangle 6 cm by 4 cm, the side opposite the 6 cm side (6 cm); a square of side 3 cm, each angle (90 degrees); a rectangle 8 cm by 3 cm, how many lengths must be marked by compass or ruler (two, one on each perpendicular); a rectangle 5 cm by 2 cm, the side opposite the 2 cm side (2 cm); and a rectangle 7 cm by 4 cm, each angle (90 degrees). Hints come in order: which side is drawn first? Then: perpendiculars at the two ends of the first side stay the same distance apart, so the opposite side matches it. Then: mark the length from the card on each perpendicular, then join the two marks. Last, each step is shown done in order, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that only some lengths and angles need to be drawn, because the properties of the square and the rectangle fix the rest.

(tool id M2.S16.T16.C47.2D1, concept M2.S16.T16.C47, topic: Constructing a Square)

Tool Name: Rectangle Distance Explorer

Tool Description:

A rectangle ABCD stands in the middle of the screen, starting at AB = 7 cm and BC = 4 cm. Two sliders change its sides. A point X sits on side AD and a point Y on the opposite side BC, and each can be dragged along its side. A segment XY joins them, with a live read-out of its length. Beside the rectangle is an empty table with three columns: distance of X from A, distance of Y from B, and length of XY. A Record button adds a row.

The student first predicts where XY will be shortest and where it will be longest. Then they drag X and Y to many positions and press Record each time, so the table fills with cases. For instance, X 5 mm from A and Y 3 cm from B gives about 7.4 cm, X and Y both 1 cm away give 7 cm, and X 2 cm and Y 4 cm away give about 7.3 cm. The student sorts or scans the table to see which rows are shortest and longest.

When X and Y are the same distance from A and from B, the tool shades ABYX and shows it as a rectangle, with XY the same as AB. A slanting XY looks shorter to the eye than it is, so the table shows the slanting rows longer than the level ones. When X is at A and Y is at C, or X is at D and Y is at B, the segment turns into the diagonal AC or BD and is marked as the longest. A solved card shows the position and the XY read-out.

The six cards are: a rectangle 7 cm by 4 cm with X and Y on the two 4 cm sides, the shortest XY (7 cm); the same rectangle, the longest XY to the nearest tenth (8.1 cm); a rectangle 6 cm by 3 cm, points on the two 3 cm sides, the shortest XY (6 cm); a rectangle 4 cm by 3 cm, the longest XY (5 cm); a rectangle 8 cm by 6 cm, the longest XY (10 cm); and a rectangle 12 cm by 5 cm, the longest XY (13 cm). Hints come in order: which two sides do X and Y slide on? Then: XY is shortest when X and Y are level and longest when they are at opposite corners. Then: slide the points to the level position and to opposite corners, and read XY. Last, both positions are shown with their XY, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that level points give the shortest XY, equal to the side length, that moving the points apart in height makes XY longer, and that the longest XY is a diagonal of the rectangle.

(tool id M2.S19.T19.C61.2D1, concept M2.S19.T19.C61, topic: How Far Apart Can Two Points Be?)

Tool Name: Diagonal Explorer

Tool Description:

Rectangle PQRS fills the screen with both of its diagonals, PR and QS, drawn and labelled with their lengths. Two sliders change the sides. At each corner, the two small angles made by the diagonals are marked with read-outs, eight angles in all, with the matching ones in the same colour, and a corner total sits beside them.

The student first predicts, from the card, a diagonal length or an angle, then moves the sliders. They begin with a rectangle 6 cm by 4 cm and see both diagonals read about 7.2 cm. As the sides are changed, both diagonals change together and stay equal. The two angles at one corner read about 34 and 56 degrees, and their total reads 90 degrees. The four angles next to the long sides share one colour and the four next to the short sides share another, so the student can see which of the eight match.

A student who expects the longer side pair to give a longer diagonal sees both diagonals equal. A student who expects the two corner angles to be equal sees them equal only when the two sides are equal, and then the two colours merge at 45 degrees each. A solved card shows both diagonals and the corner angles.

The six cards ask: a rectangle 6 cm by 4 cm, the diagonal to the nearest tenth (7.2 cm); 8 cm by 6 cm, the diagonal (10 cm); one angle at a corner is 50 degrees, the other angle (40 degrees); 12 cm by 5 cm, the diagonal (13 cm); a square of side 5 cm, each small angle at a corner (45 degrees); and 4 cm by 3 cm, how long is the second diagonal (5 cm, the same as the first). Hints come in order: does the card ask for a length or an angle? Then: the two diagonals are equal, and the two angles at a corner add up to 90 degrees. Then: set the sides from the card and read the diagonal, or take the angle from 90 degrees. Last, the diagonals and the angles are shown, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that the two diagonals of a rectangle are always equal, that the two angles a diagonal makes at a corner add up to 90 degrees, and that they are equal only for a square.

(tool id M3.S26.T26.C95.2D1, concept M3.S26.T26.C95, topic: Diagonals of a Rectangle)

Tool Name: Side and Diagonal Builder

Tool Description:

The sheet opens with a task card at the top, a ruler, and a compass whose opening can be set by typing a length or by dragging. The card gives one side and the diagonal of a rectangle, for example side 5 cm and diagonal 7 cm. A button named Complete the rectangle waits at the bottom, and a read-out for the second side is empty.

The student first predicts whether the second side will be shorter or longer than the diagonal. Then they draw the base CD of the given length with the ruler, and a perpendicular at C, called the line l. The corner B must be on l, and it must be as far from D as the diagonal is. So the student sets the compass to the diagonal, puts the tip on the far end of the base, D, and sweeps a short arc across l. The point where the arc meets l is B, and the tool marks it and shows its distance from D as the diagonal. Only a short arc is needed. Then the student presses Complete the rectangle. Perpendiculars at D and at B meet at A, the sides are measured, and the second side is read.

A compass tip on the wrong end puts B at a point whose distance from D is not the diagonal, and the tool shows the measured DB beside the one asked for. A diagonal shorter than the side cannot make an arc that reaches the perpendicular, and the tool shows the arc falling short. The corners are labelled in the order they are placed, so the finished rectangle is ABCD, and ABDC is rejected because B to D is a diagonal and not a side. A solved card shows the base, the arc, the third corner and the finished rectangle.

The six cards are: side 5 cm and diagonal 7 cm (second side 4.9 cm); side 4 cm and diagonal 8 cm (6.9 cm); side 3 cm and diagonal 7 cm (6.3 cm); side 3 cm and diagonal 5 cm (4 cm); side 6 cm and diagonal 10 cm (8 cm); and side 5 cm and diagonal 13 cm (12 cm). Hints come in order: which line is drawn first? Then: the third corner is on the perpendicular and is as far from the other end of the base as the diagonal. Then: set the compass to the diagonal in the card and put the tip at the far end of the base. Last, the arc is shown meeting the perpendicular, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a rectangle is fixed by one side and a diagonal, because a circle around one end holds every point at the diagonal's distance and so finds the third corner without trial and error.

(tool id M3.S32.T32.C119.2D1, concept M3.S32.T32.C119, topic: Constructing a Rectangle from a Side and a Diagonal)

Tool Name: Equal Distance Explorer

Tool Description:

Two dots labelled B and C sit on an open sheet. A slider sets how far apart they are, a radius dial sets the compass opening, and a read-out names the distance of any marked point from B and from C. A card at the top gives the case, for example B and C 5 cm apart with a radius of 5 cm.

The student first predicts whether the arcs will cross, then sets the distance between B and C and the radius. Pressing Sweep draws an arc round B, a ring of points that are all that radius from B, and then an arc round C. Where the arcs cross, the tool marks the point and writes its two distances, one from B and one from C. The student can stop the sweep early and see that only a short arc near the crossing is needed, not two full circles. Then the student reads off the distance or the height of the crossing point above the line BC, using the ruler on the sheet, or says that there is none.

Drawing only one arc leaves many possible points, and the tool shows the second arc cutting it at the point. A radius that is too small leaves a gap between the arcs, and the tool shows the gap as a length. A solved card shows both arcs and the crossing point with its two distances.

The six cards are: B and C 5 cm apart, radius 5 cm, the distance of the crossing point from B (5 cm); B and C 6 cm apart, radius 5 cm, the height above the middle of BC (4 cm); 8 cm apart, radius 5 cm, the height above the middle (3 cm); 8 cm apart, radius 3 cm, do the arcs cross (no, they stop 2 cm short of each other); 10 cm apart, radius 5 cm, where do the arcs meet (at the middle of BC); and 5 cm apart, radius 5 cm, the height of the crossing point above BC to the nearest tenth (4.3 cm, the top of the house). Hints come in order: what must be true about the distance of the new point from B and from C? Then: draw an arc around each point, and they cross where the point is that far from both. Then: set the radius from the card and find out whether the arcs reach each other. Last, both arcs and the crossing are shown, and a similar card follows. Afterwards the student can set both to 7 cm and see the top of the bigger house. Through doing, the student learns that an arc around a point holds every point at that distance, that the crossing of two arcs is the same distance from both points, and that the arcs meet only when the radius is big enough.

(tool id M4.S35.T35.C129.2D1, concept M4.S35.T35.C129, topic: Points the Same Distance from Two Points)
