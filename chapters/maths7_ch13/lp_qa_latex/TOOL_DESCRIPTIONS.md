# maths7_ch13 - tool descriptions (10 tools)

Tool Name: Variability Test Question Sorter

Tool Description:

A deck of six question cards sits at the top of the screen, with two bins below it, Statistical and Not statistical. Under each bin is a reason strip with three choices: the answers vary, one fixed fact, one person's reply. A closed panel beside the deck is labelled imagined answers.

The student reads the top card and, before touching anything, predicts its bin. Then the student opens the panel, which shows three answers the question would collect. For 'How many hours do the children in your class sleep on a school night?' the three answers are different numbers of hours, so the question needs a whole collection of values. For 'How many legs does a spider have?' the panel shows the same answer three times. For 'What is your favourite colour?' the panel shows the reply of the one person asked, the same each time, so there is nothing to collect and summarise. The student then drags the card into a bin and taps the reason that fits.

The six cards are the sleep-hours question (statistical), the spider (one fixed fact), the favourite colour (one person's reply), how much vegetable prices change across the weeks of a month (statistical), how tall the tallest tree in the school ground is (not statistical, one measurement, even though it is about height), and how long children in the area take to walk to school (statistical). A card in the wrong bin comes back with its imagined answers side by side, so the variation, or the lack of it, can be seen. A card in the right bin with the wrong reason stays where it is and the reason is asked again. At the end of the deck a count of correct cards and the reasons chosen appears, and any card that came back can be reviewed.

Hints come in order. First: who or what would you ask to answer this question? Then: imagine collecting several answers, would they all be the same? Then: if the answers would differ from person to person or day to day, the question is statistical. Last, the answers for the card are shown, the card is placed with its reason, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns to test a question by imagining its answers: a question is statistical when the answers vary and have to be collected and summarised, and it is not statistical when one fixed fact, one measurement or one person's reply settles it.

(tool id M1.S2.T2.C5.2D1, concept M1.S2.T2.C5, topic: Statistical Statements and Statistical Questions)

Tool Name: Bar Levelling Mean Finder

Tool Description:

The student sees a row of bars made of unit blocks, one bar for each value of a small data set. On the first card there are five bars of 3, 8, 10, 5 and 4 guavas. A total counter above the row reads 30 and never changes. Beside the bars are a dashed level line that the student can place and a check panel that stays closed for now.

Before moving any block, the student predicts the level by placing the dashed line at the height every bar should have. Then blocks are dragged one at a time from the taller bars to the shorter ones. The bars change height at once and the total counter stays at 30. If a block is lost or added by mistake the counter glows, because levelling never changes the total. If the student asks for the check while some bars are still uneven, the bars that differ from the dashed line flash. When every bar reaches the same height, here 6, the check panel opens and shows the total divided by the count, 30 divided by 5 equals 6, next to the level the student reached and the earlier prediction.

The six cards are the guavas 3, 8, 10, 5, 4 (level 6), the six bars 5, 4, 6, 3, 4, 8 (level 5), the hibiscus flowers 2, 7, 9, 4, 3 (level 5, the number that would bloom each day if every day were alike), the bounces 6, 2, 9, 5, 4, 6, 3, 5 (level 5), the four scores 26, 53, 2 and 15 drawn in tens and ones blocks (level 24, where a tens block has to be broken into ones to be shared), and the seven times 17, 18, 17, 16, 19, 17, 18. The last set does not level exactly: it has 122 blocks over 7 bars, so after every bar reaches 17 three blocks are left, and the student splits each leftover block into parts to share them. The level then sits between 17 and 18, which is 122 divided by 7, about 17.43.

Hints go in order: which bars are above the level you predicted, and which are below? Then: move blocks from the tallest bar to the shortest bar, one at a time. Then: add all the blocks and divide by the number of bars to find where they must stop. Last, the bars are levelled for you, the level is shown as total divided by count, and a new set follows. Through doing, the student learns that the mean is the fair share, that levelling moves units without changing the total, and that the mean need not be one of the data values.

(tool id M1.S5.T5.C13.2D1, concept M1.S5.T5.C13, topic: Average as a Fair Share)

Tool Name: Stacking Dot Plot Builder

Tool Description:

A strip of value chips runs across the top of the screen, one chip for each number in the data, and below it is a blank number line that the student has to set up. The student chooses where the line starts, where it ends and the step between marks. Next to the line sit a readout panel for minimum, maximum and range, and a toggle that adds a second data set in a second colour.

The first card holds the twelve monthly onion prices of the first town: 25, 24, 26, 28, 30, 35, 39, 43, 49, 56, 59 and 44 rupees per kilogram. The line must reach both the smallest and the largest price, so the student tries a line from 10 to 60. Uneven steps are refused, with the gaps highlighted, because equal spacing is what makes the picture fair. The student then drags each chip to its price, and a chip dropped at the wrong place slides back to the strip with its value enlarged. Equal values stack upward, as happens for 42 in the second town and for 16 in the week of newspaper sizes. Once the dots are down, tapping one shows its value but not its month, so the student sees for themselves that the dot plot has lost the original order.

The student then types the minimum, maximum and range, which are 24, 59 and 35 for the first town. A wrong range draws a bracket joining the two end dots, labelled maximum minus minimum. The six cards are the first town (range 35), the second town 19, 17, 23, 30, 38, 35, 42, 39, 53, 60, 52, 42 (range 43), both towns together, the seven newspaper sizes 16, 18, 20, 22, 26, 16, 10 on a line from 10 to 30 (range 16), six newborn weights 3.5, 4.1, 2.6, 3.2, 3.4, 3.8 on a line in steps of 0.1 (range 1.5), and fifteen counts of stories read, 6, 8, 5, 15, 3, 0, 2, 7, 12, 40, 10, 5, 0, 8, 1, where one dot lands far from the rest at 40. With both towns on one line, the student finds that only the second town has prices between 11 and 20, namely 17 and 19, and says which town is more spread out.

Hints go in order: which is the smallest value and which is the largest, since the line must reach both? Then: put the chips in order first, and let equal values sit on top of each other. Then: the range is the largest value minus the smallest value. Last, the dots are placed for you, the range bracket is drawn, and a new data set follows. Through doing, the student learns that a dot plot puts every value on an equally spaced line and stacks equal values, so spread, clusters and extreme values show at a glance, at the cost of losing which month each price came from.

(tool id M2.S8.T8.C31.2D1, concept M2.S8.T8.C31, topic: Know Your Onions: Describing Data with a Dot Plot)

Tool Name: Outlier Pull Mean Median Tracker

Tool Description:

A dot plot of a small data set fills the screen with one dot drawn in a bright colour. Below the number line two markers move live, a triangle for the mean and a diamond for the median. A prediction panel asks which marker will move further, and the student must answer it before the dot can be dragged.

The first card is Poovizhi's family: heights 170, 173, 165 and 175 cm, and a fifth dot that the student drags down to 118. While the dot moves, both markers slide and each leaves a trail, and when the dot is released the two trail lengths are written on the screen. The mean falls to 160.2 while the median stays at 170, so the mean ends up lower than four of the five heights. A wrong prediction replays the drag slowly with both trail lengths labelled. When a drag carries the dot across the middle of the sorted list and the median does change, the sorted list appears with the middle value boxed to show why. A Remove outlier button then shows the mean and median before and after, side by side.

Five more cases follow. In the stories read, the highlighted dot among the 15 values is dragged from 15 up to 40, and the mean climbs from about 6.47 to about 8.13 while the median stays at 6. In the seven newspaper sizes 16, 18, 20, 22, 26, 16, 10, the dot at 26 is dragged to 60 and back, and the mean swings between about 18.3 and 23.1 while the median stays at 18. The values 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 get two dots dragged out to 2 and 45, one on each side, and the pulls nearly cancel, with mean about 22.43 and median 22. With an even count, 155, 160, 164, 165, 169, 173, the dot at 173 is dragged to 200: the median stays 164.5, the mean of the two middle values, while the mean rises from about 164.3 to about 168.8. Last come the 29 palm heights, mean about 55.9 and median 56, where the student predicts and then checks what happens when the tallest tree is dragged to 100 feet.

Hints go in order: which marker uses every value in its calculation? Then: does the middle of the sorted list change when the end value moves further away? Then: the mean is the total divided by the count, so a bigger value makes a bigger total. Last, the trails show that the mean moved further than the median, and a new case follows. After all six cases the student states which measure resists an outlier. Through doing, the student learns that an outlier pulls the mean towards itself because every unit counts in the total, while the median, the middle of the sorted values, hardly moves.

(tool id M2.S9.T9.C35.2D1, concept M2.S9.T9.C35, topic: Outliers and the Median)

Tool Name: Zero or Missing Entry Sorter

Tool Description:

A question banner at the top says what the data is supposed to describe, such as the mean runs per match played. Below it is a row of data tiles, some showing numbers, some showing 0 and some showing no entry, and each tile can be tagged Count it or Leave it out. A live panel at the side shows the total, the count, the mean, and the sorted list of counted values with the median boxed.

The first card is a player's series, 57, 13, 0, 84, no entry, 51, 27. For each tile the student asks whether it came from something that happened. The 0 is a match that was played and scored nothing, so it is tagged Count it, and the no entry is a match not played, so it is tagged Leave it out. The panel then divides 232 by 6, not by 7, and shows a mean of about 38.67. A zero tagged Leave it out returns with the note that the player did play and scored 0. A no entry tagged Count it shows the count growing to 7 and the mean falling to about 33.14, so the student sees the damage.

The other cards test the same question in new places. England's eleven batters scored 19, 0, 0, 22, 158, 0, 184, 5, 0, 0, 0: every zero is a real score, so all eleven are counted, giving a total of 388 and a mean of about 35.27, while the sorted list has six zeros and the boxed median is 0, even though the team made 407 with 19 extras that belong to no batter. Sita's mango tree gives 0, 0, 8, 24, 41, 16, 5, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0 from January to December, and under the banner mangoes per month in season the student counts only the five summer months for a mean of 18.8 and a median of 16. Counting all twelve months instead gives about 7.83 and 0, and the words in season are highlighted in the banner when an off-season zero is counted. The class pet survey has 22 tiles with two no entry tiles, leaving 20 values with mean 3.6 and median 2. Game points 0, 8, 6, 4 keep the 0 as a game played for a mean of 4.5, and 8, 11, did not play, 13 are divided by 3 for a mean of about 10.67.

Hints go in order: did this entry come from something that happened, or from nothing at all? Then: a zero is a real result, and a missing entry has no value to add. Then: divide by the number of tiles tagged Count it, not by the number of tiles. Last, the tiles are tagged for you, the mean uses only the counted values, and a new case follows. Through doing, the student learns that a zero is a value and counts, that a missing entry or a value outside the question is left out of both the total and the count, and that choosing the data first changes the mean and the median.

(tool id M2.S13.T13.C52.2D1, concept M2.S13.T13.C52, topic: Zero, No Value and Choosing the Data)

Tool Name: Clustered Bar Graph Reader

Tool Description:

The student opens a clustered bar graph of worldwide rocket launches. Each organisation has a cluster of three bars, one colour for each of the years 2021, 2022 and 2023, and the bars run sideways with guidelines along the bottom. Beside the graph are an Identify panel to fill in, a reading line that slides along the bars, and a statement card with Justified and Not justified buttons.

The work has two steps, and the student starts with Identify. The panel asks what is on each line, what the three colours stand for, and what the scale is. The student reads two neighbouring guidelines, finds the gap between them, and enters that one unit length stands for 20 rockets. A scale entered wrongly flashes the nearest two guidelines with their values and asks for the gap between them.

Then the student infers. The reading line snaps to the guidelines and shows the value under it, so the student sets it at the top of a bar and records an estimate with the word about; a reading far from the bar top flashes the nearest two guidelines. The first reading is SpaceX, about 31 in 2021 and about 61 in 2022, which the student describes as about twice as many. Then come statement cards. Arianespace launched fewer rockets every year (15, 6, 3, justified). Roscosmos launched more than 60 rockets in the three years (16, 21, 18 make 55, not justified). CASC averages around 40 (48, 35, 45 make 128, and 128 divided by 3 is about 42.67, justified). A wrong verdict highlights the deciding bars with their readings. The last card asks for the 2023 world total, found by adding the 2023 bars, including about 25 for Others, and the student picks the range that holds it: about 220, so 200 to 400.

Hints go in order: what does each guideline stand for, found from the gap between two of them? Then: which bars does this statement talk about, and read only those. Then: add the readings for a total, or divide the total by the number of years for an average. Last, the deciding bars are read for you, the verdict is shown, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns to read a clustered bar graph in two steps, identify first and infer second, to treat every bar reading as an estimate, and to claim only what the bars can show.

(tool id M3.S15.T15.C70.2D1, concept M3.S15.T15.C70, topic: Clubbing the Columns: Clustered Bar Graphs)

Tool Name: Monthly to Daily Daylight Converter

Tool Description:

Two twelve-month tables sit side by side, City 1 and City 2, giving the hours of daylight in each whole month, from 210 hours for City 1 in January to 468 hours for City 2 in December. On the right an empty clustered bar chart waits, with a bar for a full 24 hours drawn beside it for comparison. A calculator strip below the tables divides by a number of days that the student chooses.

The student picks a month, chooses its number of days, 28, 30 or 31, divides the month's total by that number and enters the daily value for both cities. For June, City 1 has 564 hours over 30 days, which is 18.8 hours a day, and City 2 has 276 hours, which is 9.2. As each value is entered a pair of bars grows in the chart, and the student compares it with the 24-hour bar. Dividing by the wrong number of days shows the month's calendar with the days counted. A daily value over 24 is refused, with the 24-hour bar highlighted, because no day has more than 24 hours. The chart's vertical line uses 1 unit = 5 hours.

The cards are June for City 1 (18.8), June for City 2 (9.2), December for City 1 (186 hours over 31 days, exactly 6 a day), February for City 1 (257 hours over 28 days, about 9.18 a day), the question of what fraction of a 24-hour day is 18 hours (three quarters, so City 1 has daylight for about three quarters of a June day), and the full set, all twelve months for both cities. Then the pattern panel asks where each city peaks. City 1 peaks in June and is lowest in December. City 2 is lowest in June and peaks in December, at 468 hours over 31 days, about 15.1 a day. An answer that misses the opposite peaks shows both cities' bars with their peaks circled.

Hints go in order: how many days does this month have? Then: daylight per day is the month's total shared over its days. Then: divide the monthly total by 28, 30 or 31, whichever fits the month. Last, the daily value is filled in, its bar is drawn, and a new month follows. Through doing, the student learns that dividing a monthly total by the days in that month gives a fair daily average, that no day can pass 24 hours, and that opposite patterns in two cities point to opposite hemispheres, with the bigger swings belonging to the city further from the Equator.

(tool id M3.S17.T17.C77.2D1, concept M3.S17.T17.C77, topic: Summer and Winter at the Same Time)

Tool Name: Double Bar Graph Scale Chooser

Tool Description:

The student starts from a data table for two groups, beside an empty grid and a scale picker that offers several units per length, such as 1, 2, 4, 5, 10 or 200. The grid has room for about twelve units of height, and its vertical line is relabelled every time a scale is picked. Under the grid are draggable bar tops for each category and each group, a key box for the two colours, and a check panel that shows value divided by scale for each bar.

On the first card the student compares Grade 5 and Grade 9 choices of a superpower: water, air, space or none. The largest count is 13, for Grade 5 and air. A scale of 1 unit = 1 would run the tallest bar off the grid, and the tool refuses it by showing the overflowing bar. A scale of 1 unit = 2 fits, and the 13 sits at 6.5 units. The student then drags each bar top to its height, and a bar at the wrong height shows its value divided by the scale beside it. The two colours must be named in the key, and without a key they blink until one is added. Finally the student answers a question printed under the graph from the finished bars, such as which choice is the most popular in each grade.

The six cards are the superpower choices, the Jodhpur temperatures at eight times of day on two days with the scale given as 1 unit = 4 degrees C (so 43 degrees C is 10.75 units), the sports watched and played by a colony with largest value 1240 and a scale of 1 unit = 200 people (cricket watched is 6.2 units), the estimated and measured lengths of a pen, an eraser, a palm, a geometry box and a notebook with 1 unit = 5 cm, the onion prices of two towns with 1 unit = 10 rupees, and a last card where the student must choose a scale for values up to 96 rockets so that the grid has at most 10 lines.

Hints go in order: what is the largest value, since your vertical line must reach it? Then: try a scale where the largest value needs about 5 to 10 units. Then: bar length equals value divided by scale, for example 43 degrees C with 1 unit = 4 degrees C is 10.75 units. Last, a scale is chosen and the bars are drawn, and a new data set follows. Through doing, the student learns that one unit can stand for 2, 4, 5, 10 or more, that the scale must let the largest value fit, and that every bar's length is its value divided by the scale.

(tool id M3.S18.T18.C79.2D1, concept M3.S18.T18.C79, topic: All it Takes is a Minute: Runs per Over)

Tool Name: Justified Claim Sorter

Tool Description:

A table of average heights fills the left of the screen, with ages 5 to 19 down the side and, for each of 1989, 1999, 2009 and 2019, one column for boys and one for girls. On the right is a stack of claim cards, and below it three bins: Justified, Not justified and Cannot tell. A reason strip sits under the bins.

The student reads the top claim and taps the table cells it mentions, which lights them up. Take the claim that 13-year-old girls in 1989 are taller on average than 14-year-old girls in 2009. The student taps the girls' column for 1989 at age 13 and the girls' column for 2009 at age 14, and reads 143.2 against 148. The claim is wrong, so it goes into Not justified. Then comes the claim that 15-year-old boys in 2019 are taller on average than 16-year-old boys in 1989, which compares 159 against 158.9. It is justified, but only just, so the decimals must be read carefully. After choosing a bin the student picks the reason: the cells show it, the cells contradict it, it is about every individual, or the data does not cover it.

The other claims make the student think about what an average can say. 'All girls aged 13 are taller than all girls aged 11' is about every girl, and averages cannot settle it, so it is not justified. 'At every age 5 to 19, boys are taller on average than girls' fails at age 11 in 2019, where girls average 138.6 and boys 137. 'Boys keep growing beyond 19' goes into Cannot tell, because the table stops at 19. 'Average heights at every age rose from 1989 to 2019' is checked age by age and is justified. A card in the wrong bin returns with the deciding cells highlighted. A right bin with a wrong reason asks for the reason again. Tapping cells the claim does not mention shows the claim's own words underlined, so the student sees which words name the age, the year and the group.

Hints go in order: which age, which year and which group does the claim talk about? Then: find those cells and compare the two numbers. Then: if the claim is about every child, or about an age not in the table, the averages cannot decide it. Last, the deciding cells are highlighted, the card is placed for you, and a new claim follows. Through doing, the student learns to point to the exact cells that settle a claim, that averages describe groups and not every member, and that data cannot speak about ages or years it does not include.

(tool id M4.S21.T21.C93.2D1, concept M4.S21.T21.C93, topic: Telling Tall Tales: Data Detective)

Tool Name: Zoomed Axis Bar Comparer

Tool Description:

Two tall bars stand on a chart whose vertical axis starts at 145 cm instead of 0, so each bar is drawn only from the axis start up to its height. A slider sets the starting value of the axis. Live readouts show the length of each bar above the axis, the ratio of the two lengths, and the ratio of the true values. A prediction box asks how many times taller the first bar looks.

The first card shows heights of 165 cm and 155 cm. Before touching the slider the student predicts how many times taller the first bar looks. The bars measure 20 and 10 above the axis, so the first looks 2 times as tall, and the prediction box is checked against that. The readouts then show the true ratio, 165 divided by 155, which is only about 1.06, since a 10 cm difference sits on top of a height of more than 150 cm. A prediction far from the length ratio shows both lengths measured from the axis. The true ratio appears beside the length ratio whenever the axis starts above zero.

The student now slides the starting value from 145 down to 0 and watches the bars grow and the length ratio sink from 2 towards 1.06; at 0 the picture is honest, and moving the axis to 0 shows it next to the zoomed one. The next cards repeat the idea with heights of 160 and 150 on an axis from 145 (the bars look 3 times as tall, 15 against 5, while the true ratio is about 1.07), then with 12.5 and 4.5 on an axis from 0, where the lengths already give the true ratio of about 2.78, and with the skyscraper pair 300 and 86 on an axis from 0, about 3.49 times. On the last card the student picks a starting value that makes a 10 cm difference easy to see, and then states the true ratio in words.

Hints go in order: where does the vertical line start? Then: measure each bar from where the line starts, not from zero. Then: the true comparison divides the values themselves, read from the axis. Last, the lengths and the true ratio are shown for you, and a new pair follows. Through doing, the student learns that when an axis starts above zero, a bar can look twice as long without its value being twice as big, and that the true comparison comes from reading the values on the axis.

(tool id M4.S23.T23.C100.2D1, concept M4.S23.T23.C100, topic: Zoomed Charts and a Mean Decision)
