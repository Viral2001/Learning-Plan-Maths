Tool Name: Lakh Ruler

Tool Description:

A long ruler runs across the screen from 0 to 2,00,000, with a bold mark at 1,00,000 labelled One Lakh and light ticks at 50,000, 1,50,000 and 2,00,000. A round pointer waits at the start with a number box above it. Beside the ruler are a Below / Above switch for predictions, an empty gap bar, and a button that adds a second pointer.

For each card the student first predicts whether the number lies below or above the mark, then drags the pointer to it or types the number in the box, and the pointer jumps to the right place. A bar stretches from the pointer to the mark and says 'less than one lakh' or 'more than one lakh', in one colour on the left of the mark and another on the right. In the first card the student places 75,000, reads the bar and types how far it is from one lakh. Check writes the working in a line, 1,00,000 minus 75,000 = 25,000. The second card places 1,06,000 on the other side, and the working is 1,06,000 minus 1,00,000 = 6,000. In the third card the second pointer is added. With 75,000 and 1,06,000 on the ruler, a bar joins the two pointers across the mark in two pieces, 25,000 and 6,000, and the student types the change between them, 31,000, and sees that it is the larger number minus the smaller number.

The last three cards place 99,500, a gap of only 500, so thin that a magnifier opens around the mark; then 1,25,000, the same gap as the first card but on the other side; and then the pair 36,500 and 1,09,500, the varieties tasted in 100 years at one and at three a day. Here the student decides which of the two is closer to one lakh and finds that 1,09,500 is only 9,500 away while 36,500 is 63,500 away. A wrong gap shows the subtraction with the larger number written first. A pointer on the wrong side turns the bar to the other colour and names less than or more than.

Hints come in order. First: is the number to the left or to the right of the mark? Then: the gap is the larger number minus the smaller number. Then: subtract the number on the left of the mark from 1,00,000, or 1,00,000 from the number on the right. Last, the subtraction is shown with its answer and a new number follows. Through doing, the student learns that the distance from one lakh is a subtraction whose order depends on the side of the mark, that the change between two numbers is the larger minus the smaller, and that a number above one lakh can be closer to it than a number below.

Tool Name: Comma Ladder

Tool Description:

The student sees a row of digit boxes, as many as the number has digits and up to ten, with a ladder of place names under it that runs from the right: ones, tens, hundreds, thousands, ten thousands, lakhs, ten lakhs, crores, ten crores and arabs. A tray of comma markers sits beside the row and an empty words line waits below it. Each card fills the boxes with the digits of a number, with no commas yet.

First the student predicts where the first comma will go. Then comma markers are dragged into the gaps between the boxes, working from the right. The first comma must come after 3 digits and each later one after 2 more. As a group closes it is tinted, the ladder rung under its first digit lights up, and the words line adds the group with its name. In 4,50,678 the groups read four lakh, fifty thousand and six hundred seventy eight. In 27,05,300 the middle group is 05 and the line says five thousand. In 3,08,00,000 the last two groups are all zeros, so the line says only three crore eight lakh, with a note that nothing is said for an empty group. A comma in the wrong place shakes and shows the group size that belongs there, 3 first and then 2. A group read with the wrong place name is flagged and the ladder rung it should have used is highlighted.

The six cards are placing commas in 4,50,678, 27,05,300 and 3,08,00,000 and reading them; writing 90,00,500 in words (ninety lakh five hundred); turning twelve lakh six thousand ten into numerals; and placing commas in 1,00,00,00,000 and reading it as one arab. In the numerals card the student switches mode, enters the parts of the name against the lakh, thousand and ones rungs, and must write a zero in each of the three empty places, ten thousands, hundreds and ones, before the digits and commas appear as 12,06,010. In the last card all ten boxes are used, the ladder reaches the arab rung, and the student sees that an arab is a 1 followed by nine zeros.

Hints come in order. First: how many digits are in the first group from the right? Then: in the Indian system the first group from the right has 3 digits and every group after has 2. Then: drag the first comma after 3 digits and the next after 2 more. Last, the commas are placed, the words line is read aloud and a new number follows. Through doing, the student learns that commas cut a number into the groups that carry the names thousand, lakh, crore and arab, and that an empty place keeps a zero in the digits but is not spoken in the name.

Tool Name: Trading Board

Tool Description:

A board of six columns stands on the screen, one for each button: plus one, plus ten, plus hundred, plus thousand, plus ten thousand and plus lakh. Above it is the target number. To one side is a total box that shows what the counters add up to and how far that is from the target, and below the board is an expression line that starts empty. A list headed Ways Found waits at the side.

The student predicts how many presses each button will need, then taps a column to drop a counter in it, one counter for each press. The expression line rewrites itself with every counter, for 8300 first as (8 times 1000) + (3 times 100). When the total equals the target, Check adds this way to the list with its expression and its count of presses, 11 here. To find another way the student uses a trade arrow between neighbouring columns. One counter in a column turns into ten counters of the next smaller button, and ten counters can be traded back for one in the next bigger column. Trading one thousand for ten hundreds changes the board to 7 thousands and 13 hundreds, and the total stays 8300. A trade that is not ten for one, such as one hundred for five tens, is refused and the exchange rate is shown. A total that misses the target is highlighted with the size of the gap.

The six cards ask for two different ways to make 8300 (8 thousands and 3 hundreds, or 83 hundreds), 40629, 56354, 66666 and 367813, which brings the plus lakh column into use with 3 counters. The last card asks for 997 using 34 presses. The student starts from 9 hundreds, 9 tens and 7 ones, which is 25 presses, trades one hundred for ten tens to get (8 times 100) + (19 times 10) + (7 times 1), and watches the press count rise by 9 to 34 while the total stays 997.

Hints come in order. First: which button has the biggest value that fits into the target? Then: trade one counter for ten counters of the next smaller button. Then: check that the expression still adds to the target. Last, one trade is done for the student, its expression is shown and a new number follows. Through doing, the student learns that one number can be made in many ways, that a trade of one press for ten keeps the total but adds nine presses, and that every way is a sum of presses times button value that adds to the number.

Tool Name: Fewest Presses

Tool Description:

The student faces a calculator with six buttons: plus one, plus ten, plus hundred, plus thousand, plus ten thousand and plus lakh. Under each button a small counter shows how many times it has been pressed. The display at the top shows the running number, and next to it sit the target number, a total press counter and a Trade helper. A strip for the digits of the target stays covered at first.

For each target the student first predicts the fewest presses, then presses the buttons until the display reaches the target. Every press adds the button's value to the display and one to the total counter. When the display equals the target, the strip opens and shows the digits of the target and their sum beside the press total, so the two can be compared. A button pressed ten or more times is flagged with the swap that saves nine presses. Tapping Trade swaps ten presses of that button for one press of the next bigger button: the display does not change and the total falls by 9. A way that is not the fewest is still accepted, but it is marked with how many more presses it uses than the best way.

The cards are 8300 (8 presses of plus thousand and 3 of plus hundred, 11 in all), 40629 (21), 56354 (23), 66666 (30) and 367813 (28). The last card starts from a ready-made 23 press way to make 5072, with 5 presses of plus thousand, 6 of plus ten and 12 of plus one. The student uses Trade on the 12 presses of plus one, which leaves 5 thousands, 7 tens and 2 ones, 14 presses. By the third card the student notices that every total equals the sum of the digits of the target, for example 5 + 6 + 3 + 5 + 4 = 23.

Hints come in order. First: which digit of the target tells how many presses one button needs? Then: ten presses of one button can be swapped for one press of the next button. Then: make each button's presses a single digit from 0 to 9. Last, the presses are set to the digits of the number, the total is shown and a new target follows. Through doing, the student learns that the fewest presses are the digits of the number, so the fewest-press expression is the place value notation itself and the number of presses is the sum of the digits.

(tool id M2.S14.T14.C49.2D1, concept M2.S14.T14.C49, topic: Systematic Sippy and the Crore)

Tool Name: System Converter

Tool Description:

The same row of digit boxes appears twice on the screen, the upper copy for the Indian system and the lower copy for the international system. Under each copy is a words line that is empty at first. A conversion strip runs along the side with thousand, lakh, crore and arab on one edge and thousand, million and billion on the other, joined to show that 10 lakh is 1 million and 100 crore is 1 billion. A Compare switch sits at the bottom.

The student types a number without commas and it appears in both rows. The student predicts the commas and then taps the gaps. In the upper row they must go in groups of 3 and then 2, in the lower row in groups of 3. Each row tints its groups, so the student sees the same digits cut in two different ways. The words lines then fill from the groups. For 4050678 the upper row reads 40,50,678, forty lakh fifty thousand six hundred seventy eight, and the lower row reads 4,050,678, four million fifty thousand six hundred seventy eight. Dragging the 40 lakh group onto the conversion strip turns it into 4 million. A wrong comma group shows the 3-2-2 or the 3-3-3 pattern for that system, and a wrong unit in a words line is flagged beside the place ladder.

The six cards are 4050678, 48121620, 20022002, 246813579, 345000543 and 1020304050. In 20022002 the Indian row has an all-zero lakh group, 2,00,22,002, read as two crore twenty two thousand two, while the international row 20,022,002 reads twenty million twenty two thousand two. The last number has ten digits, and the upper row reads one arab two crore three lakh four thousand fifty while the lower row reads one billion twenty million three hundred four thousand fifty. In Compare mode the student sets two amounts, such as 500 lakhs and 5 million, or 640 crore and 60 billion, and chooses less than, equal to or greater than. A sign chosen too early is held back until both amounts are in one unit. Turning 500 lakhs into 50 million shows it is greater than 5 million, and 640 crore is 6 billion 400 million, less than 60 billion.

Hints come in order. First: how are the digits grouped from the right in each system? Then: the Indian system uses 3 digits and then 2 each time, the international system uses 3 each time. Then: name each group with its place word, thousand, lakh, crore or arab, or thousand, million or billion. Last, both systems are shown with their names and a new number follows. Through doing, the student learns that one quantity has two spellings, that only the grouping and the names change, and that lakhs and crores are turned into millions and billions by the fixed rates 10 lakh to 1 million and 100 crore to 1 billion.

(tool id M3.S17.T17.C61.2D1, concept M3.S17.T17.C61, topic: Indian and International Systems)

Tool Name: Rounding Line

Tool Description:

A number line fills the middle of the screen with one marker set between two neighbouring ticks. Above the line is a zoom control with five stops: thousand, ten thousand, lakh, ten lakh and crore. Beneath the line a box waits for the nearest neighbour, and to one side a table has five empty rows, one for each place. The first number is 6,72,85,183.

The student first predicts which neighbour is closer, then picks a place on the zoom control. The line zooms until the two neighbours of that place stand on either side of the marker, and the digit just to the right of the chosen place is highlighted along with the part that will be dropped. At the thousand the neighbours are 6,72,85,000 and 6,72,86,000. The student types the nearest one and presses Check. A correct answer is written into the table with the digit that decided it. A neighbour on the wrong side is marked and the distance to each neighbour is shown, so the student can see which one is nearer. Place by place the table fills with 6,72,85,000, 6,72,90,000, 6,73,00,000, 6,70,00,000 and 7,00,00,000.

The six cards are the five neighbours of 6,72,85,183, of 3,87,69,957 and of 29,05,32,481; then 76,068 to the nearest thousand and ten thousand (76,000 and 80,000); then a number exactly halfway between two neighbours; and last a number within 500 of 5,00,00,000. In the third card the student sees that one number can round up at one place and down at another: 29,05,32,481 goes up to 29,10,00,000 at the ten lakh but down to 29,00,00,000 at the crore. In the halfway card the student drags the marker onto the midpoint, for example 6,72,85,500 between 6,72,85,000 and 6,72,86,000, and the line states that a tie goes up. In the last card the student slides the marker around 5,00,00,000 and watches the five rows. The nearest thousand is the first to change, which gives the range from 500 below to 499 above, because at 5,00,00,500 the thousand moves on to 5,00,01,000.

Hints come in order. First: which place are you rounding to, and which digit is just to its right? Then: if that digit is 5 or more, round up, and if it is less than 5, round down. Then: keep the digits up to the place and fill the rest with zeros. Last, the two neighbours are shown with their distances, the nearer one is chosen and a new number follows. Through doing, the student learns that every large number sits between two neighbours at each place, that the digit just right of the place decides, and that the nearest thousand is the strictest of the five.

Tool Name: Halve and Double

Tool Description:

The student gets a product line made of number cards, with a running product box above it that always shows the value of the whole line. Under the line are three buttons: Halve, Double and Pair. The first card is 2, 1768 and 50 in a row, and the box reads 1,76,800.

The student first predicts which cards can be joined to make 10, 100 or 1000. Selecting 2 and 50 and pressing Pair merges them into one card, 100, and the product box does not change. What remains is 1768 times 100, which is read as 1768 followed by two zeros, 1,76,800, so the power of ten is multiplied last. When no pair is ready, the student selects one card and presses Halve, then selects another card and presses Double. In the second card 72 and 125 do not pair, and if the student tries, the cards pop apart again with their product 9000 shown. Halving 72 and doubling 125 again and again gives 36 and 250, then 18 and 500, then 9 and 1000, and the line becomes 9 followed by three zeros, while the box stays at 9000 through every step. A Halve on an odd card is refused with a note to halve another card.

The six cards are 2 times 1768 times 50; 72 times 125; 125 times 40 times 8 times 25, where 125 pairs with 8 to make 1000 and 40 pairs with 25 to make 1000, leaving 10,00,000; 25 times 12 and 25 times 240, ending at 300 and 6000; 250 times 120 and 2500 times 12, which both end at 30,000, so the student sees two different lines with the same running product; and last, find two numbers whose product is 12,00,00,000. In the last card the student builds two cards and the product box checks them. Many pairs are accepted, for example 1200 and 1,00,000.

Hints come in order. First: which two factors make 10, 100 or 1000 when multiplied? Then: halve one factor and double another if that helps a pair form. Then: pair the factors and multiply the pairs. Last, the pairs are joined, the product is read as a number with zeros and a new product follows. Through doing, the student learns that halving one factor and doubling another leaves the product the same, and that factors paired to a power of ten turn a long product into a short number followed by zeros.

(tool id M5.S26.T26.C95.2D1, concept M5.S26.T26.C95, topic: A Multiplication Shortcut)

Tool Name: Digit Count Predictor

Tool Description:

Two steppers sit at the top of the screen, one for the number of digits in the first factor and one for the second. Below them is a prediction box where the student sets the digit counts the product can have, two panels labelled Smallest and Largest that stay covered until a prediction is made, and a free multiply box at the bottom.

The first card is a 2-digit number times a 2-digit number. The student sets the steppers, predicts 3 or 4 digits and presses Reveal. The Smallest panel is built from the smallest numbers of those lengths, 10 times 10 = 100, which has 3 digits. The Largest panel is built from the largest numbers, 99 times 99 = 9801, which has 4 digits. Every other product must lie between these two, which is why two checks are enough. A prediction with the wrong count shows the smallest product beside it. In the free multiply box the student types pairs of their own, such as 12 times 12 or 25 times 40, and each product lands on a bar between the smallest and the largest products. The tool shows 144 with 3 digits and 1000 with 4 digits, so both counts really occur.

The next cards are 3-digit times 3-digit (100 times 100 = 10,000 and 999 times 999 = 9,98,001, so 5 or 6 digits), 4-digit times 2-digit (1000 times 10 = 10,000 and 9999 times 99 = 9,89,901, so 5 or 6 digits), 5-digit times 5-digit (9 or 10), 8-digit times 3-digit (10 or 11) and 12-digit times 13-digit (24 or 25). For the long ones the tool writes each smallest factor as a 1 followed by zeros, so the smallest product is a 1 followed by 23 zeros, and the student counts digits instead of multiplying. A correct answer states the rule with the two digit counts added: the product has that many digits or one fewer.

Hints come in order. First: which numbers give the smallest product and the largest product? Then: count the digits of the smallest product and of the largest product. Then: compare with the sum of the two digit counts. Last, the smallest and largest products are shown with their digits, the rule is read out and a new pair follows. Through doing, the student learns that an m-digit number times an n-digit number has m plus n digits or one fewer, and that checking the smallest and the largest case covers every other case.

Tool Name: Matchstick Builder

Tool Description:

A calculator-style display fills the top of the screen, each digit drawn from sticks laid out like the segments of a calculator. Beside it are a stick counter and a total box saying how many sticks are in use. A palette shows the digits 0 to 9 with their stick counts: 0 takes 6, 1 takes 2, 2 takes 5, 3 takes 5, 4 takes 4, 5 takes 5, 6 takes 6, 7 takes 3, 8 takes 7 and 9 takes 6. A list called Numbers Found waits at the side.

The first card asks for 5108. The student predicts the count, then builds the number from the palette or by tapping empty stick slots one at a time. The counter updates after every stick, and 5 uses 5, 1 uses 2, 0 uses 6 and 8 uses 7, so 20 sticks in all. The second card builds 42,019 and finds 23. A digit shape that is not a real digit is shaken and the nearest valid digit is shown. In the third card the student adds exactly two sticks to 42,019 to make a bigger number. Tapping an empty slot adds a stick and the counter ticks up to 25. One stick can turn a 0 into an 8, a 1 into a 7 or a 9 into an 8, two sticks can turn a 1 into a 4, a 2 into an 8 or a 4 into a 9, and a new 1 in front costs exactly two sticks. Each bigger number, such as 42,078, 48,019, 92,019 or 1,42,019, joins the list with its stick count.

The fourth card starts from 63,890, which uses 30 sticks, and lets the student move exactly four sticks. A lifted stick goes to a spare tray, placing it again counts one move, and the total stays at 30. Numbers such as 88,078, 66,006 and 99,985 appear on the list. The last two cards ask for any number with exactly 24 sticks, such as 2008 (5 + 6 + 6 + 7), and then for the biggest and the smallest such number. The student finds that the cheap digit 1 can be used 12 times, so the biggest number is twelve 1s. Three digits hold at most 21 sticks, so the smallest number needs four digits, and 2008 is the smallest.

Hints come in order. First: how many sticks does each digit need? Then: add up the sticks for all the digits. Then: to make a bigger number, change digits near the front first. Last, the best number is shown with its sticks counted and a new puzzle follows. Through doing, the student learns that the stick count of a number is the total for its digits, that 1 is the cheapest digit and 8 the dearest, and that the length and order of the digits, not only the stick total, decide how big a number is.

(tool id M6.S36.T36.C162.2D1, concept M6.S36.T36.C162, topic: Puzzle Time: Toothpick Digits)
