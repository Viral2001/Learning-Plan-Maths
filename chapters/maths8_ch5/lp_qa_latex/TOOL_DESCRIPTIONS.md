# maths8_ch5 - tool descriptions (10 tools)

Tool Name: Sign Flip Board: Parity of Signed Sums

Tool Description:

Four number cards sit in a row across the board, with a small plus or minus switch in each of the three gaps. Below the row, the result shows in large digits with an even or odd tag, and a change meter shows how far the last switch moved it. On the right a history list keeps every expression tried, above a tree diagram with the first number at its root and two branches, plus and minus, at every gap, ending in eight empty leaves. A chooser sets the cards to four numbers the student types or to four consecutive numbers from a chosen start, and in the consecutive view the cards read n, n plus 1, n plus 2 and n plus 3.

The first card opens with 3, 4, 5, 6. The student flips the switches, and each new expression joins the history and its leaf on the tree. A leaf not yet reached stays highlighted until all eight expressions are listed: 18, 6, 8, minus 4, 10, minus 2, 0 and minus 12. Negative results are tagged even too, which clears the doubt that a negative number cannot be even. From the second expression on, the student taps even or odd before the result is revealed. A wrong guess brings up the change meter, which shows the last switch moved the result by twice the number switched, and every result is checked against the parity of the all plus sum.

The six cards ask the student to: list all eight expressions for 3, 4, 5, 6; switch plus 4 to minus 4 in 3 plus 4 minus 5 minus 6 and predict the change first, which is 8, twice 4; choose four consecutive numbers and find the three results that always appear, minus 4, minus 2 and 0, while the others grow with the start, such as 2n and 2n plus 4; try 2, 7, 4, 9 and decide whether any expression can be even, finding that all are because the all plus sum is 22; add a fifth card and see whether one parity still holds; and find four numbers whose eight results are all odd, which needs an odd all plus sum.

Hints come in order. The first asks what happens to the result when one sign switches. The second asks the student to compare the result before and after a switch and see how far apart they are. The third says that switching plus b to minus b takes b away twice, so the result moves by 2b. The last shows the change as twice the number, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that every switch moves the result by twice a number, so no switch can change whether it is even or odd, however many cards are in the row.

(tool id M1.S2.T2.C6.2D1, concept M1.S2.T2.C6, topic: Consecutive Numbers and Signs)

Tool Name: Dot Grid: Pairs of Even Numbers

Tool Description:

Two stacks of dots stand side by side on a grid that is always four columns wide. Above each stack is a number slot that takes any even number. As soon as a number is entered, its dots fill rows of four from the top, and any dots that do not make a full row are drawn in a different colour as a short last row. Each stack carries a label: 4p when no dots are left over and 4p plus 2 when two are left, with p shown as the count of full rows. Under the grid a read-out gives the full rows, the leftover dots and the remainder on division by 4, and a Join button waits between the stacks.

Before pressing Join, the student predicts whether the joined stack will be made of full rows only. Join slides the second stack under the first. When both stacks have two leftover dots, the two loose pairs are highlighted as they form one new full row. When only one stack has a leftover pair, the two loose dots stay at the bottom of the grid, and a wrong prediction of full rows only is shown in exactly this way. By joining different pairs the student sees that two numbers of the same type always make full rows, while one of each type leaves two dots.

The six cards use these numbers. Lay out 12 and 16 and join them: 7 full rows and nothing left over. Lay out 6 and 10: the two leftover pairs make one more full row, giving 4 full rows. Lay out 4 and 6: after joining, 2 full rows and 2 dots remain, so the remainder is 2. Sort 18, 22, 36 and 42 into the forms 4p and 4p plus 2, where only 36 is 4p. Find two even numbers that add to 30 and say which types they must be, one of each, since 30 leaves a remainder of 2. Finally join 6 and 10, then join 14 to the result, and say how many leftover dots there are and what remainder the sum leaves: the first join gives 16 with nothing left over, 14 brings a pair, and the sum 30 leaves 2.

Hints go in order. The first asks how many dots are left over when each number fills rows of four. The second asks the student to compare the leftover dots of the two numbers and see whether together they make a whole row. The third says that two leftover pairs of 2 make 4 dots, which is one more full row. The last shows the joined stack with its leftover dots and remainder, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that every even number is either full rows of four or full rows with a pair left, and that two even numbers add to a multiple of 4 only when both are the same type.

(tool id M1.S7.T7.C24.2D1, concept M1.S7.T7.C24, topic: Pairs to Make Fours)

Tool Name: Claim Tester: Always, Sometimes or Never

Tool Description:

A claim card sits at the top of the screen, for example: the sum of two even numbers is a multiple of 3. Below it a number picker offers the kind of numbers the claim talks about, here pairs of even numbers. Under the picker is a ledger in two columns, Fits and Breaks, and at the bottom is a verdict tray with three slots named Always, Sometimes and Never. The tray stays shut until the ledger holds what a verdict needs.

The student picks numbers, and each trial goes to the ledger as a fit or a break. For the first claim, 2 and 4 make 6 and fit, while 2 and 2 make 4 and break. With one fit and one break in the ledger, the Sometimes slot opens. The student is asked to try to break a claim before trusting it, because a long list of fits proves nothing. Always opens only when the student also gives a reason that covers every number, written with letters, such as a multiple of 6 plus a multiple of 9 written as 6a plus 9b, which is 3 times (2a plus 3b). Never opens when a reason shows that no number can fit: odd plus even is odd, and every multiple of 6 is even.

A wrong verdict is answered with evidence. Always with a break in the ledger shows that number next to the claim. Sometimes with no fit or no break asks for the missing kind of number. Never with a fit in the ledger shows that number next to the claim. The six claims are: the sum of two even numbers is a multiple of 3; the sum of a multiple of 6 and a multiple of 9 is a multiple of 3; the sum of a multiple of 6 and a multiple of 3 is a multiple of 9; if two numbers are not divisible by 6, their sum is not divisible by 6; when an odd number is added to an even number the sum is a multiple of 6; and a number divisible by both 6 and 4 is divisible by 24, where 24 fits and 12 breaks. Only the second is always true and only the fifth is never true.

Hints come in order. The first asks which numbers the claim talks about. The second says to try small numbers first and look for one that breaks the claim. The third says to write the numbers as multiples, such as 6a and 9b, and look for a common factor. The last shows the verdict with its fitting and breaking numbers, and a new claim follows. Through doing, the student learns that many fitting examples prove nothing, that one breaking example ends a claim of always, and that a reason written with letters is what proves a claim for every number.

(tool id M1.S10.T10.C33.2D1, concept M1.S10.T10.C33, topic: Always, Sometimes or Never)

Tool Name: Remainder Rows: Sums and Conditions

Tool Description:

Row length is the first thing the student sets, with a stepper from 2 to 7. A number typed into the board is laid out as full rows of that length, the leftover dots are drawn in colour at the bottom, and the counts of full rows and leftover dots are printed beside it. Large numbers such as 4779 are drawn as a stack labelled with its number of full rows, and only the leftover dots are drawn one by one. Join and Take away bring in a second number, and a strip of numbers along the bottom can be sieved for up to three remainder conditions at once.

With rows of 5, the student lays out 17 and 24: 17 leaves 2 and 24 leaves 4. Before pressing Join the student predicts the leftover. On Join, only the leftover dots of the two numbers move together. The 2 and 4 make 6 dots, five of them are circled as they form a new full row, and 1 dot remains, so 17 plus 24 leaves 1. Take away removes the 2 leftover dots of 17 from the 4 of 24 and leaves 2. A wrong prediction shows the leftover dots of both numbers joined before they regroup. The same is done in rows of 7 for 661, which leaves 3, and 4779, which leaves 5: their sum leaves 1 and their difference leaves 2.

In the sieve the student picks conditions, and the numbers that fail any of them fade. For remainder 2 on division by 3 and by 4 over 2 to 60, the survivors 2, 14, 26, 38 and 50 stay, and the tool shows that they repeat every 12, the LCM of 3 and 4, so the student writes them as 12k plus 2. Other cards ask the student to join two numbers that each leave 2 on division by 6 and then a third, where 2, 2 and 2 make a full row and the remainder is 0; to decide whether numbers with 3 left over in rows of 5 match 5k plus 3 or 3k plus 5, where 3, 8 and 13 fit the first while the second gives 5, which leaves 0; and to sieve 1 to 99 for remainder 1 on division by 2, 3 and 5, which leaves 1, 31, 61 and 91, of which 91 is a multiple of 7.

Hints come in order. The first asks how many dots are left over when the number fills rows of the given length. The second says to join only the leftover dots of the two numbers and regroup them into rows. The third gives an example: 5 leftover dots and 3 leftover dots make 8 in rows of 7, which is one full row and 1 left. The last shows the leftover dots regrouped with the remainder, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a number with remainder r is full rows plus r dots, that only the leftovers matter when numbers are added or taken away, and that several conditions at once repeat every LCM of the divisors.

(tool id M1.S13.T13.C49.2D1, concept M1.S13.T13.C49, topic: What Remains?)

Tool Name: Divisibility Rule Bench

Tool Description:

A number stands in large separate digit cells across the top of the bench, opening on 7316, and below it is a row of testers, one each for 2, 4, 5, 8, 10 and 11. Every tester draws a bracket over exactly the digits its rule reads, growing from the units end leftwards, and shows a pass or fail chip beside it. Under the number runs a place strip. The places that are already multiples of a tester's divisor are struck through and tagged, so the bracket covers only the places left over. The tester for 11 prints the sum of the digits in alternate places and the difference of the two sums, and a difference of 0 or any multiple of 11 reads as a pass. Its reason comes later in the chapter; here the student only sees that its bracket spans every digit.

The student first predicts, with every tester dark: if the thousands digit of 7316 were edited, which rules could change their answer? Only then do the testers light one at a time. The testers for 2, 5 and 10 reach only the units cell, the tester for 4 reaches two cells, the tester for 8 reaches three, and the tester for 11 reaches all four. 7316 passes 2 and 4, since 16 is a multiple of 4, and fails 5, 10 and 11. It also fails 8, because 316 is not a multiple of 8. The student then taps the thousands digit and changes 7 to 8. Only the tester for 11 flips, to a pass, while the other five are named as unaffected, and the predicted list sits beside the list that really moved.

Trap cards follow. On 5324 the student finds that the testers for 2 and 4 pass and the tester for 8 fails, because 24 is a multiple of 4 and 324 is not a multiple of 8, so passing two rules need not give a third. On the two digit number 96, the tester for 8 has fewer digits than it needs, so its bracket covers the whole number and says so, and 96 divided by 8 is 12. The student names the digits a rule takes: the tester for 4 on 7192 reads 92, and the tester for 8 on 9176 reads 176. In 6483 the student taps the three digits the tester for 8 reads. Then the student counts how many of the six testers pass on 3512, 4620, 7305 and 9856, which are 3, 5, 1 and 4, and puts the numbers in order. Stepping a number up by one reports which verdicts flipped, so 7316 to 7317 flips only the testers for 2 and 4.

A wrong answer highlights the digits the student's rule would have read beside the digits the true rule reads, so a student who tests 4 on the units digit alone sees the bracket stop one cell short. Support runs from the bracket of the tester in question, to the struck-through places on the strip, to the last two or three digits written as a number and divided. Through doing, the student learns that each rule reads only the places that are not already multiples of its divisor, 100 for 4 and 1000 for 8, so a digit outside the bracket can never change the verdict.

(tool id M2.S16.T16.C70.2D1, concept M2.S16.T16.C70, topic: Why the Shortcuts for 10, 5, 2, 4 and 8 Work)

Tool Name: Nines and Ones: Remainders by 9

Tool Description:

The student types a number and it appears as place value blocks, one stack for each digit. For 427 that is four hundred blocks, two ten blocks and seven unit blocks. Each block has a Split button. Splitting a hundred block separates it into 99 and a single one, a ten block into 9 and a single one, and a thousand block into 999 and a single one. The single ones slide into a tray on the right, where groups of nine can be tapped away one group at a time until fewer than nine are left. A divisor switch changes the groups from nine to three.

For 427 the student splits the four hundred blocks and gets four loose ones, splits the two ten blocks and gets two loose ones, and the seven unit blocks are already ones. The tray holds 13 ones. Before removing anything the student predicts the remainder, then takes away one group of nine and reads 4, the remainder of 427 on division by 9. The digit sum 4 plus 2 plus 7 shows next to the tray, so the student sees that the loose ones are the digit sum. A wrong remainder shows the tray with the groups of nine still to be removed, and every split block keeps showing its nines and its one.

The six cards continue. For 7309 the student counts 19 loose ones, removes two groups of nine and is left with 1. For 99009 each digit 9 gives nine loose ones, exactly one group, so three groups go and none is left. The student then builds a number with remainder 5 on division by 9. With the switch set to 3, the student tests 123456, whose loose ones total 21 and make seven groups of three with none left, so 123456 is divisible by 3. The last card shows 109: it ends in 9, yet its 10 loose ones leave 1 after one group of nine, so the last digit alone cannot decide divisibility by 9.

Hints come in order. The first asks what is left over when 10 or 100 is divided by 9. The second asks how many loose ones each digit gives when its block is split. The third says that the loose ones add up to the digit sum, and nines are taken away from that. The last shows the remainder from the tray, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that every place value is a block of nines plus one, so a number leaves the same remainder as its digit sum.

(tool id M2.S18.T18.C77.2D1, concept M2.S18.T18.C77, topic: Shortcuts for 9 and 3)

Tool Name: Excess and Shortfall Board: Remainders by 11

Tool Description:

Digit cells numbered from the units end hold the number the student types. Above every cell is a mark: one more for the units, hundreds and ten thousands places, and one less for the tens and thousands places. Two pans hang under the cells, an Excess pan on the left and a Shortfall pan on the right. Between them are two totals, a live difference read-out, and a dial numbered 0 to 10 that shows the remainder on division by 11.

The student begins with 462. The digits 2 and 4 sit over one more places and the digit 6 over a one less place. The student slides each digit into the pan that matches its mark, 2 and 4 to Excess and 6 to Shortfall, so the totals read 6 and 6, the difference is 0 and the pans level. Before the dial is revealed the student predicts the remainder, then reveals 0, so 11 divides 462. A digit dropped into the wrong pan is shown with its place mark. After each card the remainder on the dial is checked against the division.

Then come the harder cards. For 481, Excess holds 1 and 4, Shortfall holds 8, and the difference is minus 3, which the dial shows as a count back of 3 from 11 to land on 8. For 5529, Excess is 9 plus 5, which is 14, Shortfall is 2 plus 5, which is 7, and the difference 7 is the remainder. For 90904, Excess is 4 plus 9 plus 9, which is 22, Shortfall is 0, and a difference of 22 is a multiple of 11, so the number is divisible by 11. For 320185, Excess is 8, Shortfall is 11, the difference is minus 3, and the remainder is again 8. In the last card the tens digit of 4?2 is missing, and the student adjusts it until the pans balance, finding 6.

Hints come in order. The first asks which places are one more than a multiple of 11 and which are one less. The second says to count the units digit as one more and alternate to the left. The third says to add the digits in the one more places, add those in the one less places, and subtract. The last fills the pans, turns the difference into a remainder, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a number leaves the remainder of excess minus shortfall on division by 11, with a negative difference counted back from 11 and a difference of 0 or a multiple of 11 meaning divisible.

(tool id M2.S21.T21.C97.2D1, concept M2.S21.T21.C97, topic: A Shortcut for 11)

Tool Name: Combined Divisibility Trap

Tool Description:

The student meets a combination claim: if a number divides by a and by b, then it divides by a times b. Two steppers set the divisors a and b between 1 and 12, and the screen opens on 2 and 4 with the claim for 8. Beneath the claim runs a strip of numbers up to 200. Tapping a number fills three verdict chips from left to right: divisible by a, by b, and by the product.

First the student predicts whether passing the tests for 2 and for 4 must give 8. Then the student taps numbers. 6 gives pass, fail, fail. 8 gives pass, pass, pass. 12 gives pass, pass, fail, and the pattern of two passes and a fail is the sign of a counterexample. The student submits a number believed to break the claim, and the tool checks it instead of taking it on trust. A number that is not a counterexample comes back with the test it failed: 24 is returned with the note that it divides by 8. An accepted number such as 12 lifts out of the strip and docks beside the claim, which ends it, and the strip lists the others, 4, 12, 20, 28 and so on, 25 in all up to 200. Prime blocks show that 12 carries the factor 2 twice, while 8 needs it three times.

Next the student sets the pair to 2 and 3 with the claim for 6 and sweeps the whole strip. The result comes in two lines: nothing found, and then the reason none can exist, which is that 2 and 3 share no factor above 1. Only now do the HCF badges appear, 2 for the pair 2 and 4 and 1 for the pair 2 and 3. The student tries more pairs: 4 and 6 for 24, broken by 12; 3 and 9 for 27, broken by 9, 18 and 36; and 4 and 9 for 36 and 3 and 8 for 24, which sweep empty. A divisor of 1 is allowed, and the claim then holds trivially. Finally the student types a rule for when two tests combine. The tool tests it on a third pair, so a rule such as one divisor odd and the other even fails on 5 and 7, which combine although both are odd.

In the questions that follow, a wrong answer shows the student's number with its three verdicts. Passing both tests only tells that the number is a multiple of the LCM of the two divisors, such as 12 for 4 and 6. Through doing, the student learns that two divisibility tests combine into the test for the product exactly when the divisors have no common factor above 1.

(tool id M2.S24.T24.C106.2D1, concept M2.S24.T24.C106, topic: More on Divisibility Shortcuts)

Tool Name: Digital Root Dial

Tool Description:

A round dial has the digits 1 to 9 spaced round its rim like the hours of a clock, with a pointer that rests on the digital root of the current number. A box takes a number, and pressing Fold writes its digits as a sum, then adds the digits of that sum again, line by line, until one digit is left. The pointer swings to that digit. A second box chooses a number to add, and a Step button adds it, so the pointer jumps forward round the dial by the digital root of the added number and wraps from 9 back to 1. A trail beside the dial records every stop, and the remainder on division by 9 is printed next to the root.

The student folds 489710: 4 plus 8 plus 9 plus 7 plus 1 plus 0 is 29, then 2 plus 9 is 11, then 1 plus 1 is 2, and the pointer lands on 2. Folding 4581 gives 18 and then 9, so the pointer stops on 9, the number is marked as a multiple of 9, and the remainder shows as 0 and not as a root of 0. Before each Step, the student predicts where the pointer will land. A wrong landing place shows the root of the added number and counts the steps round the dial.

The six cards use these numbers. Fold 489710 and fold 4581. Start at 5 and add 11 five times: 11 has root 2, so the pointer goes 7, 9, 2, 4, 6. List the digital roots of the multiples of 4, which are 4, 8, 12 up to 36, and get 4, 8, 3, 7, 2, 6, 1, 5, 9, every place on the dial once. List the roots of 7, 13, 19, 25 and 31, which are one more than multiples of 6, and get 7, 4, 1, 7, 4, a cycle of three. Last, find the digital root of 9a plus 36b plus 13 for any whole numbers a and b: 9a and 36b are multiples of 9 and leave the pointer where it is, and 13 has root 4, so the pointer always rests on 4.

Hints come in order. The first asks for the digital root of the number being added. The second says to move the pointer forward by that many places and to go back to 1 after 9. The third gives an example: 11 has digital root 2, so each addition moves the pointer 2 places round the dial. The last makes the pointer jump and fills in the trail, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that the digital root is the remainder on division by 9 with 9 in place of 0, and that adding a number moves the root forward by that number's root.

(tool id M2.S27.T27.C121.2D1, concept M2.S27.T27.C121, topic: Digital Roots)

Tool Name: Digit Puzzle Solver

Tool Description:

An addition is set out as it would be in a notebook: two numbers stacked with letters in place of digits, a line under them, the total below, and a row of carry boxes above the columns. A tray of digits 0 to 9 lies beneath. The two standing rules sit beside the puzzle: different letters take different digits, and a leading letter is never 0. A notes panel lists every letter with the digits the student has ruled out. The first puzzles are A1 plus 1B equals B0, then AB plus CA equals BBC.

The student begins by predicting, with every letter empty, which digit a named letter definitely cannot be. Then the student drags a digit onto a letter and it fills wherever that letter appears. A digit already used is refused and both letters light up. A 0 on a leading letter is refused and springs back beside a ghost of the total drawn one column short, which shows why the number would stop having that many digits. In A1 plus 1B equals B0 the units column 1 plus B ends in 0, so B is 9 and a carry of 1 moves to the next column, where A plus 1 plus the carry makes 9, so A is 7.

In AB plus CA equals BBC the total has one more digit than the numbers added. The worst column is 9 plus 9 plus a carry of 1, which is 19, so in an addition of two numbers a carry is never more than 1. The leading column holds only the carry, so B is 1 by deduction and not by search. The units column gives A plus 1 equals C, and the tens column needs A plus C to make 11, writing a 1 and sending a carry of 1 up to the leading B. Together these give A as 5 and C as 6, and the columns turn green from the units end: 51 plus 65 is 116. The student then tries AB plus BA equals CAC, which gives 29 plus 92 equals 121, and AB plus AC equals BCA, which gives 91 plus 98 equals 189. Short drills ask for P when the units column 8 plus P ends in 5, or when the tens column Q plus 6 plus a carry of 1 ends in 4.

Check judges every column that can be judged. A consistent column turns green, and one that cannot yet be judged stays neutral. For a wrong grid the tool names the failing column but never the failing letter, so the student has to find which letter in the column is at fault. Clearing a letter returns its columns to unjudged, not to wrong. A puzzle with more than one answer is flagged and any valid answer is accepted. The student also writes the one deduction they are surest of. Through doing, the student learns to solve a puzzle by the units column, the carry and the size of the total, not by trying every assignment.

(tool id M3.S30.T30.C138.2D1, concept M3.S30.T30.C138, topic: Digits in Disguise)
