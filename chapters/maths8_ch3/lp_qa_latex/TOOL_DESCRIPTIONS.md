Tool Name: Stick Matching Board

Tool Description:

A herd of cows stands in a row across the top of the board, with a pile of sticks beside it and a second herd or pile below for comparing. No number is printed anywhere until the student has made the pairing. The student first looks at the two collections and predicts which one has more. Then the student drags one stick onto each cow, and a line is drawn for every pair. A stick dropped on a cow that already has one bounces back with a message that each cow gets one stick, so the pairing stays one to one.

When the pairing is done, every cow or stick without a partner glows, and the student counts only the glowing ones to answer. On the first card 8 sticks were kept in the morning, one for each cow, and 6 cows return in the evening. The student drags each returning cow onto a stick to take it out, and 2 sticks glow, so 2 cows are still missing. On the second card herd A has 9 cows and herd B has 6, and 3 cows of A glow, so A has more and B needs 3 more cows to match it. On the third card two herds of 7 pair off completely and nothing glows.

The last four cards turn pairing into arithmetic on piles of sticks. The student joins a pile of 4 and a pile of 5 and finds 9 sticks. The student pairs 3 sticks off a pile of 8 and takes the pairs away, leaving 5. A pile of 3 sticks is repeated once for each of 4 sticks to make 12. Then 13 sticks are shared into groups of 4, giving 3 full groups with 1 stick left over. A wrong answer replays the pairing and counts the leftover sticks off one at a time.

Hints come in order. First: which collection has an object with no partner yet? Then: pair one stick with one cow and see what is left over. Then: count only the unpaired ones, because they answer the question. Last, the pairing is completed and the leftover is shown, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that counting needs no number names, only a one to one match, that the unpaired sticks show what is missing, who has more and how many more, and that adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing are joining, pairing off, repeating and sharing out. Because every cow needs its own stick, the sticks never run out, but a big herd needs a big pile.

Tool Name: Gumulgal Name Builder

Tool Description:

Two tiles lie in a tray, ukasar worth 2 and urapon worth 1, with a name row below where tiles are placed from left to right. A count strip under the row shows how many objects the name stands for once the row is complete. Each card gives a number to name. The student first splits the number into twos and at most one extra one, then drags ukasar tiles into the row, and last an urapon tile if 1 is left over. Two urapon tiles side by side are flagged, because two 1s make one ukasar.

The first cards ask for 3, which is ukasar-urapon, and then for 5 and 6, which are ukasar-ukasar-urapon and ukasar-ukasar-ukasar. A wrong name shows its tiles counted as 2s and 1s next to the number that was asked for. On the third card the student tries to build 7 with the original words and finds there is no name for it, because the Gumulgal called every number above 6 ras. The tool then offers the extended name, ukasar-ukasar-ukasar-urapon, made in the same way of counting by 2s.

Next the student reads ukasar-ukasar-ukasar-ukasar-urapon back by counting 2 for each ukasar and 1 for the urapon, which gives 9, and extends the names to build 9 and 12, which are four ukasars with an urapon and six ukasars. The last two cards do arithmetic on names. To add ukasar-ukasar-ukasar-ukasar-urapon and ukasar-ukasar-ukasar-urapon, the student joins the two rows, trades the two urapons for one more ukasar, and counts that ukasar is written 8 times. To subtract ukasar-ukasar-ukasar from ukasar-ukasar-ukasar-ukasar-urapon, the student takes three ukasar tiles away and is left with ukasar-urapon.

Hints come in order. First: how many twos fit in the number on the card? Then: use ukasar for each 2, and add urapon only if 1 is left over. Then: check by counting 2 for each ukasar and 1 for each urapon. Last, the tiles are placed and the count is shown, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a number name can be built from groups of 2, that repeating the group word extends the names past 6, and that counting in one group size alone makes names long quickly, since 12 already takes six ukasars.

Tool Name: Roman Numeral Builder

Tool Description:

A target number sits at the top of the screen, with a tray of seven landmark tiles below it, I, V, X, L, C, D and M, marked 1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500 and 1000. The student drags tiles into a build row from left to right, and a running total under the row updates as each tile lands. For each target the student first splits it into thousands, hundreds, tens and ones, and then builds it with the largest landmark first.

The first card is 1222, built as M, C, C, X, X, I, I with the total climbing 1000, 1100, 1200, 1210, 1220, 1221, 1222. If the student places a fourth copy of one tile in a row, a flag suggests the subtractive pair instead, so four I's become IV. The second card is 2999, which needs pairs: MM, CM, XC and IX. Each pair snaps together and shows its value, for example XC as 100 minus 10, which is 90. A pair the Romans did not use, such as IC, is turned away with the reason, and the tool shows that 99 is built from XC and IX. The third card has 302 and 715, built as CCCII and DCCXV. On the fourth card MMCCCLXVII is already in the row, and the student reads it back as 2367.

The last two cards add and compare. For CCXXXII plus CCCCXIII the student joins both rows, counts 6 C's, 4 X's and 5 I's, and regroups: five I's make a V, four X's are written XL, and five C's make a D with one C left, giving DCXLV. For CDXC against CCCC the student builds both and compares the totals, 490 against 400. Here the flag only notes that CD is the shorter way, since CCCC is the numeral being compared. A wrong total shows each tile's value under it, so the stray tile is easy to see.

Hints come in order. First: which landmark numbers fit inside the target? Then: start with the largest landmark that fits and take it away. Then: repeat with what is left, using a pair like XC where four symbols would be needed. Last, the numeral is built tile by tile with the total shown, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns to group a number into landmarks, largest first, and sees that Roman landmarks grow by 5 and then by 2, so regrouping a sum needs care and the group size keeps changing.

(tool id M2.S8.T8.C27.2D1, concept M2.S8.T8.C27, topic: Roman Numerals: Grouping into Landmarks)

Tool Name: Egyptian Symbol Board

Tool Description:

A tray of Egyptian landmark symbols runs along the bottom of the screen, each with its value: stroke 1, heel 10, coil 100, lotus 1000 and finger 10000. Above it is a drawing area where symbols are dropped in any order, a count shown under each symbol, and a running total. Each card gives a number to draw or a numeral to read.

On the drawing cards the student first splits the number into thousands, hundreds, tens and ones, and then drags the right number of each symbol into the area. Draw 324 takes 3 coils, 2 heels and 4 strokes. Draw 2153 takes 2 lotuses, 1 coil, 5 heels and 3 strokes. Draw 1023 leaves one landmark with no symbols, and the student names it: the coils. Draw 10458 takes 1 finger, 4 coils, 5 heels and 8 strokes. The student can shuffle the dropped symbols into any order, and the total stays the same, because a symbol keeps its value wherever it is drawn.

When ten or more of one symbol sit in the area, they glow and offer a trade for one symbol of the next landmark. On the last card the student drops 12 heels, trades 10 of them for a coil, and is left with 1 coil and 2 heels, which is 120. On the reading cards the board shows 2 coils, 7 heels and 6 strokes, then 4 lotuses, 3 coils, 2 heels and 2 strokes, and the student adds up the values and types 276 and 4322. A wrong total shows each symbol's count times its value added up.

Hints come in order. First: which landmark symbols fit inside the number? Then: take as many of the largest symbol as fit, then move down to the next. Then: add the values of the symbols to check the total against the card. Last, the symbols are placed and the total is shown, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that each Egyptian landmark is ten of the one before, that a symbol is drawn at most 9 times because ten are always traded up, and that a numeral is read by adding the value of every symbol.

(tool id M2.S12.T12.C42.2D1, concept M2.S12.T12.C42, topic: The Egyptian System: Landmarks that are Powers of Ten)

Tool Name: Base Grouping Builder

Tool Description:

The student begins with a base selector that runs from 2 to 10, a pile of loose unit counters, and a row of landmark bins labelled 1, n, n squared, n cubed and so on, filled in with the real numbers of the chosen base. In base 5 the bins also carry the symbols triangle 1, square 5, hexagon 25, circle 125 and wave 625. A readout shows the number as counts of each landmark. Changing the base resets the bins and shows the new landmarks.

For each card the student picks the base, pours the target number of counters into the 1s bin, then groups n counters at a time and pushes each group into the next bin, where it fuses into one counter. Any bin holding n or more counters glows and asks for a group to be made. The student stops when every bin holds fewer than n counters and reads the counts. In base 5, 89 counters become 3 hexagons, 2 squares and 4 triangles. Also in base 5, 15 becomes 3 squares, 50 becomes 2 hexagons, and 137 becomes 1 circle, 2 squares and 2 triangles. In base 3 the bins show 1, 3, 9, 27 and 81, and 50 counters become one 27, two 9s, one 3 and two 1s. In base 2, 19 counters become one 16, no 8, no 4, one 2 and one 1.

The fifth card asks for 651 in base 5, and the student finds which bin needs the wave: the 625 bin gets 1 counter, with 1 hexagon and 1 triangle. A wrong reading shows each bin's count times its landmark and the total. The last card asks why no bin can keep 5 counters in base 5, and the student tries to leave 5 in a bin and watches them fuse into one in the next bin.

Hints come in order. First: what are the landmark numbers in this base? Then: how many counters make one counter in the next bin? Then: keep grouping until every bin has fewer than the base. Last, the counters are grouped and the counts read out, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that in a base-n system n counters of one landmark make one of the next, so the landmarks are the powers of n, and no landmark is ever needed n or more times.

Tool Name: Column Regrouping Board

Tool Description:

A counting board stretches across the screen with five columns headed 1s, 10s, 100s, 1000s and 10000s. The student places counters on the columns, one colour for the first number and a second colour for the second number. A regroup button trades 10 counters in a column for 1 counter in the next column to the left, and a column holding 10 or more counters stays highlighted until it is regrouped.

The first card asks for 3426: 3 counters in the thousands, 4 in the hundreds, 2 in the tens and 6 in the ones. On the adding cards the student places both numbers, counts the counters in each column, and then regroups. For 87 plus 78 the ones column holds 15, so 10 are traded for a ten, the tens column then holds 16, so 10 are traded for a hundred, and the board reads 1 hundred, 6 tens and 5 ones, which is 165. For 9608 plus 507 a chain of trades runs all the way to the 10000s column and gives 10115, with nothing in the thousands. For 1080 plus 46 the board reads 1126.

On the multiplying card the student shifts every counter of 522 one column to the left, and the board reads 5220, which is 522 times 10. A shift that misses a column is undone and the column is named. A wrong reading shows the count in each column times its landmark. The last card asks why a sum never needs more than 9 counters in a column, and the student answers by seeing that ten counters are always traded for one.

Hints come in order. First: which column does each digit go in? Then: count the counters in each column after both numbers are placed. Then: trade 10 counters in a column for 1 in the next column. Last, the counters are regrouped and the sum read off, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that adding in a base needs only regrouping by the same size each time, and that multiplying by the base moves every counter up one landmark.

(tool id M2.S18.T18.C64.2D1, concept M2.S18.T18.C64, topic: Adding and Multiplying with a Base)

Tool Name: Base 60 Place Board

Tool Description:

Three place columns stand side by side, labelled 1s, 60s and 3600s, with the landmark symbols hidden so that only the position tells which landmark a count belongs to. Each column has a count box, a total readout sits below, and a regroup button trades 60 in a column for 1 in the column to its left. For each card the student first predicts how many 3600s, 60s and 1s the number holds, then types the count in each column, leaving a column empty when it has no count.

The readout writes the places from left to right with a bar between them. 63 gives 1 | 3, 132 gives 2 | 12, and 200 gives 3 | 20. For 60 the 1s place stays empty, so the numeral is a 1 followed by a blank. For 3605 the 60s place stays empty and the numeral is 1 | blank | 5. If the student closes up an empty place, the numeral slides left and reads a smaller number, so the blank in 3605 closes up to 1 | 5, which is only 65. The card for 7530 gives 2 | 5 | 30. Every count must be from 1 to 59, and a count of 60 or more glows until it is regrouped. A wrong total shows each count times its place value added up.

The last card gives 2 | 75 | 10, and the student regroups so that no count is 60 or more. Trading 60 of the 75 sixties for one 3600 gives 3 | 15 | 10, and the total stays 11710, the same as before.

Hints come in order. First: which landmarks are 1, 60 and 3600, and how many of the biggest one fit? Then: take as many 3600s as fit, then as many 60s, and what is left is the 1s. Then: check that no column holds 60 or more, and leave a column empty if it has nothing. Last, the counts are set and the total shown, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a number is grouped into the powers of 60, that each count is from 1 to 59, that the position of a count tells its landmark, and that an empty place was left blank.

(tool id M3.S22.T22.C78.2D1, concept M3.S22.T22.C78, topic: Place Value: The Mesopotamian System)

Tool Name: Zero Placeholder Breaker

Tool Description:

The student sees a row of place columns with the ones at the right, each column headed by its value, a tray of digit tiles below, and a reading strip with a number line under it. The zero tile supply is switched off at the start. The board opens with 3 in the hundreds and 5 in the ones, and the tens slot is drawn as a hollow gap. The reading strip is hidden, and the student predicts what number the board will read before anything is shown.

Then the strip reveals what the board actually says. The 3 and the 5 slide together and read 35, while the expanded form beside it still says 3 times 100 plus 0 times 10 plus 5, which is 305. The number line plots 305 and 35 from the same origin and marks the shortfall of 270 between them, so the error has a size. The student switches the zero tile supply on and drops a 0 into the tens slot. The digits slide back and the board reads 305. The tool never places a zero for the student. The zero adds nothing to the total, but it holds the place open.

More boards follow. 4 in the thousands, 6 in the tens and 2 in the ones, with the hundreds left empty, reads 462 instead of 4062. 5 in the thousands, 7 in the tens and 2 in the ones reads 572 instead of 5072, the Chinese rod numeral that was written with a blank. A board for 3005 has two gaps, and repairing only one still reads 305. A leading zero, as in 09, holds nothing open and is dropped, and a single digit such as 6 needs no zero at all. In the rule tester the student writes a rule for when a zero is needed, and the tool runs it on 5000 and on 5. A rule that zeros matter only in the middle fails on 5000, because taking one zero away leaves 500, a tenth of the number. A rule that every number needs a zero fails on 5. A caption links the board to the chapter: the Mesopotamian numeral 2 | 5 with a hidden gap could mean 125 or 7205, and the Mayan numeral for 100 is a bar over a shell, with the shell at the end.

Hints come in order. First: which columns are empty? Then: close up the gaps and read the digits as one number. Then: which digit holds a place open while adding nothing? Last, the zero is seated and the board reads the full number, and a similar board follows. Through doing, the student learns that an empty place does not stay empty, it vanishes and every digit to its left loses a place, so a place value system needs a placeholder, and the rule is about places and not about numbers.

(tool id M3.S24.T24.C85.2D1, concept M3.S24.T24.C85, topic: The Empty Place: Mesopotamian, Mayan and Chinese Numerals)

Tool Name: Numeral System Sorter

Tool Description:

A sorting table holds a row of numeral cards, with a two by two grid above it. The grid asks every card two questions and only two: does the place of a symbol decide its value, and is there a symbol that holds an empty place open? Every card writes the same quantity, which opens at 125 and stays on screen. The table starts with four cards, tally marks bunched in fives, Roman, Mesopotamian and Hindu, and a shelf adds the Egyptian, Mayan and Chinese rod cards. The student first predicts which system would make a long addition easiest. Tapping a card opens it into one drawn symbol at a time with a live count, so the student sees the writing change length while the number never changes. For 125 the Roman card reads CXXV, the Egyptian card shows 1 coil, 2 heels and 5 strokes, and the Mesopotamian card reads 2 | 5.

The student drags each card into a cell. A card in the wrong cell is not marked with a cross. It travels back to the table and names the property it failed. The tally, Roman and Egyptian cards belong where place does not decide the value, since a lotus is always 1000, and where there is no symbol for an empty place. The Mesopotamian card, written with a blank for an empty place, lands where place decides the value but there is no zero, which is why the grid needs four cells. The Chinese rod card, which also leaves a blank, lands in the same cell. The Mayan card with its shell, and the Hindu card with its 0, land where place decides the value and a zero holds the empty place. The student then looks for a system for the last cell, where place does not matter but a zero exists, and finds that none fits, because a zero has nothing to hold open where there are no columns.

Next the student runs an addition demo of 87 plus 78 in the chosen card. In the Roman card the symbols pool and are regrouped by changing sizes: five I's make a V, two V's make an X, five X's make an L, and two L's make a C, giving CLXV. In the Egyptian card 15 heels and 15 strokes regroup by ten each time into 1 coil, 6 heels and 5 strokes. In the Hindu card the columns add with a carry each time and give 165. Last the student picks which property makes arithmetic easier. A reason such as it is the one we know is turned back, and the student is asked whether the reason rests on position or on the zero.

Hints come in order. First: does the place a symbol sits in change its value? Then: is there a symbol that holds an empty place open? Then: use the two answers to name the cell. Last, the card is placed with its two properties named, and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a change of system changes the writing and never the number, and that place value and a zero are two separate ideas, which together make the Hindu system the easiest to calculate with.

(tool id M3.S26.T26.C93.2D1, concept M3.S26.T26.C93, topic: The Hindu Number System and Zero)
