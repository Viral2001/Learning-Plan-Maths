# maths7_ch9 - tool descriptions (11 tools)

Tool Name: Twin Checker

Tool Description:

The student opens a stage split by a thin vertical line. On the left, a fixed amber outline of one figure is the target. On the right, a translucent grey sheet of tracing paper carries a teal outline of a second figure, and the whole sheet can be dragged. A move bar has three large buttons: slide (on by default), turn (a ring handle appears with the angle in degrees) and flip (one tap mirrors the sheet). A verdict strip along the bottom has three states only: not touching, overlapping but not landed, and landed. The challenge card sits at the bottom left, with a reset arrow at the top left.

On each card the student first predicts whether the tracing will land exactly or something will stick out. Then the student drags the sheet over the target, turns it with the ring, flips it, and watches the strip. With no gap and no overhang it says landed: 'Landed. Not a gap anywhere.' A near miss says 'Close. Look at the gap.' Pressing twins before the sheet has landed gets 'Show me. Make it land first,' so a verdict is earned by landing, not by looking. The student presses not twins only when no moving can work; on a pair that can land the tool says 'Not so fast. Try flipping it over.'

The seven cards climb in steps. Card 1 is two identical arrows, one slid to the right, so sliding alone lands it. Card 2 is a pair of L-shaped brackets, the second turned 100 degrees. Card 3 is two semicircle shapes that are mirror images, which only a flip lands. Card 4 is a flag turned 150 degrees and flipped, so both moves are needed. Card 5 shows the two signboard symbols with arms of 4 and 8 and angles of 80 and 55 degrees: the arms match but the elbow does not, so it never lands and the student presses not twins. Card 6 shows squares of side 5 and 6 centimetres; the student presses not twins and picks the reason, that one is bigger and no move changes size. Card 7 shows a target and three candidates, a turned copy, a flipped copy and a slightly wider copy, and the student finds the two that land.

Hints ask what the student did to be sure, since looking is a guess and the tracing is the test; then which of slide, turn and flip are still untried; then whether any edge still sticks out however the sheet is moved. Afterwards a tray of twelve loose figures opens, arrows, brackets, leaves and blobs in two sizes each. Nothing is asked: the student drags any two onto the stage and tests them while a quiet counter reads pairs tested. Through doing, the student learns that congruent means one lands exactly on the other, that sliding, turning and flipping never change a length or an angle, and that a pair of different sizes can never land.

Tool Name: Shortest List

Tool Description:

The student sits at a painter's desk. On the left is the original figure in teal, with a small grey chip beside each measurable part, such as side AB, angle B or radius. On the right is a lined notebook headed by a running count, Numbers written. One big button, send it, sits at the bottom centre. A picker of seven shape icons chooses the figure, and a record strip at the top right keeps the smallest list found so far for each shape.

The student taps a chip, which shows its value and writes it as a line in the notebook; tapping it again rubs the line out. Before sending, the student may press forced or not forced to record a guess about whether four painters across town will draw the same figure. The tool never requires the guess, but it notes when it was right. Send it turns the right half into a copy shop where four painters each draw a figure that obeys every line. If something is still free, the four drawings differ, sit side by side in amber and carry warning markers: 'Four painters, four answers. Something is still free to move.' If the list forces the figure, the four stack into one outlined in green with the word FORCED. A longer list than needed gets 'That works, but could you take one away?' and a shorter one than before gets 'New record.' Try a shorter list clears the notebook on the same shape.

Card 1 is a circle: one number, the radius. Card 2 is a 6 by 4 rectangle: length and breadth, since the corners are right angles already. Card 3 is the signboard symbol with arms of 4 and 8: two numbers send back four different symbols, and the student finds the third, the angle between the arms. Card 4 is a square of side 5, which needs one number. Card 5 is three roads from a crossing: three lengths and two angles make five numbers, because the third angle is what is left of the full turn. Card 6 asks the student to force a triangle using angles only. However many angles are written, the four painters draw four sizes, so the student presses impossible and picks the reason: angles fix the shape but not the size. Card 7 lets the student pick any shape.

Hints ask which measurements could change while the shape stays the same kind, since those must go on the list; then which could not change, since those need no place; then what happens to the four drawings when one line is taken out. Free play unlocks every shape, plus a parallelogram, a regular hexagon and a five-pointed star, and a pad for drawing a closed figure of up to six corners. Through doing, the student learns that a list is long enough exactly when nobody can draw a second, different figure that obeys it, and that angles alone never fix a size.

Tool Name: Three Sticks

Tool Description:

Three teal sticks lie loose on a bench in front of the student, each with a round hinge at both ends and its length printed along it. Dragging one end near another snaps them into a hinge, and dragging a joint swings the sticks about it. Three sliders on the left set the lengths from 1 to 12 centimetres. On the right, a gallery keeps every closed triangle built on the card as a small thumbnail, under a line that reads Different triangles found: 1. A flip button, a turn ring and a wobble button along the bottom act on whatever is on the bench.

Before building, the student predicts whether the same three sticks can make two triangles that are not twins. The student sets 4, 6 and 8, hinges the ends and swings the joints until the triangle closes: 'Closed. Nothing can move now.' Add to gallery saves it. Then the student tries to build a different one, by swinging the joints another way, or flipping and turning what is on the bench, and adds it again. A congruent build is stacked on the first thumbnail with a green edge and counted as one: 'Same triangle. It's just lying differently.' A genuinely different build would sit apart, but with three fixed lengths it never does. Wobble on the closed triangle gives 'It won't budge,' while two sticks hinged but not closed flop about.

Card 4 builds Meera's 40, 60 and 80 centimetre frame as a model at a tenth of the size, 4, 6 and 8, and meets the same triangle again. Card 5 builds 5, 5 and 8 and flips it: the flip lands on itself, because this triangle has a fold line of its own. Card 6 asks for a triangle from 2, 3 and 9. The sticks will not meet, so the student presses impossible and picks the reason: two and three together are shorter than nine. Card 7 asks for two triangles from 6, 7 and 10 that are not twins. Every attempt lands on the first, so the student presses impossible with the reason that three sides fix everything and any build is the first one turned or flipped.

Hints ask what the wobble does to the shape; then what the two shorter sticks add up to compared with the longest; then whether the new build, flipped or turned, covers the thumbnail already in the gallery. In free play the sliders reach 20 centimetres and a fourth stick appears, which wobbles. Through doing, the student learns that three fixed lengths leave a closed triangle no freedom, that a triangle closes only when two sides together are longer than the third, and that this is why a three-sided frame holds its shape.

(tool id M2.S5.T5.C22.2D1, concept M2.S5.T5.C22, topic: Three Sides Are Enough)

Tool Name: Name the Twins

Tool Description:

Two congruent triangles lie at different tilts on the stage, the left one teal and the right one amber, each with its corners labelled. Tick marks show which sides are equal, but the labels are not arranged helpfully. Below them is a statement bar with six empty slots: a triangle sign, three slots, the congruent sign, a triangle sign and three more slots. Two trays hold draggable letter tiles, the three letters of each triangle. A big OBEY IT button sits at the bottom with CLEAR, SHOW TICKS or HIDE TICKS, and ROTATE amber. A counter reads correct ways found, for example 2 of 6, and never nags.

The student drags letters into the slots, predicts whether the statement will land or leave a corner hanging, and presses OBEY IT. The teal triangle flies onto the amber one exactly as written, slot 1 onto slot 4, slot 2 onto slot 5, slot 3 onto slot 6, right or wrong. A correct statement lands exactly, 'It landed. That statement is a correct one,' and is saved in the counter. A wrong one lands crooked, a long side on a short side, with the overhanging corner in the failure colour, and stays until the student changes the statement: 'Look where it put the corners. Follow your own letters.' So a wrong statement is an instruction followed to the letter. ROTATE amber spins the amber triangle, so the student can check that a correct answer does not depend on how it lies.

Card 1 asks for triangle ABC and triangle XYZ to land. Card 2 asks for a second correct way, such as ACB with XZY. Card 3 asks for all six. Card 4 gives triangle HEN and triangle BIG and asks for the other five: HNE with BGI, EHN with IBG, ENH with IGB, NHE with GBI and NEH with GIB. Card 5 is the rectangle cut along BD, triangle ABD and triangle CDB. In card 6 Chintu wrote triangle ABD and triangle CBD. The student presses OBEY IT, watches it land crooked, and names the fault from three choices; the true one is that it puts a long side on a short side. In card 7 the student is asked for a correct statement with A matched to Y in a pair where A has to go to X. No order works, so the student presses impossible and gives the reason that A's two sides are a long and a medium one while Y's are a medium and a short one.

Hints ask the student to read the letters one by one, first on first, second on second, third on third; then to look at the sides before the corners, since the shortest sides must land on each other; then, on the rectangle pair, to pull the triangles apart and mark the shared side first. In free play a shuffle button brings new pairs with the ticks hidden. Through doing, the student learns that a congruence statement is an instruction, that six statements say the same thing, and that shuffling only one name says something else.

(tool id M2.S6.T6.C26.2D1, concept M2.S6.T6.C26, topic: Writing the Match Down)

Tool Name: Pull Them Apart

Tool Description:

The student faces a crowded picture on the left of a stage: a kite, an arrowhead, an X of crossed lines or a rectangle with a diagonal, with the given facts printed beneath it in plain words, for example AB = AD and CB = CD. In the centre is the bench, two empty dashed outlines. On the right is a two-column sheet headed what the question tells and what comes free. Along the bottom is a palette of one, two and three bars, a single and a double angle arc and a right-angle square, with a match meter of three empty slots above the bench.

The student predicts how many matches the two triangles will give, then drags a closed triangle out of the tangle. It lifts away, leaves a ghost behind so nothing is lost, and settles in the teal outline; the second goes to the amber one. Undo or return puts a triangle back. The student taps a palette mark and then a side or angle, and must do the same on its partner in the other triangle, because a mark is never copied across: matching is the student's job. Each fact lands in the right column of the sheet by itself. When the same symbol sits on a side or angle in both triangles a slot fills green: 'Match. Two to go.' A mark on one triangle only says nothing, and the meter simply does not fill. Marking the shared side on both copies gives 'Free side. You spotted it yourself, the question never said.' At three, a rule menu opens (SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, RHS, none of these). On these cards the matches are sides, and a rule that does not fit the marks gets 'Are they three sides, or a mixture?'

Card 1 has triangle RED and triangle JAM already apart, with three pairs of equal sides. Card 2 is the kite with AB = AD, CB = CD and the diagonal AC: triangles ABC and ADC give two given matches and the free side AC. Card 3 asks what AC does to the angle at A. The twins are known, so the angle at A in one triangle equals the angle at A in the other, and AC cuts it in two equal parts, which the sheet files as free. Card 4 is the arrowhead with DF = DG, FE = GE and the line DE, and card 5 asks for its congruence: write it opens six letter slots, and F lands on G while D and E land on themselves. Card 6 is the rectangle with the diagonal BD. Card 7 shows two triangles sharing a side with one other fact given: the meter stops at two, and the student presses not enough with the reason that two matches give no way to a third.

Hints ask which side the finger traces twice, once in each triangle; then whether both columns are filled in; then whether the three matches are sides or angles. Free play adds twelve tangles, such as an hourglass of crossed lines. Through doing, the student learns that pulling the triangles apart and marking them makes a tangled picture easy, that a shared side is equal to itself, and that three matches that fit a rule make twins while two do not.

(tool id M2.S8.T8.C35.2D1, concept M2.S8.T8.C35, topic: Pulling Two Triangles Apart)

Tool Name: Swinging Gate

Tool Description:

The student looks at a horizontal base PQ in teal, with P at the left and a long grey ray leaving P at the set angle. At Q hangs a side with a round hinge pin, the gate. A dashed reach line runs from Q straight down to the ray with its length printed, so the student sees how long the gate must be to reach at all. Three dials on the left set the base PQ from 3 to 10 centimetres, the angle at P from 10 to 150 degrees and the gate length from 1 to 12 centimetres in half-centimetre steps. One big lever, SWING, sits below them, a large landing counter reads 0, 1 or 2, and a tray at the bottom holds up to two built triangles.

Before each swing the student predicts how many places the gate can land on the ray: none, one or two. SWING sweeps the gate slowly through its arc and leaves a grey trail. Each time the tip touches the ray, a dot drops and stays. Tapping a dot builds that triangle in the tray, and COMPARE lays one over the other. For base 6, angle 30 and gate 4 there are two landings, about 2.55 and 7.84 centimetres from P, and the triangles do not match: 'Same three measurements. Different triangles.' CLEAR wipes the dots, and NO LANDING lets the student declare that the gate cannot reach.

Card 1 swings that gate of 4 and counts two. Card 2 builds both and puts one on the other. Card 3 shortens the gate to 2.5: 'It never reached,' and the reach line, 3 centimetres, shows why. Card 4 sets exactly 3 and swings very slowly: it just touches, one landing. Cards 5 and 6 set the gate to 6, the same as the base, and to 7. Each gives one landing, because the other swing position falls on P or behind it, where no triangle forms. In card 7 the angle switch moves the angle from P to Q, between the two sides; the gate is pinned and every length tried gives one landing. Card 8 asks for two landings with the angle at Q. The student presses impossible and picks the reason: with the angle between them, the two sides are pinned at both ends and nothing is left to swing.

Hints ask whether the angle is squeezed between the two sides given; then how far Q is from the ray, which is the reach line; then which gate lengths gave two landings so far. Free play unlocks every dial and a trail mode that keeps the dots of every swing, with a tally of gate lengths tried, so the student maps the lengths that give none, one or two. Through doing, the student learns that two sides and the angle between them force one triangle, that two sides and an angle not between them can leave two, and that the number of landings depends on the length of the gate.

(tool id M3.S10.T10.C45.2D1, concept M3.S10.T10.C45, topic: What Fails and What Works)

Tool Name: Angle Side Angle

Tool Description:

A base lies horizontally in teal, labelled with its length, with a round grey protractor at each end. Two rays run out from the ends at the angles the protractors show, solid teal up to the crossing and grey beyond. The crossing is a bright green labelled dot that moves live, with a readout of its height above the base. On the left, three dials set the base from 2 to 12 centimetres and the left and right angles each from 5 to 170 degrees. A fourth readout shows third angle equals 180 minus left minus right, and greys out when the sum reaches 180. A sum bar fills as the angles grow, with a red line at 180 degrees. A top-angle switch, COUNT CROSSINGS and IMPOSSIBLE complete the controls.

The student first predicts whether the rays will cross once, twice or sometimes not at all, then turns the dials and watches the green dot move: 'One crossing. One triangle.' Card 1 builds a base of 5 with angles of 50 and 30 degrees. Card 2 keeps the angles and changes the base to 9: the triangle is bigger with the same shape, so it is not a twin of the first. Card 3 presses COUNT CROSSINGS, which extends both rays to the edge of the stage and marks every crossing, and the answer is always 1.

Cards 4 and 5 use the top-angle mode. The switch hides one end angle and shows the angle at the top instead, and the student must work out the missing end angle and type it into the entry box before the rays will draw. Card 4 has base 4, top angle 35 and right-hand angle 75, so the left angle is 180 minus 35 minus 75, which is 70. Card 5 has base 7, top angle 40 and left angle 60, so the right-hand angle is 80. A wrong entry gets 'Check the sum. Three angles, 180 degrees between them.' Card 6 sets both end angles to 95 degrees. The sum bar passes the red line and the rays never cross: 'Those two angles have used up the whole 180 degrees. There is nothing left for the top.' Card 7 asks for the rays to cross in two places. The student presses IMPOSSIBLE and picks the reason: two straight lines that are not parallel cross once.

Hints ask whether the base is squeezed between the two angles given; then, in top-angle mode, to find the missing end angle first; then to look at the sum bar. In free play both angle dials and the base are free, and a ghost button leaves a faint copy of every triangle built, so a family sharing one base appears, with a quiet count of triangles built. Through doing, the student learns that two angles and the side between them leave exactly one triangle, that a known side and any two angles do the same job because the third angle is what is left of 180 degrees, and that the same angles with a different side give a different size.

(tool id M3.S11.T11.C49.2D1, concept M3.S11.T11.C49, topic: Two Angles and a Side)

Tool Name: Right Angle Rescue

Tool Description:

The student comes back to the gate stage met before, with three changes. The base angle dial snaps to 90 degrees with a click and a small square appears at that corner. The line from that end of the base is drawn upward and downward, full length, in grey. A fold lever folds the stage along the base, so whatever is above swings down onto whatever is below. Dials set the base from 2 to 12 centimetres, the base angle from 10 to 170 degrees and the swinging side, the hypotenuse, from 1 to 14 centimetres. SWING drops landing dots, teal above the base and amber below it, and a compare panel holds the two built triangles with SAME and DIFFERENT buttons.

Before pressing SWING, the student predicts whether the second landing will be a new triangle or the first one flipped. Card 1 sets a right angle, base 4 and swinging side 5: one dot lands above and one below. Card 2 presses BUILD BOTH, which puts the two triangles side by side, and the student presses SAME or DIFFERENT. Card 3 asks for proof, by pulling the FOLD lever. The upper triangle swings down onto the lower one and the figure goes green: 'They landed on each other. One triangle, two ways up.' A student who chose SAME after a successful fold hears 'And you proved it with the fold, not by looking.'

Cards 4 and 5 take the right angle away. With the base angle at 30 degrees, base 6 and side 4, both dots land above the base. Building both and folding, the triangles do not land on each other and are outlined in orange: 'These really are two different triangles.' Card 6 puts the right angle back and tries a swinging side of 3.5 on a base of 4. 'It never reaches the line,' because the side opposite the right angle must be longer than the base. Card 7 asks for two landings on the same side of the base with a right angle. The student tries bases and sides, presses impossible, and picks the reason: with the right angle the arc always cuts once above and once below, never twice on one side.

Hints ask whether the two dots sit on the same side of the base or on opposite sides; then what the fold does to them; then whether the swinging side is longer than the base. In free play every dial is free, the angle can snap to 90 degrees or roam, and a map mode draws a coloured strip of which side lengths give no landing, one or two for a fixed base. Through doing, the student learns that a right angle at the end of the base makes the second landing a mirror image of the first, so two sides and a right angle give one triangle, which repairs the two-sides-and-any-angle trap.

(tool id M3.S13.T13.C62.2D1, concept M3.S13.T13.C62, topic: The Repaired Case)

Tool Name: Fold the Isosceles

Tool Description:

The student gets a teal triangle in the centre of a white stage with three draggable corner handles. A panel on the left always reads out the three side lengths and the three angles live. The student can tap any two sides to lock them equal, drag a dashed pink crease from any corner to the opposite side, press snap to altitude, and pull a fold lever to fold the triangle along the crease. A small angle tracker shows the two base angles side by side, with a green link between them whenever they are equal. Press measure and press impossible complete the controls.

The student predicts whether the two halves will land, then locks two sides equal, drags the crease from the corner where they meet, and pulls the lever. The folded half turns amber and settles on the other half. If they coincide the figure flashes green and the word LANDED appears. If not, the overhang is outlined in orange with its size shown: 'It hangs over. This one doesn't fold onto itself.' While the student drags a corner of the locked triangle, the tracker keeps the two base angles linked in green, with no words, and the student notices it unaided.

Card 1 locks two sides, snaps to altitude and folds. Card 2 hides the base angles and asks whether they are equal. Card 3 makes the equal sides 7 centimetres and the top angle 80 degrees; the student works out 50 degrees each, then presses measure, and from this card on the base angles stay hidden until then. Card 4 uses a triangle with no equal sides and folds from each of the three corners: it never lands. Card 5 locks all three sides equal, and it lands from all three corners. Card 6 reads off its angles, 60 degrees each. Card 7 reverses the question: the student drags a triangle until the tracker links two angles and then looks at the two sides opposite, which are equal as well. Card 8 asks for two equal sides with different opposite angles. The student presses impossible and picks the reason: the fold down the middle always lands and carries one angle exactly onto the other.

Hints ask what the fold does to the two bottom corners; then whether the halves land when two sides are equal and when none are; then which angles the crease carries onto each other. Free play adds a trace mode that keeps a faint outline of every triangle made, and a gallery of six to recreate by dragging: equilateral, right isosceles, 3-4-5, a very flat one, a very tall one, and one with angles of 30, 60 and 90 degrees. Through doing, the student learns that a triangle folds onto itself exactly when two sides are equal, that the fold is why the two angles opposite them are equal, and that two equal angles bringing two equal sides is a second fact, not the same sentence turned round.

(tool id M4.S14.T14.C66.2D1, concept M4.S14.T14.C66, topic: Equal Sides, Equal Angles)

Tool Name: Proof Builder

Tool Description:

The student works at a board in three bands. The top band shows the problem's figure with its given facts printed beneath it in plain words, and tapping any side or angle pulls a fact block out of it. The middle band is the fact shelf, where grey blocks are what the question told and green blocks are free. Four free-fact badges sit beside it: free side, free angles, free from parallel and free from twins. The bottom band is the chain: three empty slots, an arrow, a rule slot, an arrow and a conclusion slot. A route counter sits at the top right.

The student predicts which three facts the figure will need, taps parts of the figure to collect them, drags a badge in when a fact comes free, puts three blocks in the slots, picks a rule from the menu (SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, RHS or none of these) and presses check. A holding chain makes the figure show the two triangles separate, land on each other and go green. An and so row then appears, where any further equal part can be claimed with the free from twins badge: 'Free from twins. You never measured it.' A rule that does not fit the blocks gets 'Look at your three blocks. How many are sides and how many are angles?' Two sides and an angle not between them make the figure swing the third side and show two possible triangles: 'That chain won't hold.'

The nine cards are: a rectangle cut by its diagonal BD, where AB = CD, AD = CB and the free side BD give SSS; the kite, where SSS and the twins show AC cuts angle BAD in half; the X figure, where AO = OD, BO = OC and the free angles at O give SAS and then AB = CD; the hourglass, where free from parallel twice and DC = AB give ASA; and two equal angle pairs with the free side BC, giving ASA and then angle BAC = angle BDC. Card 6 is an isosceles triangle ABC with AB = AC and a line AD to the base, to be shown with equal base angles in two routes: AD as the altitude with RHS, or as the angle bisector with SAS. Card 7 shows AB parallel to CD from SAS and the equal alternate angles of the twins. Card 8 is the square in two ways, SSS with two matchings. The counter reads routes found 1 of 2 on cards 6 and 8 and never reveals the second route; clear route or new route starts another and keeps the first. Card 9 gives two triangles sharing a side with one more equal pair, so the student presses not enough: two matches and no way to a third.

Hints ask how many of the three facts are sides and how many are angles; then to sketch and put a finger on the angle: is it between the two sides? In free play the student builds any two triangles, marks parts as equal, and the tool says whether it is enough and which rule it is. Through doing, the student learns that an argument is a chain from facts to a rule to twins to every matching part, and that knowing the facts are not enough is a real answer.

(tool id M4.S16.T16.C73.2D1, concept M4.S16.T16.C73, topic: Putting the Rules to Work)

Tool Name: Six Congruent Pieces

Tool Description:

A five by five grid sits in the centre of the stage, with the middle square solid green and out of play and the other 24 squares white with thin borders. Six colours are ready, one for each piece. On the right, a panel holds small thumbnails of the pieces built so far, and two pieces that are twins, turns and flips allowed, are joined by a green line. Two plain counters are always visible, squares used and pieces made, for example squares used: 20 of 24 and pieces: 5. A control bar has next piece, undo, clear all and check all, and each thumbnail has rotate and flip buttons.

Card 1 comes before any painting: how many squares are in play, and how many must each piece have? The student works out 24 squares shared by six pieces, so 4 each, which settles the size before the shape. Card 2 asks for one piece of four squares of any shape. The student taps squares to paint them into the current piece, taps a painted square again to remove it, and presses next piece to close it. Card 3 asks for a second piece congruent to the first. The student can rotate or flip a thumbnail by hand and watch it land on the other, and when the two are twins the panel draws a green link with no words. Pieces that are not twins have no link, so the student learns to trust the link and the landing test, not the eye.

Card 4 asks for all six. The counters rise as squares are painted, and check all lights only when all 24 squares are used and there are exactly six pieces. Then each piece lifts, turns and lands on the first, as a tracing would: 'Six pieces. Every one a twin.' The full solution is six L-shaped pieces, some of them turned or flipped. Card 5 asks for the 24 squares in 5 congruent pieces. After trying, the student presses impossible and picks the reason: 24 does not divide by 5, so the pieces could not all be the same size.

Hints ask how many squares each piece must have before any shape is tried; then whether a turned or flipped piece is still a twin, with the thumbnail lifted and turned by hand; then what the panel's link says about two pieces. In free play the grid resizes to 4 by 4, 5 by 5 or 6 by 6, the blocked square can be moved or removed, and the student sets how many pieces to make. Some settings have no solution and the tool does not say which. Through doing, the student learns that congruent is a demand you can build to and not only a question you answer, that size comes first and shape second, and that if the squares cannot be shared out evenly then equal pieces are impossible whatever the shape.

(tool id M5.S18.T18.C87.2D1, concept M5.S18.T18.C87, topic: What the Twins Taught Us)
