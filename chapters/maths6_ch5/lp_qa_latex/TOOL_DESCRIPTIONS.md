# maths6_ch5 - tool descriptions (10 tools)

Tool Name: Idli-Vada Caller

Tool Description:

A ring of children numbered 1 to 60 fills the screen, each one a small round face with the number beside it. Above the ring are two steppers, one for the idli number and one for the vada number, each running from 2 to 12, and a Play button. Beside the ring a tally keeps four counts: just idli, just vada, idli-vada and plain numbers. Before Play is pressed every child is plain and every count is zero.

On each card the student first predicts by typing the first number at which the children will say idli-vada. Then the student sets the two steppers and presses Play, and the game is called round the ring one child at a time. A child who must say idli glows orange, a child who must say vada glows green and a child who must say both glows gold, while the tally counts up. For 3 and 5 the gold children are 15, 30, 45 and 60, and the tally ends at 16 just idli, 8 just vada and 4 idli-vada. Tapping a gold child opens the two skip-counting lists side by side, 3, 6, 9 up to 15 and 5, 10, 15, with the child's number in both, so the student sees that a common multiple is simply a number found in both lists.

The six cards are: idli 3 and vada 5, first idli-vada number (15); idli 4 and vada 6 (12); idli 2 and vada 5 up to 60, how many idli-vada calls (6, at 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60); and idli 3 and vada 7 (21). The last two are open challenges. On the fifth the student chooses two numbers so that nobody ever says just vada, for example idli 4 and vada 8, keeping the vada number as the larger as in the game. The just-vada count stays at zero and every green child is also gold. On the sixth the student chooses two numbers below 10 whose first idli-vada call comes after 50, for example 7 and 8, whose first gold child is 56.

A wrong first number is checked against the glowing children one by one, walking along the ring and stopping at the first gold child, so a student who answers 30 for 3 and 5 sees that 15 came earlier. A solved card lists all the gold numbers as multiples of the first one, which answers the idea that only the first one counts. Hints come in order: which numbers does each child call, read the colours; the idli-vada numbers are the numbers that are in both lists; press Play and look at the first gold number; and last the first gold number is shown with the others following it, and a similar card comes next. Through doing, the student learns that a common multiple is a number in both skip-counting lists, that the first one shows where all the others fall, and that nobody says just vada when the larger number is a multiple of the smaller.

(tool id M1.S2.T2.C5.2D1, concept M1.S2.T2.C5, topic: Multiples and Common Multiples)

Tool Name: Jump Jackpot

Tool Description:

A number line from 0 to 72 stretches across the screen with Jumpy standing at 0. A stepper places one treasure marker on the line, and a second stepper can place a second one. A jump size stepper runs from 1 to 36, and a Jump button moves Jumpy forward by the chosen size each time it is pressed. Every spot where Jumpy lands is marked on the line, and a list called Jump sizes that worked sits beside it, empty at the start.

With one treasure on 24 the student chooses a jump size and presses Jump again and again until Jumpy reaches or passes the treasure. Jump size 4 lands on 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 and 24, the treasure opens, and 4 goes into the list. Jump size 5 lands on 20 and then 25, and a small arc over 24 shows Jumpy hopping over the treasure. The student tries more sizes until the list holds 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 24, including 1 and 24 themselves. The solved card pairs them as 1 and 24, 2 and 12, 3 and 8, 4 and 6, and names these numbers the factors of 24, the numbers that divide it exactly.

Then the student sets two treasures and looks for sizes that reach both. On 14 and 36 a jump of 7 lands on 14, then 21, 28, 35 and 42, and the path shows that it jumps over 36. The list soon shows that 1 and 2 reach both, and these are called the common factors. The remaining cards are treasures on 15 and 30 (1, 3, 5, 15), on 28 and 70 (1, 2, 7, 14), on 20 and 28 (1, 2, 4), and on 12 and 18, where the student names the biggest size that reaches both (6). If the student lists a size that does not work for both, the tool replays it and shows the path landing on one treasure and jumping over the other. A size the student leaves out, such as 1 or the treasure number itself, is pointed out on the line with its landing spots.

Hints come in order: where does Jumpy land with this jump size; a jump size works if it divides the treasure number exactly, so check 1 and the number itself too; try each jump size from 1 upward and mark the ones that land on the treasure; and last the working sizes are shown on the line and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that the factors of a number are the jump sizes that land exactly on it, that 1 and the number are always among them, that common factors are the sizes that reach two numbers, and that the landing spots are multiples while the jump size is a factor.

(tool id M1.S5.T5.C34.2D1, concept M1.S5.T5.C34, topic: Factors and Common Factors)

Tool Name: Rectangle Maker

Tool Description:

A tray of loose dot counters sits on the left of the screen, with a stepper that sets how many dots it holds, from 1 to 60, and a Make Rectangles button beneath it. The wide area on the right is empty, ready to hold boxes of figs drawn as rows and columns of dots.

On each card the student first guesses how many different rectangles the dots can make, then sets the number and presses the button. The tool lays out every rectangle, each labelled with its rows and columns. For 12 dots it draws 1 by 12, 2 by 6 and 3 by 4, and a small turn-round mark shows that 6 by 2 is the same box turned on its side. A count underneath lists the factors, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12, and says there are six. For 7 dots only one rectangle appears, 1 by 7, and the only factors are 1 and 7. The student sees that the rows times the columns always give the number, so both are factors. Then the student types the factors or taps a coloured tag: prime, composite or neither.

The six cards are: 12 dots, list all the factors; 7 dots, how many rectangles (one, so 7 is prime); 18 dots, how many factors (six, from 1 by 18, 2 by 9 and 3 by 6); 1 dot, prime, composite or neither (neither: a single box with one factor, while a prime needs exactly two); find an even number below 30 with exactly two factors (only 2, because 4, 6, 8 and the rest each make more than one rectangle); and find all the primes from 21 to 30 (23 and 29, while 25 makes 1 by 25 and 5 by 5 and so has three factors).

A wrong tag shows the count of factors beside the rule, exactly two factors means prime and more than two means composite. A missed factor pair shows as a missing rectangle, and a solved card shows the rectangles and the factor count together. Hints come in order: how many rows and how many columns can the dots make; each pair of rows and columns multiplies to the number, so both are factors; try rows 1, 2, 3 and so on in turn and stop when the columns become smaller than the rows; and last the rectangles are shown with their factors and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that each rectangle is a pair of factors, that a prime makes only one rectangle, that 2 is an even prime, and that 1 is neither prime nor composite.

(tool id M2.S8.T8.C48.2D1, concept M2.S8.T8.C48, topic: Prime and Composite Numbers)

Tool Name: Eratosthenes Sieve

Tool Description:

A 10 by 10 board holding the numbers 1 to 100 fills the screen. Below it are a Next step button and a counter that reads Primes found: 0. All the numbers start plain.

Before each press the student predicts by tapping the number that the sieve will circle next. Then the student presses Next step and watches. The first press crosses out 1, because it is neither prime nor composite. The next press circles 2 in green and flashes each of its multiples, 4, 6, 8 and on, as it turns them grey. The press after that circles 3, the first number still uncrossed, and flashes 6, 9, 12 and the rest. The following presses circle 5 and then 7. The counter rises with each circle, and the student keeps pressing until every number is either circled or crossed out. Circling 11 crosses nothing new, because all its multiples up to 100 are already grey, and the remaining uncrossed numbers are circled one after another.

The student can also tap any number and say prime or composite. A tap on a crossed-out number shows the circled number that removed it: 91 shows that 7 crossed it out as 7 times 13, so a crossed number is a multiple of a smaller circled number and has a third factor. A number left uncrossed has no smaller circled number dividing it, so it has only two factors. Numbers such as 9, 15, 25 and 49 are odd and stay open after the step for 2, yet later steps remove them, so odd does not mean prime. When the board is finished, the long grey stretch from 90 to 96, between the primes 89 and 97, shows how many composite numbers can sit in a row.

The six cards are: after circling 2 and 3, which number is circled next (5, since 4 is already crossed out); how many primes there are from 1 to 30 (10); how many from 1 to 100 (25); the number that 7 crosses out which 2, 3 and 5 did not (49, which is 7 times 7; the same step also removes 77 and 91 for the first time, and the tool shows all three, the card asks for the smallest); all the twin primes up to 30 (3 and 5, 5 and 7, 11 and 13, 17 and 19); and the largest prime below 100 (97). Hints come in order: which number is the first one left uncrossed; the first uncrossed number is prime, so cross out its multiples; circle it, cross out its multiples and look for the next uncrossed number; and last the tool does the next step and shows why the number is prime, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that the sieve is not magic: what survives every crossing has no factor except 1 and itself.

(tool id M2.S11.T11.C59.2D1, concept M2.S11.T11.C59, topic: The Sieve of Eratosthenes)

Tool Name: Thread Art Co-prime Check

Tool Description:

A circle of pegs, like a thread-art board, opens with 12 pegs numbered 0 to 11 round the edge. Two steppers sit beside it, one for the number of pegs from 6 to 24 and one for the thread gap from 1 to 12, with a Draw button and a read-out that shows the pegs reached and a co-prime tick.

On each card the student first predicts whether the thread will touch every peg. Then the student sets the pegs and the gap and presses Draw. The thread is tied to peg 0 and then drawn one segment at a time to every gap-th peg, and each peg it touches turns gold. It stops when it comes back to the start, and the pegs it missed stay grey. With 12 pegs and gap 4 the thread touches only 0, 4 and 8, and the nine grey pegs show that 12 and 4 share the common factor 4, so the tick stays off. With 13 pegs and gap 4 the thread reaches all 13 pegs and the tick comes on, although 4 is not a prime. With 12 pegs and gap 5 every peg is gold. With 10 pegs and gap 4 the thread touches 0, 4, 8, 2 and 6, five pegs, the even ones, because 10 and 4 share the factor 2.

The six cards are: 12 pegs, gap 4, does the thread reach every peg (no, 12 and 4 share 4); 13 pegs, gap 4 (yes, 13 and 4 are co-prime); 12 pegs, gap 5 (every peg); find a gap below 12 for 12 pegs that reaches every peg (5, 7 or 11, and gap 1 works trivially); 10 pegs, gap 4, how many pegs are reached (5); and which of 18 and 35, 15 and 37, 30 and 415 are co-prime (18 and 35, and 15 and 37). The numbers on the last card are too big for the board, so the pegs are replaced by two factor lists that the student fills in for each pair, and 30 and 415 are caught by the common factor 5.

A wrong co-prime answer shows the common factor of the two numbers, and a solved card shows the pegs reached with the tick. Hints come in order: which pegs does the thread touch; the thread reaches every peg when the two numbers have no common factor other than 1; list the factors of both numbers and look for one besides 1; and last the common factor is shown with the pegs it misses, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that two numbers are co-prime when their only common factor is 1, that a thread with gap g on n pegs reaches every peg exactly when n and g are co-prime, and that co-prime numbers need not be prime.

(tool id M2.S13.T13.C81.2D1, concept M2.S13.T13.C81, topic: Co-prime Numbers)

Tool Name: Factor Tree Builder

Tool Description:

The student opens a blank canvas with the number 72 at the top of a tree. A Split button sits below it, and a bar along the bottom reads Primes found, empty at the start. Prime leaves will turn green and composite leaves will stay open, so the student can always see what is still to be broken down.

The student taps the number or any open leaf, presses Split, and types two numbers that multiply to it, for example 12 and 6 for 72. If they do not multiply to the number the split is turned back with the product shown, such as 8 times 6 is 48, not 72. A split into 1 and the number itself is also turned back, since it gets nowhere. Each correct split grows two branches. A leaf that is prime turns green and drops into the bar in increasing order, while a composite leaf stays open and shows its factors waiting, for example 4 shows 2 and 2 still to come. The student splits 12 into 3 and 4, 4 into 2 and 2, and 6 into 2 and 3, and the bar ends as 2, 2, 2, 3, 3. The primes of 12 and of 6 are seen joining in the bar, so the primes of 72 are the primes of its two factors put together. Multiplying the bar back gives 72.

The six cards are: 72, build a tree and write its prime factorisation (2 times 2 times 2 times 3 times 3); 72 again with a different tree, such as 8 times 9 or 2 times 36, and compare the leaves (same primes, so the solved card puts the two trees side by side); 64 (six 2s); 105 (3 times 5 times 7); a number with one 2, two 3s and one 11, where the student multiplies prime tiles to get 198 and checks by splitting it; and three primes below 30 whose product is 1955, where the student starts from 1955, tries primes from the tray, and finds 5, 17 and 23.

Hints come in order: which two numbers multiply to the number on this leaf; split every composite leaf and stop only when every leaf is prime; start with the smallest prime that divides the number; and last the tree is finished and the primes are read out in order, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a number is broken down until only primes are left, that different trees of one number end in the same primes, and that the order of multiplying makes no difference.

(tool id M3.S16.T16.C100.2D1, concept M3.S16.T16.C100, topic: Prime Factorisation)

Tool Name: Factorisation Compare

Tool Description:

Two number boxes sit side by side at the top of the screen. Under each one the prime factorisation appears as a row of coloured tiles, one colour for each prime, in increasing order, with the two rows lined up one above the other. Between them is a shared-primes marker, and below are two verdicts to fill in: co-prime, yes or no, and divisible, yes or no.

The student types two numbers, for example 56 and 63, and predicts both verdicts before touching the tiles. The rows show 56 as 2, 2, 2, 7 and 63 as 3, 3, 7. The student then matches tiles by dragging each tile of the smaller number onto an unused tile of the same colour in the bigger number's row. Tiles of the same colour in both rows light up in the shared marker, so the 7 in both is seen at once and the pair is not co-prime. For divisibility every tile of the smaller number needs its own partner. Tiles that find no match turn red, so the student sees which primes are missing.

The six cards are: 56 and 63, co-prime (no, they share 7); 40 and 231, co-prime (yes, 2 and 5 against 3, 7 and 11); 168 and 12, is 168 divisible by 12 (yes, 2, 2, 3 find partners and the 2 and 7 left over make 14); 75 and 21, is 75 divisible by 21 (no, the 7 of 21 has no partner in 3, 5, 5); 42 and 12, is 42 divisible by 12 (no, 12 needs two 2s and 42 has only one, even though every prime colour of 12 appears in 42); and 30 and 45, where both answers are no, because they share 3 and 5 and the 2 of 30 has no partner in 45.

A wrong verdict shows the shared tiles highlighted, or the red tiles for divisibility, and a solved card shows both verdicts with the tiles. Hints come in order: what colours do the two rows share; no shared prime means co-prime and every prime of the smaller inside the bigger means divisible; count each prime in both rows and compare the counts; and last the tiles are matched and the verdicts read out, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that no common prime means the numbers are co-prime, that one number divides another when its primes, count by count, sit inside the other's, and that a shared prime alone is not enough for division.

(tool id M3.S19.T19.C115.2D1, concept M3.S19.T19.C115, topic: Using Prime Factorisation)

Tool Name: Last Digit Lens

Tool Description:

A large number box sits at the top of the screen, and a round glass lens covers only its last digit while the other digits are dimmed. Below it are three test lights labelled for 10, for 5 and for 2, all off at the start, and a Check by division button.

The student types a number, reads only what the lens shows and switches on the lights they think are right. The lens also names the rule for each light in a word. Pressing Check by division makes the tool divide by 10, 5 and 2, and the remainders appear beside each light so the student sees whether the prediction matched. For 8560 the lens shows 0 and all three lights come on, with remainders 0, 0 and 0. For 125 the lens shows 5, only the light for 5 comes on, and the remainders are 5, 0 and 1. For 682 the lens shows 2, only the light for 2 comes on, and the remainders are 2, 2 and 0. For 173 the lens shows 3, no light comes on, and the remainders are 3, 3 and 1.

The six cards are: 8560, which of 10, 5 and 2 divide it (all three); 125 (5 only); 682 (2 only); 173 (none); find the largest number below 399 divisible by 5, where the student tries 398, 397, 396 and sees the last digit fall to 5 (395); and the remainders of 2345 on dividing by 10, 5 and 2 (5, 0 and 1), which the student reads from the last digit 5 and then confirms with the division.

A wrong prediction shows the remainder from the division. This also corrects the idea that only numbers ending in 5 are multiples of 5, since 8560 and 20 end in 0 and still light the 5 light. A solved card shows the last digit and the three lights. Hints come in order: which digit does the lens show; the last digit alone decides 10, 5 and 2; check whether it is 0, whether it is 0 or 5, and whether it is even; and last the lights are lit and the remainders shown, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a number is divisible by 10 when it ends in 0, by 5 when it ends in 0 or 5, and by 2 when it ends in 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8, without any long division.

(tool id M4.S22.T22.C138.2D1, concept M4.S22.T22.C138, topic: Divisibility by 10, 5 and 2)

Tool Name: Last Two and Three Digits

Tool Description:

A number box holding 8536 sits above a lens window that starts over the last two digits and can be widened to three. Two test lights, for 4 and for 8, are off at the start. Beside the number is a Check by division button and a short list of the multiples of 4 and of 8 that lie near the number inside the lens window.

The student types a number, slides the lens to two digits or three, reads the number in the window and predicts the lights before pressing Check by division. For 8536 the window reads 36, the list shows 32, 36 and 40, and the student sees that 36 is a multiple of 4, so the 4 light comes on. A note shows why the digits outside the lens cannot change the answer: they stand for hundreds, and 100 is a multiple of 4. For 8, the 8 light stays dim while the lens is two digits wide. If the student tries it anyway, the tool shows 108 and 208, both ending in 08, and the list shows that 208 is a multiple of 8 and 108 is not, since 104 and 112 come either side of it. Widening the lens to three digits settles it, because 1000 is a multiple of 8.

The six cards are: 8536, is it divisible by 4 (yes, 36 is); 1732 and 1736, which is divisible by 4 (both, 32 and 36); 8560, change the last two digits to make a multiple of 8 (8552, since 552 is 8 times 69; 8560 itself already works because 560 is 8 times 70, and the card asks for a changed number); 1128, is it divisible by 8 (yes, 128 is); the largest 4-digit palindrome divisible by 4, where the student finds that 9 at both ends gives an odd number and 8998 fails because 98 is not on the list (8888); and 2024 to 2099, how many leap years (19, from 2024 to 2096 in steps of 4).

A wrong prediction shows why, with the digits beyond the lens as multiples of 100 or 1000. A solved card shows the digits used and the light. Hints come in order: how many digits does the lens show; for 4 look at the last two digits and for 8 look at the last three; divide only the number in the lens by 4 or by 8; and last the lens number and the answer are shown, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that the last digit cannot decide 4, since 12 and 22 differ, that the last two digits decide 4, and that the last three digits decide 8.

(tool id M4.S25.T25.C152.2D1, concept M4.S25.T25.C152, topic: Divisibility by 4)

Tool Name: Prime Grid Puzzle

Tool Description:

A 3 by 3 grid of empty cells fills the middle of the screen. The row products are written to the right of the rows and the column products below the columns, and a read-out under each line shows the product of what is placed in it. Under the grid is a prime keypad with 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17 and 19. Tiles from the keypad drop into cells, and a number can also be typed into a cell.

The first four cards open only one line of the grid at a time. On a row of 30 the student breaks the clue into primes and drops 2, 3 and 5 into the three cells in any order, and the read-out turns green at 30. The student can swap the order and see it still gives 30. A row of 27 takes 3, 3 and 3, a row of 8 takes 2, 2 and 2, and a column of 105 takes 3, 5 and 7. A tile that is not prime, such as 15 or 6, is refused and the keypad reminds that only primes are allowed.

On the fifth card the whole grid opens with every clue given, for example row products 105, 20 and 30 and column products 28, 125 and 18. The student writes each clue as primes first: 105 is 3, 5, 7; 20 is 2, 2, 5; 30 is 2, 3, 5; 28 is 2, 2, 7; 125 is 5, 5, 5; 18 is 2, 3, 3. Starting with the clue that has the fewest choices, 125 can only be 5 times 5 times 5, so the middle column is all 5s, and the 7 in 28 can only come from the first row, which fixes the rest. The finished grid reads 7, 5, 3 then 2, 5, 2 then 2, 5, 3. On the sixth card the student fills a second puzzle, with row products 8, 105 and 70 and column products 30, 70 and 28, and checks it by multiplying every row and column by hand and typing the six products before the read-outs are shown.

A product that does not match is shown beside its clue, and a solved puzzle shows every row and column product. Hints come in order: which primes multiply to the clue; break the clue into primes, since each cell holds one prime of its row and column; start with the clue that has the fewest prime factors; and last the row is filled and the column products are checked, and a similar puzzle follows. Through doing, the student learns that every clue breaks into primes in only one way apart from order, and that this single breakdown, checked against rows and columns together, solves the puzzle.

(tool id M4.S32.T32.C183.2D1, concept M4.S32.T32.C183, topic: A Prime Puzzle)
