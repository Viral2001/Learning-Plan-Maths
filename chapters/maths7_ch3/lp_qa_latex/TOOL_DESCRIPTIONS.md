# maths7_ch3 - tool descriptions (9 tools)

Tool Name: Equal Part Scale Splitter

Tool Description:

A ruler marked from 0 to 6 units runs across the screen. Under it sits a unit strip with a Split control that cuts one unit into 2, 4, 5 or 10 equal parts, and an object strip (a bolt, a straw, a ribbon) that the student drags along the ruler. Two answer boxes wait below, one for the length as units and tenths and one for the same length as a count of tenths, and a row of length cards lies at the bottom, ready to be dragged into order.

The first card is a bolt that ends 3 parts after the 4 mark. The student drags it until it rests on the ruler and notes the two whole marks it lies between, 4 and 5. Then the student picks a split. With 4 equal parts the end of the bolt falls between two marks, and that split is shown beside the split into 10 so the sizes of the parts can be compared. With 10 equal parts the end lands exactly on the third mark. The student counts the parts after the lower mark and writes 4 3/10 in the first box and 43/10 in the second. Reading 7 parts after the 2 mark as 7 units is caught, because the strip shows that 7 parts fill only part of one unit and the length is 2 7/10. A tenths count that does not match the mixed form is shown on the strip, ten parts to each unit.

The next cards turn the task around and then compare. A strip that ends 9 parts after the 0 mark is read as 9/10. A ribbon that is 34 tenths long is grouped ten tenths at a time into units, which leaves 3 units and 4 tenths, 3 4/10. Three straws at 2 7/10, 3 2/10 and 2 4/10 are dragged into increasing order, with each length also shown as a count of tenths (27, 32 and 24) so the order can be checked. Then 4/10, 41/10, 4 1/10 and 41 1/10 appear side by side, each read aloud in words, and the student names the two that are equal, 41/10 and 4 1/10. The last card orders 9/10, 1 7/10, 130/10 and 13 1/10. The ruler stops at 6, so the student writes each length as a count of tenths (9, 17, 130 and 131) and arranges the cards by those counts.

Hints come in order. First: which two whole marks is the object between? Then: split the unit into 10 equal parts and count the parts after the lower mark. Then: the length is the lower mark plus that many tenths, and ten tenths make one unit, so also count all the tenths. Last, the ruler shows the length in both forms and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a length between two marks is read as whole units plus tenths or as a count of tenths, and that lengths written in different ways are compared by turning them all into counts of tenths.

Tool Name: Hundredth Cell Zoom Grid

Tool Description:

One unit lies across the screen as a long strip cut into 10 equal tenths, with a second strip beside it for the next unit. A Zoom button opens any chosen tenth into 10 equal hundredths. Under the strips a read-out is ready to light the units, the tenths and the hundredths in three colours and to write the same length as a count of hundredths. Length cards wait at the side to be placed on the strips.

The first card is a wire that ends 4 small parts after the 1 1/10 mark. The student predicts how many hundredths long the wire is, then finds the tenth it ends in, presses Zoom on it and counts the 4 small parts. The read-out lights 1 unit, 1 tenth and 4 hundredths and writes the length three ways: 1 1/10 4/100, 1 14/100 and 114/100. The student sees that 1 1/10 is already 110 hundredths, and 4 more make 114. A hundredth placed in the tenths column is shown as a part that is too big, since ten hundredths make one tenth. Counting all the small parts but forgetting the tenths is corrected by filling the tenths first.

The folded paper card starts with a sheet 8 9/10 long that is folded in half. Its end falls between 4 4/10 and 4 5/10 where there is no mark, so the student zooms into that tenth and places the end on the 5th small part, which gives 4 4/10 5/100. The student also tests whether this can be said as 4 units and 45 hundredths. Then 55/100 is placed as 5 tenths and 5 hundredths, and 202/100 is read as 2 2/100, with nothing in the tenths. For 3/10, 33/100 and 3/100 the student places all three on the strips, turns 3/10 into 30/100, and picks 33/100 as the longest and 3/100 as the shortest. The last card is a ruler reading of 5 37/100, which the student writes as 5 units, 3 tenths and 7 hundredths and as 537/100.

Hints come in order. First: which tenth does the length end in? Then: zoom into that tenth, which is split into 10 equal hundredths. Then: count the whole tenths first, then the hundredths in the tenth you opened. Last, the strip lights the units, tenths and hundredths and gives the count of hundredths, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that 10 hundredths make 1 tenth and 100 hundredths make 1 unit, that one length can be written as units, tenths and hundredths or as a count of hundredths, and that lengths are easiest to compare when all are counts of hundredths.

Tool Name: Two Level Carry and Break Bench

Tool Description:

A workbench shows three trays labelled Units, Tenths and Hundredths. Two amounts are laid out as counters, one above the other, and the matching written column stands beside the bench, growing line by line as the student works. Three buttons run along the bottom: Combine two trays, Carry ten counters up one tray, and Break one counter down into ten in the tray below. When a count is large, as with 15 units, the tray shows it on a number chip instead of crowding the tray with counters.

The first card is 15 3/10 4/100 plus 2 6/10 8/100. The student predicts whether the hundredths together will reach 10, then works from the Hundredths tray. Combining 4 and 8 puts 12 counters in the tray, so ten are carried up to become one tenth, and 2 hundredths stay. The Tenths tray now holds 3, 6 and the carried 1, which is 10, so it is carried up too. The Units tray holds 15, 2 and the carried 1, and the sum is 18 2/100. A tray left holding ten or more counters is shown as unfinished, so a missed carry is caught. A student who writes 4/100 plus 8/100 as 12/200 sees the twelve counters sitting in one tray, and ten of them still make one tenth.

Subtraction runs the other way. In 25 9/10 minus 6 4/10 7/100 the top Hundredths tray is empty, and taking 7 away is blocked until the student breaks one tenth into ten hundredths, which leaves 8 tenths and 10 hundredths. Then hundredths, tenths and units are taken in turn, and the answer is 19 4/10 3/100. In 15 3/10 4/100 minus 2 6/10 8/100 two breaks are needed: a tenth into hundredths (14 hundredths), then a unit into tenths (12 tenths), giving 12 6/10 6/100. After a subtraction the student adds the answer back to the amount taken away to check that the first amount returns. The remaining cards are 9 5/10 7/100 plus 2 1/10 3/100 (11 7/10, where the hundredths come to exactly ten and leave none), 7 7/100 minus 4 4/100 (3 3/100, where no break is needed) and 12 6/10 2/100 minus 9/10 9/100 (11 63/100).

Hints come in order. First: which tray do you work on first? Then: ten counters in a tray make one counter in the next tray up. Then: if a tray cannot give enough, break one counter from the tray above into ten here. Last, the bench makes the carry or break and the written column follows, and a similar card comes next. Through doing, the student learns that sums and differences with tenths and hundredths are worked from the right, that each place is worth ten times the place to its right, and that carrying and breaking follow the same regrouping as whole numbers.

(tool id M1.S8.T8.C40.2D1, concept M1.S8.T8.C40, topic: Adding and Subtracting Hundredths)

Tool Name: Place Chart to Decimal Writer

Tool Description:

A place chart fills the screen with six columns headed Hundreds, Tens, Units, Tenths, Hundredths and Thousandths. Digit cards from 0 to 9 sit in a tray below the chart, and a movable decimal point marker is parked after the Units column. A read-out line under the chart writes the number and shows how it is said aloud. Each card gives a quantity in words, such as 2 ones, 3 tenths and 5 hundredths.

The student reads the quantity and drops each digit card into its column. For 2 ones, 3 tenths and 5 hundredths the cards 2, 3 and 5 go under Units, Tenths and Hundredths, and the point is set after Units. The read-out writes 2.35 and says two point three five. In 4 ones and 6 hundredths the Tenths column is empty, so a 0 has to go there, and the read-out shows 4.06 and says four point zero six. If the zero is left out, the chart reads the number back as 4.6, which is 4 ones and 6 tenths, not 6 hundredths. A point placed at the wrong column is shown by reading the place value of the first digit after it. The card 1 ten and 5 tenths needs a 0 in the Units column to give 10.5, and 1 hundred, 1 one and 1 hundredth needs zeroes in the Tens and Tenths columns to give 101.01.

The fifth card gives counts instead of places. For 234 tenths the last digit goes under Tenths, the 3 under Units and the 2 under Tens, which makes 23.4. For 234 hundredths the whole group moves one column to the right and the number becomes 2.34, so the same digits 2, 3 and 4 take different places depending on what is being counted. A count of tenths dropped as digits in the Units column is flagged by the place headings. The last card gives 2 1/100, 4 1/10 and 7 7/1000, which the student writes as 2.01, 4.1 and 7.007. After each number is written the student chooses the words for it, saying every digit after the point on its own, so 7.007 is seven point zero zero seven and 0.274 is zero point two seven four, never two hundred seventy four.

Hints come in order. First: which columns does the quantity use, and which are empty? Then: every empty column between two digits needs a zero. Then: the decimal point goes right after the Units column. Last, the chart shows the number with the heading of each digit and reads it aloud, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a decimal is the place value chart written without its headings, that the point marks where the units end, and that the digits after the point are read one at a time.

(tool id M2.S14.T14.C60.2D1, concept M2.S14.T14.C60, topic: Decimal Notation: Writing and Reading)

Tool Name: Exchange Rate Step Counter

Tool Description:

Three step ladders of units stand side by side. The length ladder climbs from millimetre to centimetre, metre and kilometre, the weight ladder from milligram to gram and kilogram, and the money ladder from paise to rupee. Every step is labelled with its exchange rate: 10 between millimetre and centimetre, 100 between centimetre and metre, and 1000 between metre and kilometre, between milligram and gram, and between gram and kilogram, and 100 between paise and rupee. In the middle is a number card with a decimal point that slides along its digits.

The student chooses the starting unit and the unit wanted, and reads the exchange rate between them. Before climbing, the student predicts whether the number will grow or shrink. For 5.6 cm in millimetres the student steps down one rung, the rate is 10, and the point slides one place to the right, so the card reads 56 mm. A smaller unit needs more of them. For 36 cm in metres the student steps up one rung, the rate is 100, and the point slides two places to the left, with a zero added in front, giving 0.36 m. Climbing the wrong way shows the quantity getting bigger when it should get smaller. A point moved too few places is caught by reading the answer back in the first unit: if the student writes 3.6 m, converting it back gives 360 cm, not 36 cm. A quantity written with two units at once is split by the ladder into its two parts before either part is changed.

The other cards use the same ladder. 254 g in kilograms has rate 1000, so the point slides three places to the left and the card reads 0.254 kg. 250 paise in rupees has rate 100 and reads 2.50 rupees. 1 kilometre in millimetres takes three rungs down, with rates 1000, 100 and 10, so the point slides 3, 2 and 1 places and the number becomes 1000000 mm. The last card gives 45 paise paid by each of 100000 passengers. The student first finds the total in paise, 4500000, and then climbs the money ladder by 100 to get 45000 rupees. Every conversion ends with a Check that converts the answer back.

Hints come in order. First: is the new unit smaller or bigger than the old one? Then: a smaller unit needs more of them, so the number grows, and a bigger unit needs fewer. Then: count the zeroes in the exchange rate and move the decimal point that many places. Last, the ladder shows the climb and the shifted number, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that changing a unit multiplies or divides by 10, 100 or 1000, so the decimal point moves right or left by as many places as the exchange rate has zeroes.

(tool id M2.S17.T17.C78.2D1, concept M2.S17.T17.C78, topic: Units of Measurement)

Tool Name: Digit by Digit Zoom Locator

Tool Description:

A number line from 0 to 10 stretches across the top of the screen, with room beneath it for the lines the student will open. A decimal card shows the target, for example 4.185, with its digits lit one at a time. Choosing a gap on the current line opens a zoom window that cuts that gap into 10 equal parts and shows the new end marks. A second mode puts a marked point on a given line for the student to read.

To locate 4.185 the student first predicts which two whole numbers it lies between, then zooms into the gap from 4 to 5 for the units digit 4. The tenths digit 1 picks the first part, so the new line runs from 4.1 to 4.2. The hundredths digit 8 opens the gap from 4.18 to 4.19, and the last digit 5 puts the marker on the 5th of the 10 parts, the middle mark. Every earlier line stays in a stack above, so the student sees each line as a magnified piece of the one before it. A digit zoomed into the wrong gap is shown by the end marks of the new line, which do not hold the target. The same is done for 9.876, from 9 to 10, then 9.8 to 9.9, then 9.87 to 9.88, with the marker on the 6th part, and for 0.407, from 0 to 1, then 0.4 to 0.5, then 0.40 to 0.41, with the marker on the 7th part. In 0.407 the hundredths digit 0 means the zoom takes the very first gap.

In the reading mode the student must first find the value of one division. On a line from 5 to 10 cut into 10 equal parts, the line is 5 units long, so each part is half a unit, and a point 5 parts after 5 is 7.5. The student types the value of one division, and the tool checks it by dividing the length of the line by the number of parts, which also flags a line whose parts are not tenths. On the line from 8 to 8.1 each part is 0.01, so a point 5 parts after 8 reads 8.05. The last line starts at 4.3 with a mark every 0.05, and 4.8 is labelled at the tenth mark. A point one mark past 4.8 reads 4.85. A reading that ignores the end marks of the line is checked by counting the parts again.

Hints come in order. First: which two whole numbers is the decimal between? Then: zoom into that gap, which is cut into 10 equal parts, and the next digit tells which part. Then: repeat for the next digit, always opening the part you chose. Last, the zoom stack shows each line in turn and the marker lands, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that every digit of a decimal is one more zoom into one of ten equal parts, so every decimal has its own place on the number line, and that on any line the value of one division comes first: the length of the line divided by the number of parts.

(tool id M3.S20.T20.C94.2D1, concept M3.S20.T20.C94, topic: Locating Decimals on the Number Line)

Tool Name: Place by Place Comparator

Tool Description:

Two number cards sit on a shared place chart whose columns run from Tens down to Thousandths, and a glowing marker waits at the left edge. Below the chart are a verdict box, where the student says which number is greater and names the deciding place, and a closeness meter that switches on when a target number is set.

For 6.456 and 6.465 the student drops both numbers on the chart so that the decimal points line up, predicts which is greater, and then moves the marker from the left. Both have 6 units and 4 tenths, and at the hundredths the digits differ, 5 against 6, so the marker stops there and 6.465 is greater. The student names the hundredths as the deciding place. To show why the later digits cannot change the answer, the chart shades every place after the deciding one and shows that together they add less than one more hundredth. For 1.009 and 1.090 the marker stops at the hundredths, 0 against 9, and for 3.81 and 13.800 it stops in the first column, Tens, because only 13.800 has a tens digit. Comparing the digits after the point as whole numbers is shown on the chart: 0.9 is 9 tenths, while 0.12 is 1 tenth and 2 hundredths. A longer decimal chosen as greater is tested by padding the shorter one with zeroes.

The zero dilemma card sets 0.2, 0.20 and 0.200 on the chart one after another. The marker finds 2 tenths in all three, with the extra zeroes sitting at the right end, so all are equal. The student can also slide a zero in front of the 2 to make 0.02 and sees that this one does change the value, to 2 hundredths. In closeness mode the student sets a target and the meter shows the gap between each decimal and the target. For 0.9, 1.1, 1.01 and 1.11 with the target 1, the first digit is no help, so the student predicts, then reads the gaps 0.1, 0.1, 0.01 and 0.11 and picks 1.01. For 3.56, 3.65 and 3.099 with the target 4, the gaps are 0.44, 0.35 and 0.901, so 3.65 is closest. An answer picked by its first digit is checked on the meter.

Hints come in order. First: are the points lined up, and which digit comes first on the left? Then: compare place by place from the left and stop at the first place where the digits differ. Then: that place decides, and the bigger digit there means the greater number. Last, the marker stops at the deciding column and the verdict is shown, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns to compare decimals from the biggest place and stop at the first difference, that zeroes at the right end change nothing while zeroes before a digit do, and that closeness is the size of the gap.

(tool id M3.S23.T23.C113.2D1, concept M3.S23.T23.C113, topic: Comparing and Closest Decimals)

Tool Name: Point Aligned Addition Column

Tool Description:

A vertical addition frame stands on the screen with a fixed column for the decimal point running down its middle. Place columns from hundreds to thousandths sit on either side of the point, each with a small carry box above it. Digit tiles for two numbers wait at the side to be snapped into the columns, and above the frame an estimate bar runs from the sum of the whole number parts to that sum plus 2.

Each card begins with a prediction. For 2.7 + 3.5 the student writes the whole number parts, 2 and 3, and their total, 5, and the bar marks the range from 5 to 7. Then the two numbers are snapped in with their points in the point column, tenths under tenths. Adding goes from the right: 7 and 5 tenths make 12 tenths, which is 1 unit and 2 tenths, so a 1 is carried into the units box and the sum is 6.2, with the point still in its column. The sum lands inside the bar. For 75.345 + 86.691 the bar runs from 161 to 163, and the student carries out of the hundredths, the tenths, the units and the tens, ending with 162.036.

The frame is strict about alignment. Numbers lined up at their right ends instead of at the point are flagged by the tile columns. In 18 + 8.8 the student places the point of 18 after its units digit, so the tenths of 8.8 sit in an otherwise empty tenths column, and the sum is 26.8. In 0.934 + 0.6 the 6 must sit under the 9 in the tenths column, not under the 4, and the tenths 9 and 6 make 15, which carries a 1 into the units to give 1.534. Here both whole parts are 0, so the bar runs from 0 to 2. A missed carry is shown by a column that holds ten or more. The last cards are 9.01 + 9.10, with a carry only out of the units, giving 18.11, and 6.236 + 0.487, with carries out of the thousandths and the hundredths, giving 6.723. A sum that falls outside the estimate bar is highlighted against it.

Hints come in order. First: are the decimal points one under the other? Then: start at the smallest place on the right and carry any ten to the next place. Then: add tenths to tenths and hundredths to hundredths, and the point stays where it was. Last, the column adds step by step and the carries are drawn, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that decimals are added like whole numbers once the points are in one line, that each ten carries to the place on its left, and that a sum lies from the whole parts total up to that total plus 2, so it can be checked before it is worked out.

(tool id M3.S26.T26.C135.2D1, concept M3.S26.T26.C135, topic: Adding Decimals)

Tool Name: Zero Filled Subtraction Column

Tool Description:

A vertical subtraction frame has a fixed decimal point column down the middle, with the upper number on top and the lower number beneath it. Digit tiles for the two numbers wait at the side, a supply of ghost zero tiles is ready to fill out the shorter number, and thin borrow arrows can be drawn from a place to its neighbour on the left. Above the frame an estimate bar shows the range the difference must lie in, from 1 less to 1 more than the difference of the whole number parts.

The student predicts first. For 3.5 minus 2.7 the whole parts are 3 and 2, their difference is 1, and the bar runs from 0 to 2. Then the points are lined up and the subtraction starts from the right. In the tenths, 5 is smaller than 7, so the student draws a borrow arrow from the units: the 3 becomes 2 and the 5 tenths become 15 tenths. Then 15 minus 7 is 8 and 2 minus 2 is 0, so the difference is 0.8. Taking the smaller digit from the larger in each place gives a wrong answer, and the borrow arrows show where an arrow was missing. For 84.691 minus 77.345 the student draws two borrows, one into the thousandths and one into the units, and gets 7.346, which lies between 6 and 8.

When the lower number is shorter, the student fills it with ghost zero tiles on the right, which is allowed because a zero at the end of a decimal changes nothing. A missing zero tile is shown as a gap in the column. In 17 minus 16.198 the top number becomes 17.000, and the borrow has to pass through the zero tiles, which each end up as 9, to leave 0.802. In 17 minus 0.05 the top becomes 17.00 and the answer is 16.95. In 34.505 minus 18.1 the lower number gets two zero tiles to become 18.100, and a single borrow into the units gives 16.405. In 9.9 minus 9.09 the top becomes 9.90, the whole parts differ by 0, and the bar runs from minus 1 to 1, so the answer 0.81 fits inside it. A difference outside the bar is highlighted against it.

Hints come in order. First: are the points in one line, and does the lower number have as many places as the upper? Then: fill the short number with zeroes, then start from the right. Then: if a digit is too small, borrow one from the left, and it becomes ten in this place. Last, the column shows each borrow and the digits subtract, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that decimals are subtracted like whole numbers once the points are in one line and the shorter number is filled with zeroes, and that the estimate from the whole parts catches a slip before it is accepted.

(tool id M3.S30.T30.C146.2D1, concept M3.S30.T30.C146, topic: Subtracting Decimals)
