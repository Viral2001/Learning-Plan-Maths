# maths7_ch5 - tool descriptions (7 tools)

Tool Name: Crossing Angle Lab

Tool Description:

Two long lines cross at a point in the middle of the screen, and the four angles at the crossing are named a, b, c and d going round the point. One of the lines can be turned about the crossing with a handle. Four answer boxes sit beside the angles, one for each, with a Check button below them. A protractor overlay can be switched on to show a measured reading for any angle.

Each card gives one angle or a clue, for example a is 120 degrees. The student does not measure. They work out the other three angles by reasoning and type all four values before pressing Check. Neighbours on a straight line add to 180 degrees, so each neighbour is 180 minus the given angle, and the angle that faces the given one is a copy of it. Check then reveals the true value of every box and colours each one right or wrong. Afterwards the student turns the handle and watches the angles move: a and c grow and shrink together, b and d do the same, and a and b always fill a straight line between them.

A pair of neighbours that do not add to 180 is shown side by side on a straight line with the gap highlighted. Two facing angles typed as different values are shown with one copied onto the other, so the student sees that they match. When the protractor shows a reading a degree off, for instance 119 where reasoning gives 120, a note says the reasoning gives 180 and the pencil line has thickness, so the reasoned value is the one to type.

The seven cards are: a is 120 (b, c, d are 60, 120, 60); a is 35 (145, 35, 145); b is 100 (a, c, d are 80, 80, 100); c is 72 (a, b, d are 72, 108, 108); a is twice b (120, 60, 120, 60); a is 20 degrees more than b (100, 80, 100, 80); and d is 90, where the student finds the other three, all 90, and names the lines perpendicular. In the two clue cards the clue and the straight-line sum of 180 are used together. Hints come in order: which angle is given and which sit next to it on a straight line; neighbours add to 180 and opposite angles are equal; subtract the given angle from 180 for each neighbour and copy it to the opposite angle; last, the four angles are shown on the lines with their sums marked, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that one angle at a crossing fixes the other three, and that reasoning gives the exact value where a pencil and protractor can only come close.

(tool id M1.S2.T2.C6.2D1, concept M1.S2.T2.C6, topic: Intersecting Lines and Linear Pairs)

Tool Name: Dot Paper Line Studio

Tool Description:

A square grid of dots, up to 13 dots by 13 dots, fills the screen. The student joins any two dots to draw a segment, and a readout beside the grid always shows the steps across and the steps up or down of the last segment drawn. A mode switch above the grid sets the task: draw a parallel, draw a perpendicular, or decide yes or no for a pair of segments. The segment given on the card is drawn in colour.

The student first reads the steps of the given segment from the readout. To draw a parallel they start at any other dot and take the same steps across and up. To draw a perpendicular they swap the steps and change one sign, so a segment of 1 across and 3 up gives a segment of 3 across and 1 down. After Check, a correct pair is marked automatically, with arrow marks for a parallel pair and a small square between the lines for a perpendicular pair. The student can also drag an end dot of the new segment and watch the readout change, and the marks appear and vanish as the steps stop matching.

A segment that does not repeat the steps is shown with the given segment on a shared grid, the steps written on each, and the mismatch circled. A perpendicular that has the right direction but is longer or shorter than the given segment is accepted, with a note that length does not matter. A drawn pair that looks parallel but whose steps differ is extended until the two lines cross.

The seven tasks are: a flat segment, where the perpendicular is an upright segment; 2 across and 2 up, where the perpendicular is 2 across and 2 down; 1 across and 3 up, where the parallel is 1 across and 3 up from another dot; and 2 across and 3 up, where the perpendicular is 3 across and 2 down and the student says the grid must be at least 4 dots across to hold it. Then come two yes-or-no tests: 3 across and 3 up against 1 across and 1 down are not parallel because they lean opposite ways, while 5 across and 2 up against 10 across and 4 up are parallel, since 5 times 4 and 2 times 10 are both 20. The last task is to draw lines parallel to a 4 by 4 diagonal, a steep 1 by 6 segment and a shallow 5 by 2 segment. Hints ask for the steps of the given segment, say that a parallel keeps the steps and a perpendicular swaps them and changes one sign, and suggest starting at any dot and taking the same steps; last, the new segment is drawn with the steps marked on both, and a similar task follows. The student learns to read a line as steps across and up, and to spot parallel and perpendicular lines by their steps instead of by eye.

Tool Name: Eight Angle Transversal Board

Tool Description:

Two lines, l and m, and a third line t that crosses both are drawn on the board. The student can drag t and turn it. The eight angles are numbered 1 to 8, with 1 to 4 at the crossing with l and 5 to 8 at the crossing with m, and angles 1 and 5 sit in the same top left corner. Tapping an angle highlights its vertically opposite angle in one colour and its corresponding angle in another. A counter shows how many different angle measures the figure has, and a switch makes l and m parallel.

The student plays with the board before answering anything. They tap angles at both crossings and see that every angle has one vertically opposite partner and one partner in the same corner at the other crossing. Then they turn t with l and m not parallel and watch the counter. Four pairs of vertically opposite angles are always equal, so the counter never goes above 4. With the switch set to parallel, the counter drops to 2, and when t is also made perpendicular to the lines it drops to 1 because all eight angles are 90 degrees.

The seven cards ask the student to name positions before pressing Check. Four cards ask for the corresponding angle of angle 1 (angle 5), angle 4 (angle 8), angle 7 (angle 3) and angle 6 (angle 2). Three ask how many different measures there can be: at most 4 with lines that are not parallel, 2 with parallel lines, and 1, all 90 degrees, with parallel lines and a perpendicular transversal. A wrong corresponding angle is shown with its two arms against the lines, so the student sees it is at the other crossing but in the opposite corner. A wrong count shows the angles coloured by equal size. A claim that corresponding angles are always equal is shown on lines that are not parallel, with the two angles measured and unequal.

Hints go in order: at which crossing the given angle sits and in which corner; the corresponding angle is in the same corner at the other crossing; go to the other crossing and pick the same corner, top left, top right, bottom left or bottom right; last, the two angles light up in one colour at the same corner, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns where the corresponding angle sits, that a transversal makes eight angles with at most four different measures, and that corresponding angles are not equal unless the lines are parallel.

(tool id M3.S12.T12.C47.2D1, concept M3.S12.T12.C47, topic: A Line Across Two Lines)

Tool Name: Corresponding Angle Parallel Test

Tool Description:

A line l and a transversal t cross at a point X. A point Y is marked on t below X, and a second line m passes through Y and can be turned about Y with a handle. Two readouts show the corresponding angles, one at X and one at Y, taken in the same corner. A lamp beside them turns green when the two numbers are equal, and the two lines then show arrow marks. A tracing-paper overlay can be switched on: it lifts a copy of the angle at X and slides it down t to sit at Y.

In the first task the student sets the angle at X to 60 degrees and turns m until the angle at Y reads 60 as well. The tracing copy then fits the angle at Y exactly, the lamp turns green and the arrow marks appear. The other angle at each crossing is 120. Pressing Check extends both lines, and they never meet. The student then moves to testing pairs of lines: they read both corresponding angles and decide whether the lines are parallel. A pair with 75 and 75 is parallel. A pair with 64 and 66 looks parallel to the eye but is not, because the angles differ by 2, and Check extends the lines until they meet and marks the meeting point. A pair with 110 and 70 is not parallel either, because the numbers are not equal.

In the fifth card two lines lean 10 degrees apart. The student tries three different transversals one after another and reads the corresponding angles each time. They differ by 10 every time and are never equal, so equal corresponding angles cannot be found on lines that are not parallel. The last card gives parallel lines with one angle of 65 degrees at l, and the student finds the other seven angles, each either 65 or 115.

Equal angles that are not corresponding, for example vertically opposite ones, are flagged as the wrong pair. A match found at one transversal is repeated at a second transversal to confirm it. Hints ask which two angles are corresponding and whether they are in the same corner; say that equal corresponding angles make the lines parallel and unequal ones do not; ask the student to read both angles and compare the numbers; last, the lines are extended to show that parallel lines never meet and the others do, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that equal corresponding angles are a test for parallel lines and a way to draw them, and that parallel lines always give equal corresponding angles.

(tool id M3.S14.T14.C53.2D1, concept M3.S14.T14.C53, topic: Corresponding Angles and Parallel Lines)

Tool Name: Set Square Slide Constructor

Tool Description:

The student draws with a ruler and a set square on a sheet of paper. A line l runs across the paper with a ruler lying along it. A set square rests against the ruler, and the student chooses which one to use (the right angle one, the 45 degree one, or the 30 and 60 degree one) and which edge to draw along. The set square slides along the ruler without turning. A point A can be placed on the paper, and a readout shows the angle each drawn line makes with l.

The student slides the set square to one place and draws along the chosen edge, then slides to a second place, and sometimes a third, and draws again. Each drawn line carries its angle with l. After each construction the student decides whether the lines are parallel and gives the reason by tapping the two equal corresponding angles on the figure, with l acting as the transversal. A correct construction is confirmed with those angles marked in one colour.

The six constructions are these. Two lines along the right angle edge, both at 90 degrees to l. Two lines along the long side of the 45 degree set square, 45 degrees with l each time. Two lines along the long side of the 30 and 60 degree set square, the same angle with l each time. A line through the point A parallel to l: here the student lays an edge of the set square on l, sets the ruler against another edge, and slides the set square along the ruler until that edge reaches A, then draws. Two lines drawn after the set square is turned between draws, 45 degrees and then 60 degrees, which are not parallel. And a third parallel line, checked to have the same corresponding angle as the other two.

A line drawn after the set square was turned shows the two angles, 45 and 60, side by side, with both lines extended until they meet. A line that passes near A but not through it is shown with the gap enlarged. Hints ask which edge rests on the ruler and which edge will be drawn along; say that sliding without turning keeps the angle with the ruler, so the corresponding angles are equal; suggest keeping one edge on the ruler, sliding to the new place and drawing along the other edge; last, the set square is slid for the student and both lines are drawn with their angle, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that sliding keeps the angle fixed, so the lines make equal corresponding angles and are parallel, and that turning the set square spoils it.

(tool id M4.S16.T16.C58.2D1, concept M4.S16.T16.C58, topic: Drawing Parallel Lines)

Tool Name: Alternate Angle Chaser

Tool Description:

Two parallel lines, marked with arrow marks, are cut by a transversal, and the angles are named a to h. At the upper crossing a and b sit at the top with d and c below them, and at the lower crossing e and f sit at the top with h and g below them. The student taps an angle and sets its value. Two buttons then move the selection: one to the corresponding angle and one to the vertically opposite angle. A readout keeps the value shown through each move, and a switch un-parallels the lines.

The student chases an angle in two moves. With f set to 120 degrees, they press the corresponding button and the selection jumps to b, which still reads 120. They press the vertically opposite button and it lands on d, which reads 120 again. Then the student says in a sentence why the value did not change: each move keeps the size, so the alternate angle d equals f. When the switch un-parallels the lines, the chain breaks at the corresponding step, and the two corresponding angles are shown with different values.

The seven cards are: f is 120, chase to the alternate angle d (120); e is 65, chase to c (65); f is 70, find d (70); c is 100, find the alternate angle e (100). The fifth card gives angle 6 as 135, which is f in the lettered figure, and asks for all eight angles using only corresponding, vertically opposite and linear pair facts: four are 135 and four are 45. The last two cards are tests. With a at 120 and f at 70, the student finds b is 60 and sees it is not equal to f, so the lines are not parallel. With alternate angles of 80 and 100 the student decides the lines are not parallel, because alternate angles of parallel lines are equal.

A chain that skips a move shows the missing angle highlighted between the start and the end. A claim that alternate angles add to 180 is answered by showing two alternate angles on parallel lines measured as equal. Hints ask which angle the student starts from and which one must be reached; say to go first to the corresponding angle and then to the vertically opposite angle; say to copy the value across, since each partner has the same size; last, the chain is shown with both moves and the value on each angle, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that alternate angles of parallel lines are equal and not supplementary, and that if the chain ends on a different value the lines are not parallel.

(tool id M5.S19.T19.C70.2D1, concept M5.S19.T19.C70, topic: Alternate Angles)

Tool Name: Same Side Interior Angle Lab

Tool Description:

Two parallel lines and a transversal are drawn with the angles numbered 1 to 8, the four at the upper crossing first. The two interior angles on the right side, 3 and 6, are shaded in one colour, and the two on the left side, 4 and 5, in another. A slider turns the transversal. Under the figure a sum bar lays the two shaded angles end to end against a straight line marked 180 degrees. A four-sided figure mode shows a shape whose opposite sides are parallel.

The student sets one interior angle with the slider, for example angle 3 at 50 degrees, and predicts its same-side partner before looking at the bar. They type angle 6 and then check the bar: the two shaded angles laid end to end fill the straight line exactly. They move the slider and see one shaded angle grow while the other shrinks by the same amount, so the bar always fills the line. In the four-sided mode the student takes one angle of the figure, such as 60 degrees, treats the side between two corners as a transversal of the other two sides, and finds each neighbouring angle from it.

The seven cards are: angle 3 is 50, find angle 6 (130); angle 3 is 72, find angle 6 (108); angle 4 is 110, find its same-side partner angle 5 (70); angle 3 is 50, find angle 5 and angle 6 (50 and 130); a four-sided figure with two pairs of parallel sides and one angle of 60, find its two neighbours (120 and 120); angle 3 is 70 and angle 6 is 100 on lines drawn without arrow marks, are they parallel (no, the sum is 170, not 180); and parallel lines with angle 3 equal to 90, find all the angles (90 each). The student names the same-side pair and checks the sum before moving on.

A sum that is not 180 shows a gap on the straight line and the lines extended until they meet. A claim that interior angles on the same side are equal is answered with angles of 50 and 130 side by side. A correct answer shows the linear pair and the corresponding angle that explain it: angle 3 and angle 2 are a linear pair, and angle 2 equals angle 6. Hints ask which two angles are on the same side of the transversal and between the lines; say that they add to 180 degrees because one is a linear pair of the other's corresponding angle; say to subtract the given angle from 180; last, the two angles are placed end to end on a straight line, and a similar card follows. Through doing, the student learns that interior angles on the same side add to 180 degrees, why they do, and how to use the rule in a figure and as a test for parallel lines.

(tool id M5.S22.T22.C79.2D1, concept M5.S22.T22.C79, topic: Interior Angles on the Same Side)
