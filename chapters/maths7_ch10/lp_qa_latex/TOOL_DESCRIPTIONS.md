# maths7_ch10 - tool descriptions (13 tools)

Tool Name: Sum and Difference Finder

Tool Description:

A target card at the top of the screen reads Sum 25, Difference 11, and the student is asked to type a guess for the two numbers before touching anything else. Below it is a number line from -15 to 30 with two draggable markers, the first number in teal and the second in amber. Beside the line are live read-outs for the sum and for the difference (first number minus second number), an empty trial table, a Shift button and a Swap button.

After typing the guess the student drags the markers and watches both read-outs move. Every pair tried is logged as a new row of the table, so the student sees 10 and 15 give sum 25 but difference -5, 20 and 5 give 15, and 19 and 6 give 13. A pair with the right sum but the wrong difference shows how far off the difference is and in which direction, which tells the student which way to move. Pressing Shift moves 1 from the second number to the first: the sum stays 25 and the difference rises by exactly 2, and the table shows the steps of 2. Swap exchanges the two markers; the sum does not change but the read-out for the difference changes sign, so the student watches 18 and 7 (difference 11) become 7 and 18 (difference -11). A solved card turns both markers green and writes the two checks, first plus second and first minus second.

Six cards follow: sum 25 with difference 11 (18 and 7); sum 25 with difference -11 (7 and 18); sum 4 with difference 12 (8 and -4); sum 0 with difference -10 (-5 and 5); sum -7 with difference -1 (-4 and -3); and sum -7 with difference -13 (-10 and 3). Each card needs a typed guess before the markers are touched. Hints come in order: which of the two numbers must be larger for this difference; keep the sum right and shift one unit at a time while watching the difference; try the first number as (sum + difference) divided by 2 and check it; and last the pair is shown on the line with both checks written out and a new card follows. In partner mode the student hides a pair of their own and shows only its sum and difference for a partner to find.

Through doing, the student learns that a difference means first minus second so it can be negative, that moving one unit across while the sum stays fixed changes the difference by 2, that swapping the pair keeps the sum and turns the difference into its additive inverse, and that sum plus difference is twice the first number.

(tool id M1.S2.T2.C3.2D1, concept M1.S2.T2.C3, topic: Rakesh's Number Game)

Tool Name: Carrom Strike Line

Tool Description:

The student looks down at a carrom strip drawn as a number line from -12 to 12, with one coin resting at 0. Two arrow handles, a and b, can be dragged left or right to set the two strikes. As an arrow is dragged its length and direction show as a signed number, so a pull to the left reads as a negative number. A sentence strip below writes a + b = P with the numbers filled in, and a Strike button waits to play the shot.

Before every strike the student taps where the coin will stop, and a ghost coin is placed there. Pressing Strike makes the coin jump a, then b, each jump starting where the last one ended. The real coin settles beside the ghost coin, and if the prediction was far off the gap is kept on screen and written as a number. A strike set in the other direction is still played out, so the student sees where that coin would have gone. The first cards use strikes of 4 and 3, which end at 7, and strikes of 5 and -7, where the second jump crosses 0 and the coin stops at -2, two units to the left. A missing-strike card gives the first movement -4 and the final position 5, and the student drags b until the coin lands on the target, finding a jump of 9 to the right. A long run of movements 1, -2, 3, -4 and so on up to -10 is played one after another and ends at -5.

The last two cards come from three pictures in which a goes one way and b comes back. In the first the coin ends left of 0, in the second right of 0, and in the third back at 0. After each picture the student chooses in words which jump is longer, or whether they are equal, and then the tool lays the two jumps side by side so the lengths can be compared. Hints move from asking which way a negative strike moves the coin, to adding the movements with their signs, to counting the jump from the first stop to the target for a missing strike, and last the two jumps are drawn with their numbers and the sum written out before a new card follows.

Through doing, the student learns that a movement is a signed number carrying both a distance and a direction, that two strikes always end at P = a + b whichever way they point, that a missing movement is the jump from where the coin is to where it must be, and that the longer jump decides which side of 0 the coin stops on.

(tool id M1.S5.T5.C12.2D1, concept M1.S5.T5.C12, topic: Carrom Coin Integers)

Tool Name: Zero Pair Tray

Tool Description:

The student is given a tray of green tokens (+1) and red tokens (-1), with a Zero pair button, a remove zone beside the tray and a number sentence underneath. A second tray stands to the side, ready to show the adding-the-inverse version of the same question. For each card the student first predicts the answer and its sign, for example for (+7) minus (+18), before moving any token.

The student builds the starting tokens, 7 greens, and then drags tokens towards the remove zone. Trying to take out 18 greens when only 7 are there is allowed: the tray shakes gently and offers zero pairs. The student presses Zero pair as many times as they think are needed. Every pair glows as it forms, and a counter beside the tray shows that the value has not changed. With 11 pairs in, 18 greens can be removed and 11 reds are left, so the sentence reads -11. Extra pairs are harmless, because they stay in the tray still worth 0 and the answer is the same. On the paired cards the student builds the same question in the second tray by adding the inverse, and when both trays are finished they are shown side by side with their sentences.

Six cards are used. First, (+7) minus (+18): how many zero pairs are needed, giving -11. Second, 7 minus 18 beside 7 + (-18), both -11. Third, 4 minus (-12) beside 4 + 12, both 16; the tray holds only greens, so 12 zero pairs go in to give reds that can be removed. Fourth, the inverse of -18, which is 18. Fifth, (-3) minus 5, giving -8. Last, a tray for 47 - 56 + 14 - 8 + 2 - 8 + 5 shows 4 reds, and the student flips every token and reads the new total, 4, without adding the long sum again.

Hints move from asking what must be taken away and whether the tray has enough of it, to putting in zero pairs until the removal is possible, to needing as many pairs as the tokens the tray is short of, and last the pairs are placed and the removal shown step by step before a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a zero pair is worth nothing, so it can be put in to take away tokens that are not there, that subtracting an integer gives the same result as adding its additive inverse, and that flipping every sign flips the total.

(tool id M1.S8.T8.C23.2D1, concept M1.S8.T8.C23, topic: Tokens: Subtracting by Adding Zero Pairs)

Tool Name: Token Bag Multiplier

Tool Description:

An empty bag sits in the middle of the screen with the sentence multiplier times multiplicand equals product waiting beneath it. On one side is a multiplier dial from -9 to 9, and on the other a multiplicand group builder where the student assembles a set of green and red tokens. Three buttons, Put in, Take out and Zero pair, act on the bag. Before any token moves, the student predicts the sign of the product and then its value.

For 4 times (-2) the student sets the dial to 4, builds a group of 2 reds and presses Put in once for each unit of the multiplier. The bag fills with 8 reds and the sentence reads -8. With a negative dial the move changes. For (-4) times 2 the student must press Take out, but the empty bag is short of tokens: it shows this and offers zero pairs. The student puts in 2 zero pairs, takes out the 2 greens, and repeats this 4 times, and 8 reds are left. Zero pairs glow when they cancel, so the student sees why only the unpaired tokens count. The student also tries putting in 2 reds four times and finds the same 8 reds. For (-4) times (-2) the group is 2 reds, taken out 4 times after zero pairs go in, and 8 greens remain; this settles the guess made at the start of the chapter. A prediction with the other sign stays on screen next to the bag's answer, and the tool asks what the multiplier told the bag to do.

Seven cards follow: 4 times (-2); (-4) times 2 with zero pairs first; (-4) times (-2); 3 times (-2) and (-5) times (-2), giving -6 and 10; (-4) times (-1) and (-7) times 3, giving 4 and -21. Then the student puts in each of three sets for -2 four times, 2 reds, 4 reds with 2 greens, and 6 reds with 4 greens, and every bag holds -8 because the extra greens and reds cancel. On the last card, given 123 times 456 = 56088, the student chooses the signs of (-123) times 456, (-123) times (-456) and 123 times (-456).

Hints move from asking whether the multiplier is positive or negative and what that tells the bag to do, to taking the multiplicand out after zero pairs for a negative multiplier, to taking the group out as many times as the multiplier's magnitude and counting what is left, and last the bag replays the moves one by one with the sentence filled in. Through doing, the student learns that the multiplier's sign says put in or take out, the multiplicand says which group, and what is left after zero pairs cancel is the product, so changing one sign changes the product's sign and changing both changes it back.

(tool id M2.S10.T10.C33.2D1, concept M2.S10.T10.C33, topic: The Token Bag: Multiplying Integers)

Tool Name: Sign Pattern Ladder

Tool Description:

The student climbs down a vertical ladder whose rungs are the multipliers 4, 3, 2, 1, 0, -1, -2, -3 and -4, with the multiplicand fixed on the right, first as 3. The product column fills in rung by rung, and a jump arrow between rungs shows the change at each step. Beside the ladder is a 7 by 7 sign grid whose rows and columns both run from -3 to 3, and it fills in as rungs are finished.

At each rung the student predicts the next product before it is revealed, then types it. Going down from 4 times 3 gives 12, 9, 6, 3, 0, with the arrow showing a drop of 3 each time. At 0 times 3 the student must say what comes next if the pattern keeps going, and types -3, -6 and -9 for the rungs below zero. A product that breaks the pattern is kept on its rung and the jump arrow shows it does not match the others, so the student can correct it. Then the student switches the multiplicand from 3 to -3 and climbs down again: -12, -9, -6, -3, 0, and then 3, 6 and 9. This time the arrow points up, because every step down adds 3, and the products climb past 0 into the positive numbers, so (-3) times (-3) comes out as 9.

With the ladders done, the student colours the four quarters of the sign grid positive or negative. If a quarter is coloured the other way, the tool tests it with one product from that quarter before the student decides, and the middle row and column, all zeros, are left out. The coloured grid is then used for the cards: 4 times (-3), (-6) times (-3), (-5) times (-1), (-8) times 4, (-9) times 10 and 10 times (-17); the integer whose product with -1 is 27, -31, -1, 1 and 0; and the six sums, differences and products of 348 and 1064, which the student places on a number line in increasing order. Any order is accepted, and the tool then shows each value on the number line.

Hints move from asking what happens to the product each time the multiplier goes down by 1, to keeping the same step going past zero, to the next product being the last one minus the multiplicand, and last the rung is filled with the step shown. Through doing, the student learns that the pattern does not stop at zero, which fixes the sign rules: same signs give a positive product and different signs a negative one. The student also sees that multiplying by -1 gives the additive inverse and that swapping the factors changes nothing.

(tool id M2.S13.T13.C48.2D1, concept M2.S13.T13.C48, topic: Patterns and Sign Rules)

Tool Name: Mine Shaft Elevator

Tool Description:

The student stands at the top of a mine shaft drawn as a vertical number line from +180 m down to -180 m, with the elevator car waiting at its start. Two controls set the run: a signed rate in metres per minute and a count of minutes. A sentence strip, start + count times rate = end, fills in as the controls change. Context cards can swap the shaft for a thermometer, a marks board or a cement ledger with the same controls. Before each run the student places a prediction marker for the end value.

On the first card the car starts at 0, the rate is -3 and the count is 60. The student runs it and the car descends step by step to -180. A rate set with the other sign is run anyway, so the car climbs and the student sees what the missing sign did, with the prediction marker left beside the real stop. After each run the tool writes the expression and its value one step per line, and the student writes the matching expression themselves: 0 + 60 times (-3) = -180. The second card starts at 15 m and runs 45 minutes, giving 15 + 45 times (-3) = -120, and the student sees that subtracting 135 from 15 gives the same position.

The car then gives way to the other contexts. A room at 32 degrees C cools 5 degrees an hour for 10 hours and reaches -18 degrees C. A drop from 8 degrees C for 4 hours is written as 8 + 4 times (-5), which is -12 degrees C. On the cement ledger 3000 bags earn +8 each and 5000 bags earn -5 each, a total of -1000, a loss of 1000 rupees, and the student then works back to the 4000 white bags that balance 6400 grey bags. On the marks board, with +4 for a right answer and -2 for a wrong one, 15 correct answers with a total of 40 need 10 wrong answers, and 5 correct with a total of -10 need 15 wrong ones. The last card is Mala: 30 answers at +5 and 20 at -2 give 110, and the student finds the largest and smallest possible totals, 250 and -100.

Hints move from asking which way the change goes, up or down, profit or loss, to giving the rate a sign and multiplying it by the count, to end = start + count times rate with the numbers put in, and last the run is replayed step by step with the expression filled in. Through doing, the student learns to choose a sign for each direction or kind, to multiply a signed rate by a count to get the total change, and to add it to the start.

(tool id M3.S16.T16.C57.2D1, concept M3.S16.T16.C57, topic: Marks, Lifts and Temperatures)

Tool Name: Circle and Strike Grid

Tool Description:

A 4 by 4 grid of integers fills the screen: 8, -4, 12, -6 in the first row, then -28, 14, -42, 21, then 12, -6, 18, -9, and 20, -10, 30, -15 in the last. A product strip runs along the bottom and a builder button sits at the side. Before playing, the student predicts whether circling different numbers will change the final product.

In the first game the student taps -6. It is circled, and its row and column are struck out. Next the student taps 14, then 20, and only 18 is left to circle in round four. The product strip multiplies the circled numbers as they are chosen, and the student counts the negative factors: only -6 is negative, so the product is negative, and the magnitudes give -30240. Tapping a struck cell is allowed, and the cell lights its row or column to show why it is out. The student then plays three different choices of their own, and each new product is written beside the earlier ones, so the student sees that every game ends at -30240.

To find out why, the student reveals the row numbers 2, -7, 3, 5 and the column numbers 4, -2, 6, -3 around the grid and checks three cells; for example -42 is (-7) times 6 and 30 is 5 times 6. Every cell is a row number times a column number, and a legal choice takes each row number once and each column number once, so it always multiplies the same eight numbers. In builder mode the student sets four row numbers and four column numbers, for example 1, -2, 3, -1 and 2, 5, -1, 4, predicts the product, and plays the new grid to check that it is always -240. A cell typed by hand that is not row number times column number is shown changing the answer in the product strip. The last task is to build a grid whose product is positive and to explain its sign by counting the negative numbers among the eight.

Hints move from asking what stays the same about every allowed choice, to looking for a number that divides every cell in a row, to row 1 being 2 times 4, -2, 6 and -3, and last the row and column numbers are shown around the grid with each cell's product. Through doing, the student learns that the magic is in the way the grid is built, and that the sign of a product of several integers comes from counting its negative factors.

(tool id M3.S20.T20.C72.2D1, concept M3.S20.T20.C72, topic: A Magic Grid of Integers)

Tool Name: Missing Factor Finder

Tool Description:

Every card in this tool shows a multiplication sentence with a hole in it, such as 25 times ? = -100, above a sign switch and a magnitude keypad for the missing factor. A token bag waits below to replay the sentence, and a deck of the chapter's blanks and divisions is stacked at the side. For every card the student chooses the sign of the missing factor first and only then types its magnitude.

A division card must first be rewritten as a missing-factor sentence, so (-100) divided by 25 becomes 25 times ? = -100. The student sees that 25 is positive and -100 is negative, so the missing factor must be negative, sets the minus sign and types 4. Pressing Check replays the product in the bag, 25 groups of the chosen factor, and the sentence closes at -100. A factor with the other sign is replayed too, so the student sees the product come out with the wrong sign, and a magnitude that does not fit shows the product it would give beside the target. A division that is not exact leaves tokens over in the bag, which is how the student learns that no whole-number factor exists.

Six cards are used. The first has (-100) divided by 25, (-100) divided by (-4) and 50 divided by (-25), giving -4, 25 and -2. The second mixes 14 times (-15), (-16) times (-5), 36 divided by (-18) and (-46) divided by (-23), giving -210, 80, -2 and 2. The third has the blanks (-3) times ? = 27, 5 times ? = -35, ? times (-8) = -56 and ? times (-12) = 132, giving -9, -7, 7 and -11. The fourth has the blanks ? divided by (-8) = 7 and ? divided by 12 = -11, where the student finds the dividend as -56 and -132 by multiplying. The fifth has (-27) divided by 9, 84 divided by (-4) and (-56) divided by (-2), giving -3, -21 and 28. The last has four bracketed divisions, where the brackets are worked out first and the student spots the two that do not give integers, the other two being 16 and -7.

Hints move from asking what b must be multiplied by to give a, to deciding the sign first by asking whether the signs of b and a agree, to dividing the magnitudes, and last the sentence is completed and replayed in the bag. Through doing, the student learns that a divided by b is the missing factor in b times ? = a, so the multiplication sign rules decide every quotient: same signs give a positive quotient and different signs a negative one.

(tool id M4.S22.T22.C79.2D1, concept M4.S22.T22.C79, topic: Division Undoes Multiplication)

Tool Name: Sign Counter Chain

Tool Description:

A chain of factor tiles runs across the screen, starting with 5, -3 and 4. A negative counter above the chain counts how many tiles are negative, and under it sit a magnitude strip and a sign lamp. Tiles can be dragged to reorder them, and tapping between two tiles puts a bracket round them, with a running product shown under every bracket. The student first predicts the sign of the whole product by counting the negative tiles.

With 5, -3 and 4 the student brackets the first two tiles to get -15 and then times 4 gives -60. Regrouping the last two gives -12 and 5 times -12 is -60. Dragging the 5 and the 4 together gives 20, and 20 times -3 is again -60. Any grouping is allowed, and because the product under every bracket stays on screen the student sees that it never changes. On the next card, 25, -6 and 12, the student drags the tiles so that 25 and 12 meet to make 300 and finishes at -1800 in an easy order. A sign predicted the other way leaves the lamp and the counter side by side for the student to compare.

Next the student adds tiles of -1 one at a time and watches the sign lamp flip: 2, 3, 4 and 5 tiles of -1 give 1, -1, 1 and -1. Two longer chains follow, (-7) times 4 times (-1), which is 28, and (-2) times (-1) times (-5) times (-3), which is 30. For these the student counts the negatives, pairs them off, and multiplies the magnitudes separately. On the last card the student finds three consecutive integer tiles whose product is -6 and then 120, and a choice that misses shows its product on the number line next to the target. The answers are -3, -2, -1 and then 4, 5, 6.

Hints move from asking how many of the factors are negative, to pairing the negative factors since each pair makes a positive product, to even count positive and odd count negative with the magnitudes multiplied last, and last the negatives are paired on screen and the product shown. Through doing, the student learns that integers can be multiplied in any order and grouped in any way, and that an even count of negative factors gives a positive product and an odd count a negative one.

(tool id M5.S25.T25.C92.2D1, concept M5.S25.T25.C92, topic: Many Factors: Grouping and Sign)

Tool Name: Token Rectangle Splitter

Tool Description:

The student sees a rectangle of green and red tokens, four rows tall, each row holding 2 greens followed by 3 reds, with a draggable divider between the green block and the red block. Steppers set the number of rows a and the two block widths b and c, and a negative a turns every row into its inverse. Expressions for the whole rectangle and for each block update beside it. Before touching anything the student predicts the value of the whole rectangle.

First the student reads the rectangle row by row: each row is 2 + (-3) = -1, so 4 rows give 4 times (-1) = -4. Then the student drags the divider and reads block by block: 8 greens and 12 reds, 4 times 2 + 4 times (-3) = 8 + (-12) = -4. The two readings are written one under the other, so the equal values line up. Multiplying only the first block is allowed, and the tool then shows the tokens of the second block that were left out, which is the usual slip of reading a times (b + c) as a times b + c.

Next the student sets a to -4, so every row becomes 2 reds and 3 greens. Four rows make 8 reds and 12 greens, 8 zero pairs cancel, and 4 greens are left, which agrees with (-4) times 2 + (-4) times (-3) = -8 + 12 = 4. Further cards are 5 times (4 + (-2)), giving 10, and (-2) times (4 + (-3)), giving -2. Then (-5) times (18 + (-3)) is read two ways, as (-5) times 15 and as -90 + 15, and both give -75.

The number cards need a smart split. Given (-548) times 972 = -532656, the student finds (-547) times 972, (-548) times 971 and (-547) times 971; the rectangle is drawn as a strip of one row more or one row fewer, and the answers are -531684, -532108 and -531137. Given 207 times (-33 + 7) = -5382, the student finds -207 times (33 - 7), which is also -5382, because both signs have flipped. Hints move from asking what the whole rectangle stands for, to splitting it into the b block and the c block and finding each, to adding the two blocks, and last both readings are shown with their numbers. Through doing, the student learns that a times (b + c) = a times b + a times c for every integer, and that the property turns a hard product into easy ones.

(tool id M5.S28.T28.C101.2D1, concept M5.S28.T28.C101, topic: The Distributive Property)

Tool Name: Pattern Machine

Tool Description:

Three input slots feed a machine and one output star lights up. On the left are the rows of the machine, and below it an expression builder offers a, b, c, plus, minus, times and brackets. A Test button runs the student's guess on every row, and a Loop mode feeds the output back into the machine. The first machine has the rows 5, 8, 3 giving 10; 10, 11, 12 giving 9; 5, 8, -3 giving 16; -3, 10, 2 giving 5; -4, -1, -6 giving 1; and a last row -10, -12, -9 giving a question mark.

The student studies the simplest row, builds a guess as an expression and presses Test. A guess that fits only some rows is kept on screen with the rows it misses marked, so the student can improve it. Rows with negative numbers are the best tests, because they catch sign slips. When a + b - c fits every row, the student predicts the missing output, (-10) + (-12) - (-9) = -13. Machine 2 has the rows 4, 8, -3 giving -29; 6, -11, 12 giving 54; 5, 3, 7 giving -22; -3, 9, -8 giving 35; -7, 4, 6 giving 22; and -10, -12, -9 giving a question mark. The student finds that -(a times b) - c fits every row, and the missing output is -111. The end-of-chapter machine follows the same way with a - b times c, and its last output is -70.

In Loop mode the machine uses the modified Collatz rule: halve an even number, and multiply an odd number by -3 and add 1. From -7 the path runs -7, 22, 11, -32, -16, -8, -4, -2, -1, 4, 2, 1 and then back to -2, and the student also runs -21 and -6. A path that returns to an earlier number is drawn as a loop. Then the student starts at 13 and finds that the path comes back to 13 after 31 numbers, a loop of its own that never reaches 1. The student then builds the largest and smallest expressions from 3, -2, 5 and -6, using +, - and times once each with brackets as needed; the best value so far is kept and the student keeps trying, reaching 60 and -60. Last the student makes a machine of their own and swaps it with a partner.

Hints move from asking what the inputs of the simplest row do to give the output, to testing the rule on a row with negative numbers, to trying a product of two inputs and then adding or subtracting the third, and last the rule is shown working on every row. Through doing, the student learns that a guessed rule is kept only if it fits every row, that a rule can be written as an expression in a, b and c, and that brackets and the order of operations decide the largest and smallest values.

(tool id M5.S31.T31.C114.2D1, concept M5.S31.T31.C114, topic: Pick the Pattern: Rule Machines)

Tool Name: Pibs Purse

Tool Description:

The student is handed a purse holding only two kinds of coin from an alien currency, +13 pibs and -9 pibs. A counter on the table collects the coins, a number line shows the running total, and two ladders list the multiples of 13 and of 9 side by side. A price card tells the student what to make, and before dragging any coin the student predicts how many of each coin will be needed.

The student drags coins onto the counter. A +13 coin moves the total 13 to the right and a -9 coin moves it 9 back to the left, and the number line shows how far the total is from the target. Every total is allowed, and removing a coin is always allowed, so the student can undo and try again. The first price is +85. Before dragging, the student can read the ladders for a multiple of 13 and a multiple of 9 that differ by 85, and 130 and 45 do. So 10 coins of +13 bring the total to 130 and 5 coins of -9 bring it back to 85, and the student writes the total as the number sentence 10 times 13 + 5 times (-9) = 85.

Six cards follow. The second asks for +20, +40 and +8. A +13 coin with a -9 coin makes +4, so five pairs make +20, ten pairs make +40 and two pairs make +8. The third asks for -50 and -2, which need 1 coin of +13 with 7 of -9, and 4 coins of +13 with 6 of -9. The fourth asks for +10 and +1, made with 7 coins of +13 and 9 of -9, and with 7 of +13 and 10 of -9. The fifth asks whether 1568 pibs can be paid and to show one way, for example 122 coins of +13 and 2 of -9, which is 1586 - 18. In the last card, once +1 is made, the tool shows that repeating that combination reaches any price, and the student makes any price a partner names.

Hints move from asking how far the counter is from the target and in which direction, to looking for a multiple of 13 and a multiple of 9 that differ by the target, to trying one more +13 coin and seeing which -9 coins bring the total back, and last one way is shown coin by coin. Through doing, the student learns that a total is the number of +13 coins times 13 plus the number of -9 coins times (-9), that a negative coin can help to pay a positive price, and that once +1 can be made every total is possible.

Tool Name: Terhüchü Match

Tool Description:

Two players sit at one screen with the Terhuchu board between them, a 4 by 4 square of 16 cells with its diagonals and a triangle attached at each corner and at the middle of each side. Each player has a set of 9 coins that look different from the other player's, placed in the starting position. Below the board each player's lead counter shows a signed number, the coins captured minus the coins lost, and it starts at 0.

A player picks one of their coins and its legal destinations light up. The coin can slide along a line to a neighbouring empty point, or jump over an opponent's coin that has an empty point just beyond it, and the coin jumped over is captured and removed. After a jump the student checks whether another coin can be jumped from the new point, because captures can be chained in one move and the direction may change after every jump. Inside a triangle a coin may skip over an empty point to the next one. A move that is not allowed is not made: the coin returns and the rule it breaks is shown. After each capture both lead counters change, one up and one down by the same amount, so the student sees +1 on one side and -1 on the other.

The student first works four puzzle positions: making the first three moves of a game, finding the one capture that is available, finding a double capture, and using the triangle skip to escape. Then the two players play a full game. A player wins by capturing all of the opponent's coins or by leaving them no legal move, and if a draw seems unavoidable the player with more coins wins. At the end the tool shows the winner and the final lead as a signed number, such as +2 and -2.

Hints move from asking which of your coins can move and where, to looking for an opponent's coin next to yours with an empty point behind it, to checking again after a jump for a second capture, and last the best move is highlighted and the game continues.
