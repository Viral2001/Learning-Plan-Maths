# maths7_ch8 - tool descriptions (8 tools)

Tool Name: Fraction of a Journey Strip

Tool Description:

The student opens a journey desk with one long strip that stands for the distance a walker covers in 1 hour. A picker sets that distance to a whole number of kilometres from 1 to 8, and the strip is marked from 0 to that many kilometres. Below it are two pickers for the time as a fraction of an hour, a denominator from 2 to 8 and a numerator from 1 to 8, and beside the strip is a result space for the distance as a fraction and as a mixed fraction. Before anything moves, the student taps one of three choices: the new distance will be less than, equal to, or more than the distance in one hour.

Next the student sets the time. With 3 km in 1 hour and a time of 1/5 hour, the strip is cut into 5 equal parts, each part is labelled 3/5 km, and 1 part is shaded. Changing the numerator to 2 shades a second part, and the result reads 2 times 3/5 = 6/5 km, which is 1 and 1/5 km. Two lines of working appear under the strip, divide the distance by the denominator and then multiply by the numerator, so every shaded length is tied to a step. When the numerator is bigger than the denominator, for example 5/4 hour, the first strip is fully shaded and a second strip with the same cuts appears beside it to hold the extra part. If the student's prediction ignored whether the time was below or above 1 hour, the tool asks that question before it accepts the answer.

The cards use 3 km with 1/5 hour (3/5 km), 3 km with 2/5 hour (6/5 km), 5 km with 3/4 hour (15/4 km) and 7 km with 1/2 hour (7/2 km). On the fifth card the student sets the time to 5/4 hour and says why one strip is not enough. On the last card the distance is 6 km and the student finds two time settings that look different but give the same distance, such as 1/2 hour and 2/4 hour, and sees both shaded lengths end at the same point, 3 km, because they are the same fraction of the hour. A wrong distance is checked against the number of parts shaded, so the student can count the parts and correct it. Hints come in order: is the time more or less than 1 hour; cut the strip into as many equal parts as the denominator; shade as many parts as the numerator and write them as a fraction of a kilometre; and last, the strip is shown cut and shaded and a time with a different denominator follows.

Through doing, the student learns that a fraction of an hour covers that fraction of the one-hour distance: cut the distance into equal parts by the denominator and take as many parts as the numerator.

(tool id M1.S2.T2.C6.2D1, concept M1.S2.T2.C6, topic: A Fraction and a Whole Number)

Tool Name: Unit Square Product Maker

Tool Description:

A blank unit square, standing for one whole, fills the middle of the screen. A fraction picker sits on each side of it, each with a denominator from 2 to 8 and a numerator up to the denominator. Three counters wait under the square: overlap parts, all parts, and the product. On each card the student first types a prediction of how many equal parts the square will be cut into.

The student sets the first fraction and its columns appear, with the chosen columns shaded in one colour. For 2/3 that is 3 columns with 2 shaded. The student then sets the second fraction and its rows appear across the same square, with the chosen rows shaded in a second colour. For 4/5 that is 5 rows with 4 shaded. Where the two colours cross, the overlap turns a third colour. The counters read 8 overlap parts and 15 all parts, so the student types 8 as the numerator and 15 as the denominator of the product. Labels along two edges give the sides of the overlap rectangle, 2/3 and 4/5, so the product is also seen as the area of a rectangle with fractional sides. When the product is not in lowest form, as for 3/4 times 2/5 with 6 parts out of 20, the tool writes 6/20 and then 3/10.

A wrong product is compared with the overlap part by part. If the student adds across and writes 2/8 for 1/3 times 1/5, the tool shades 2 of 8 equal parts of a second unit square beside the real overlap of 1 part in 15, and the two shaded areas plainly differ. The cards are 1/3 times 1/5 (15 parts, 1 shaded), 1/4 times 1/3 (12 parts, 1 shaded), 1/5 times 1/2 (10 parts, 1 shaded), 2/3 times 4/5 (15 parts, 8 shaded) and 3/4 times 2/5 (20 parts, 6 shaded). The last card has no sliders, since the pickers stop at 8. The student explains why 1/12 times 1/18 has 216 parts by typing the 12 columns and 18 rows and multiplying them. Hints ask how many rows and columns the square will have, then say that each denominator gives one count, then ask for the overlap on top and all the parts below, and last the counters are filled in and a similar pair follows.

Through doing, the student learns that the two denominators decide how many equal parts the whole is cut into, the overlap of the two shaded sets is the numerator, and the product is the overlap over all the parts, not the two fractions added across.

(tool id M1.S6.T6.C29.2D1, concept M1.S6.T6.C29, topic: Multiplying Two Fractions with a Unit Square)

Tool Name: Cancel Then Multiply

Tool Description:

Two fractions stand side by side on a work bench with a times sign between them, and every number is a tile that can be picked up and linked. Before any link is made, the student taps the tiles that they think share a factor, so the guess is on screen before the working starts.

Then the student drags a thread from a top tile to a bottom tile. For 12/7 times 5/24 the thread from 12 to 24 opens a small panel that lists the factors of both tiles and lights up 12 as the shared factor. The student presses Divide, both tiles change, 12 becomes 1 and 24 becomes 2, and the old numbers fade out. A link between two tops is refused, since only a top and a bottom can cancel. A link between a top and a bottom with nothing in common, such as 5 and 24, is refused with the factors of both listed so the student sees that only 1 is shared. The student keeps linking until no top shares a factor with any bottom, including the bottom of its own fraction.

When no link is left, the student presses Multiply. The remaining tops are multiplied and the remaining bottoms are multiplied, giving 5/14. The tool also multiplies straight across, 60/168, and reduces it, so the student sees both routes end at 5/14 and that cancelling first keeps the numbers small and leaves the product in lowest form.

The cards are 12/7 times 5/24 (cancel 12 with 24, product 5/14), 14/15 times 25/42 (cancel 14 with 42 and 25 with 15, product 5/9), 15/4 times 48/5 (cancel 15 with 5 and 48 with 4, product 36), 9/10 times 25/27 (product 5/6) and 8/21 times 14/16 (product 1/3, which takes several rounds of linking). On the last card the student types a pair of fractions where nothing cancels, such as 2/3 times 5/7, and the tool tests every top and bottom pair to confirm that none shares a factor. Hints ask which number on top shares a factor with a number below, then say to list the factors of a numerator and of a denominator in the other fraction, then to divide both linked numbers by the biggest factor they share, and last the link is shown with its factor and the student multiplies what is left. Through doing, the student learns that a numerator and a denominator that share a factor can both be divided by it before multiplying, and that when no pair shares a factor the product is already in lowest form.

(tool id M1.S11.T11.C46.2D1, concept M1.S11.T11.C46, topic: Cancelling Common Factors)

Tool Name: Product Size Predictor

Tool Description:

Two number lines from 0 to 6 are stacked on the screen. On the upper line two coloured markers show the two numbers being multiplied, and each is moved by its own slider that takes whole numbers and fractions. The lower line holds faint copies of both markers, a tick at 1, and room for the product. Before multiplying, the student chooses where the product will land: left of both numbers, between them, or right of both.

The student then presses Multiply and the product marker slides along the lower line to its place. Three labels light up, saying whether the product is greater or smaller than the first number, than the second number and than 1. Dragging a slider moves the product live, and swapping the two numbers leaves the product marker exactly where it was. A product beyond 6 shows as an arrow with its value. Before a new pair is offered, the student must state the rule for the region just seen, choosing between short sentences such as a number above 1 makes the other number grow and a number between 0 and 1 makes it shrink. A guess that multiplication always makes a number bigger is tested with a pair between 0 and 1: for 3/4 and 2/5 the product 3/10 lands left of both, and the tool points out that it is a part of a part.

The cards are 4/3 and 4 (product 16/3, greater than both); 3/4 and 2/5 (product 3/10, smaller than both); 3/4 and 5 (product 15/4, less than 5 and greater than 3/4); any number with 1 (the product equals the other number); and 565/465 and 707/676, typed in rather than slid, both a little above 1. On that card the line zooms to the stretch from 1 to 1.5, where the three markers sit close together, and the product turns out greater than both numbers and greater than 1. The last card is a free search for a pair whose product is smaller than both. Hints ask whether each number is below or above 1, then say that a number below 1 shrinks the other number and a number above 1 grows it, then ask the student to compare the product with each number and with 1, and last the three markers are shown and a similar pair follows.

Through doing, the student learns that whether a product is greater or smaller than each number depends only on whether each number is below or above 1, and that multiplying by 1 changes nothing.

(tool id M1.S14.T14.C64.2D1, concept M1.S14.T14.C64, topic: Is the Product Always Greater?)

Tool Name: Reciprocal Flipper

Tool Description:

Two cards sit on a table with a division sign between them, the dividend on the left and the divisor on the right, and an empty check line runs underneath. The divisor card carries a Flip button. Before touching it, the student types what they think the reciprocal of the divisor will be.

When the student flips the divisor, the card turns over like a coin: its numerator and denominator swap and the division sign turns into a times sign. The check line fills at once with the divisor times its reciprocal, for example 3/5 times 5/3 = 1. The student then multiplies the dividend by the flipped divisor. Common factors are cancelled with a line drawn through them, and the product is written as a fraction and as a mixed fraction. Last, the student multiplies the quotient by the divisor and sees the dividend come back. A whole number is shown with 1 beneath it, so 3 sits as 3/1 when it needs to be handled as a fraction.

The student is also free to try flipping the wrong card. In 1/5 divided by 1/2, flipping the dividend gives 5, and the check line reads 1/2 times 5 = 5/2 and not 1, so the flip is shown not to fit. Flipping both cards gives 5 times 2 = 10, which fails the check because 1/2 times 10 is 5 and not 1/5. A mixed fraction must first be changed into a fraction, so 3 and 2/3 becomes 11/3 and 1 and 3/8 becomes 11/8, before any flip is allowed.

The cards are 1 divided by 2/3 (3/2), 3 divided by 2/3 (9/2), 1/5 divided by 1/2 (2/5), 2/3 divided by 3/5 (10/9) and 3 and 2/3 divided by 1 and 3/8, where the two 11s cancel and the answer is 8/3, which is 2 and 2/3. On the last card the student tries divisors until one has a reciprocal that is the same number, and finds 1. Hints ask which of the two fractions is turned over, then say to turn over the divisor, the fraction after the division sign, then to change the sign to times and multiply the dividend by the flipped divisor, and last the check line shows the divisor times the quotient and a similar division follows. Through doing, the student learns that dividing by a fraction means multiplying by its reciprocal, and that a fraction times its reciprocal is always 1.

(tool id M2.S17.T17.C75.2D1, concept M2.S17.T17.C75, topic: Dividing Fractions with Reciprocals)

Tool Name: Quotient Size Predictor

Tool Description:

A long ruler marked from 0 to 30 lies across the screen, with a dividend slider and a divisor slider above it, each taking whole numbers and fractions. A coloured marker shows the dividend, and once the division is run a second marker shows the quotient. Before running anything, the student chooses whether the quotient will be bigger than, smaller than or equal to the dividend.

When the student presses Divide, pieces as long as the divisor are laid end to end from 0 along the dividend, and a counter rises with each piece. For 6 divided by 3, two long pieces fit exactly, so the quotient marker sits at 2, to the left of the dividend. For 6 divided by 1/4 the pieces are tiny, 24 of them fit, and the quotient marker jumps to 24, far to the right of the dividend. For 1/8 divided by 1/4 the ruler zooms in to 0 to 1 and shows that only half of one piece fits, so the quotient is 1/2. For 9 divided by 3/2, six pieces fit, and for 9 divided by 2/3 thirteen whole pieces and half of another fit, which is 27/2. After the count, one line writes the same quotient as the dividend times the reciprocal of the divisor, such as 6 times 4.

The number of pieces is compared with the quotient the student typed, so a wrong value is checked against the pieces on the ruler. A guess that dividing always makes a number smaller is tested with a divisor between 0 and 1. The divisor 1 is explained as the case where one piece is exactly as long as the dividend, so nothing changes. After each card, the student says whether the divisor was above 1 or between 0 and 1 and what that did to the quotient. The six cards are 6 divided by 3 (2, smaller), 6 divided by 1/4 (24, greater), 1/8 divided by 1/4 (1/2, greater), 9 divided by 3/2 (6, smaller), 9 divided by 2/3 (27/2, greater), and a last search for a divisor that leaves the quotient equal to the dividend. Hints ask whether the divisor is above 1 or between 0 and 1, then say that a divisor above 1 gives a quotient smaller than the dividend and a divisor below 1 gives a larger one, then ask how many divisor-sized pieces fit in the dividend, and last the pieces are drawn on the line and a similar pair follows.

Through doing, the student learns that dividing by a number above 1 gives a smaller quotient, dividing by a number between 0 and 1 gives a larger quotient because small pieces fit many times, and dividing by 1 leaves the dividend unchanged.

(tool id M2.S19.T19.C81.2D1, concept M2.S19.T19.C81, topic: Dividend, Divisor and Quotient)

Tool Name: Multiply or Divide Chooser

Tool Description:

A story card sits at the top of the screen, such as 8 m of lace with 1/4 m used for each bag, and the question is how many bags. Under it is a balance frame reading (number of things) times (size of each) = total, with a gap box where the missing quantity goes. The student reads the story, taps the number that is the total, the number that is the size of each and the quantity that is missing, and the frame fills in: gap times 1/4 = 8.

Four expressions are offered: the multiplication of the two numbers, the division the right way round, the division the wrong way round, and the sum of the two numbers. When the student picks one, it is worked in steps. For 8 divided by 1/4, the tool writes 8 times the reciprocal 4, giving 32. The answer is then put back into the frame, 32 times 1/4 = 8, and the tool asks whether 32 bags is sensible when each bag needs less than 1 m. A reversed division, 1/4 divided by 8 = 1/32, is put back and fails: 1/32 times 1/4 is nowhere near 8, and the answer says less than one bag, which does not fit the story.

The next cards are 1/2 m of ribbon for 8 badges (frame 8 times gap = 1/2, so each badge gets 1/2 divided by 8 = 1/16 m), 5 kg of flour with 1/6 kg for each loaf (5 divided by 1/6 = 30 loaves), 5 cups of tea sharing 1/4 litre of milk (1/4 divided by 5 = 1/20 litre) and 1/5 of a cake shared by 3 friends (1/5 divided by 3 = 1/15). On the last card the student writes a story of their own for 3/4 divided by 3 by filling a frame such as 3/4 of a ___ is shared equally among 3 ___. The tool checks that the total is 3/4 and that there are 3 equal shares, then puts the answer 1/4 back, 3 times 1/4 = 3/4.

Hints ask which number in the story is the total and which is one share, then say to write the number of shares times the size of one share equals the total, with the missing one as a gap, then to divide the total by the known part using the reciprocal, and last the gap box shows the missing part and a similar story follows. Through doing, the student learns that stories about sharing or fitting are multiplications with a missing number, that the missing number is found by dividing, which is multiplying by the reciprocal, and that the answer must be put back to see whether it fits the story.

(tool id M3.S20.T20.C85.2D1, concept M3.S20.T20.C85, topic: Problems with Fractions)

Tool Name: Fraction of a Fraction Splitter

Tool Description:

The student starts with one whole square, the number 1 written above it, and an empty total bar beneath it. The student predicts the fraction of the whole that the next piece will be, then taps a piece and chooses to cut it into a number of equal parts and take some of them, one step at a time. Each step adds a line to a running product beside the square, so the fraction of the original whole is always visible.

On the first card the student takes 1/4 of the square, one of four equal squares, then taps that quarter, cuts it into 2 equal parts and takes 1. The readout shows 1/4 times 1/2 = 1/8 of the whole. On the second card the student takes 3/4 of that 1/8, cutting it into 4 parts and taking 3, and the readout grows to 1/4 times 1/2 times 3/4 = 3/32. The student types the product before the tool confirms it. If the student takes the second fraction of the whole instead of the piece, for example 3/4 of the whole square, the tool shades that much of the square beside the real piece so the difference in size is clear. Each piece fills the total bar in its own colour, and the leftover pieces are grey until the student taps each one and reads its fraction. The bar must reach 1 before the card closes.

The last cards swap the square for a colony of ants on a trail that splits equally at each fork. The third card follows one path that splits in half twice, 1/2 times 1/2 = 1/4. The fourth card shows the full trail in four stages. Half the ants go to the mango tree at the first fork, then half of the rest, 1/4, at the second. At the third fork the remaining 1/4 splits into four groups of 1/16, two go to the mango tree, one to the sugarcane field and one carries on, and the last fork sends 1/32 to each food source. The student adds the mango shares 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and 1/32 to get 29/32, then the sugarcane shares 1/16 and 1/32 to get 3/32, and the total bar shows 29/32 and 3/32 together make 1. On the last card the student makes their own piece of 3/16 of the whole by any route, such as 3/4 of 1/4, and the tool checks the product of the steps.

Hints ask whether the student is taking a fraction of the whole or of a part, then say to multiply the fraction of this step by the fraction of the piece before it, then to keep multiplying down the path and write one fraction, and last the total bar shows what is left and a similar split follows. Through doing, the student learns that a part of a part is found by multiplying the fractions down the path, and that all the pieces of one whole add back to exactly 1.

(tool id M3.S23.T23.C108.2D1, concept M3.S23.T23.C108, topic: Fractions of Fractions in a Square)
