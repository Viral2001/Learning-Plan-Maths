## 1. Hard rules (gates enforce most; fix content, never a gate)
- Class 5 students, 10 to 11 years old: short plain sentences. Characters and settings are the book's: Susie and Sunitha's coconut farm in Kerala (grove, packing shed, bags, crates, oil press), Sunitha's mother, Ibrahim, Munshi Lal and the Sundar Sabzi Mandi, Sabina, Seema, Rani, Sofia, Naina, Megha, Rahul. Do not invent facts or datasets: every number that is not a plain WE or practice number comes from the book or from section 4. The plan's own WE and tool numbers are practice values and are said to be.
- **No em dash and no en dash** in any string or comment (the book's dashes become commas, colons or "to"). Minus is U+2212 `−`. Use `×` and `÷`. Hyphen only inside words ("3-digit" is fine).
- Never write "NCERT", "textbook", "page N", "Fig.", "Figure N" or exercise numbers in anything the student reads (names, teach lines, steps, questions, tool text). Say "your maths book". Exception: the `textbook=[...]` argument of `A()` quotes the book verbatim (still no dashes).
- **Arithmetic must be true in every string.** The equation gate (`eqcheck_plain`) reads every whole-number chain `a op b = c` in steps, answers, WE text, objectives and tool configs (it does NOT read the text of a question card) and fails the build on a false chain. Traps found while designing:
  1. **A division with a remainder is never written `726 ÷ 4 = 181 R 2` or `726 ÷ 4 = 181`** (the gate reads `726 ÷ 4 = 181` and calls it false). Write "726 ÷ 4: quotient 181, remainder 2" and `726 = 4 × 181 + 2` (this chain is true and passes). The word is always spelled "remainder"; R is only the letter in N = D × Q + R.
  2. A blank before a number, `___ ÷ 18 = 100`, is read as the chain `18 = 100` and fails. Blanks written like that live only in the question text (not scanned). Never write them in a step, answer, teach line or tool text; in those write the answer: `1800 ÷ 18 = 100`.
  3. A chain with a unit word after a number can pass or fail unpredictably (`13 Tens ÷ 6 = 2 Tens` is skipped, `13 tens ÷ 6 = 2 tens` is read as `6 = 2`). In place value steps write words: "13 tens shared among 6 plates: 2 tens each, 1 ten left". Keep every chain whole-number only, one operation family per line.
  4. A chain whose sides count different things of the same number (for example `4 × 6 = 24 coconuts, 24 ÷ 6 = 4 rows`) is fine as long as each `=` really holds; write a new sentence for a new fact.
  5. **A parenthesis is never opened directly before a chain**: the gate skips `(6 × 7 = 42)` at its first number and then reads `7 = 42` as a false chain. Write "because 6 × 7 = 42, and ...".
  6. **Tool specs are scanned too** (their text is stored as `tool_config`): a quoted book statement that is false on purpose (`20 − 6 = 7 × 3`, `___ ÷ 9 = 16 ÷ 2`, `Is 726 = 4 × 181?`) fails the build inside a tool. In a tool quote the sides in words ("20 − 6 and 7 × 3 on the two sides of the equal sign", "Is 726 the same as 4 × 181?"). The same holds for a worked example (WE) question text. The question card text of a FIO is not scanned, so the book's wording stays there.
  7. Remember `a ÷ b = c` is checked left to right: `500 − 2 = 498` fine, `1992 ÷ 4 = 500 − 2 = 498` is fine (three true sides).
  Re-compute every other fact (every product, quotient, remainder, check N = D × Q + R, every conversion 5 kg = 5000 g, every comparison) with Python before you finish, with a script you keep in the scratchpad.

**Teaching concept seeds** (the WE is the plan's own, practice values; every number below is already verified and is not a number of any book question; use them):
- T1B.C1 two division facts: an array of coconuts, 3 rows of 9 = 27. 3 × 9 = 27. Split the 27 coconuts into 9 groups (the columns): 27 ÷ 9 = 3 in each group. Split into 3 groups (the rows): 27 ÷ 3 = 9 in each group. One multiplication fact gives two division facts.
- T1B.C2 dividend, divisor, quotient: 45 ÷ 5 = 9: dividend (N) 45, divisor (D) 5, quotient (Q) 9. N = D × Q: 5 × 9 = 45. Dividing by 1: 45 ÷ 1 = 45 because 1 × 45 = 45 (Think first: does dividing always make the number smaller? No).
- T2.C1 table facts for tens and hundreds: how many 9s in 270? 270 is 27 tens; 27 ÷ 9 = 3, so 3 tens = 30; check 9 × 30 = 270. Then 2700 is 27 hundreds: 2700 ÷ 9 = 300 and 2700 ÷ 90 = 30.
- T2.C2 zeros and place value: 70 ÷ 10 = 7, 700 ÷ 10 = 70, 7000 ÷ 10 = 700: one more zero in the dividend, one more zero in the quotient, the digit 7 moves one place right each time. When the dividend is the divisor with zeros added, the quotient is 10, 100: 3500 ÷ 35 = 100 because 35 × 100 = 3500.
- T3.C1 split: 2856 ÷ 7: 2856 = 2800 + 56; 2800 ÷ 7 = 400 and 56 ÷ 7 = 8; so 2856 ÷ 7 = 400 + 8 = 408. Check 7 × 408 = 2856. A convenient split makes parts the divisor divides easily.
- T3.C2 round up and take away: 2994 ÷ 3: 2994 = 3000 − 6; 3000 ÷ 3 = 1000 and 6 ÷ 3 = 2; so 2994 ÷ 3 = 1000 − 2 = 998. Check 3 × 998 = 2994. The extra that was added is taken away.
- T3.C3 halve again and again: 264 ÷ 4: halve twice: half of 264 is 132, half of 132 is 66; so 264 ÷ 4 = 66. Check 4 × 66 = 264. Dividing by 8 is three halvings: 216 ÷ 8: 108, 54, 27; check 8 × 27 = 216 (use as a second line in `teach` only if you wish, the WE is 264 ÷ 4).
- T4.C1 take away groups: 378 coconuts packed in groups of 6: 6 × 20 = 120; 378 − 120 = 258; 258 − 120 = 138; 138 − 120 = 18; 6 × 3 = 18; 18 − 18 = 0. The groups taken away: 20 + 20 + 20 + 3 = 63. So 378 ÷ 6: quotient 63. Check 6 × 63 = 378.
- T4.C2 biggest group that fits: 861 ÷ 7. Estimate first: 7 × 100 = 700 and 7 × 200 = 1400, so the answer is between 100 and 200. Take 7 × 100 = 700: 861 − 700 = 161. Take 7 × 20 = 140: 161 − 140 = 21. Take 7 × 3 = 21: 21 − 21 = 0. 100 + 20 + 3 = 123. Check 7 × 123 = 861. Too big: 7 × 200 = 1400 is more than 861.
- T4.C3 remainder rule: 487 ÷ 9: 9 × 50 = 450, 487 − 450 = 37; 9 × 4 = 36, 37 − 36 = 1; quotient 50 + 4 = 54, remainder 1. 487 = 9 × 54 + 1 (9 × 54 = 486). The remainder is always less than the divisor (1 is less than 9). N = D × Q + R.
- T5.C1 hundreds, tens, ones groups: 6,534 ÷ 18: 18 × 300 = 5400, 6534 − 5400 = 1134; 18 × 60 = 1080, 1134 − 1080 = 54; 18 × 3 = 54, 54 − 54 = 0; 300 + 60 + 3 = 363. Check 363 × 18 = 6534. Why 300 and not 400: 18 × 400 = 7200 is more than 6534.
- T6.C1 regroup and share: 83 candies among 6 children (practice values): 83 is 8 tens and 3 ones. 8 tens shared among 6: each gets 1 ten, 2 tens left. Regroup the 2 tens into 20 ones: 20 + 3 = 23 ones. 23 ones shared among 6: each gets 3 ones (6 × 3 = 18), 5 ones left. Each child gets 13, remainder 5. 83 = 6 × 13 + 5.
- T6.C2 regroup hundreds and hold the place with zero: 412 ÷ 4: 4 hundreds shared among 4: 1 hundred each; 1 ten cannot be shared among 4, so 0 tens each (write 0); regroup the 1 ten into 10 ones: 10 + 2 = 12 ones; 12 ones shared among 4: 3 each. 412 ÷ 4: quotient 103. How many digits: 215 ÷ 4: 2 hundreds cannot be shared among 4, regroup into 21 tens: 53, remainder 3, a 2-digit quotient (the first digit 2 is less than 4, so one digit fewer than the dividend); 412 has first digit 4 which is at least 4, so the quotient has 3 digits.
- T6.C3 thousands and two-digit divisors, both methods: 3,648 ÷ 12. Partial quotients: 12 × 300 = 3600, 3648 − 3600 = 48; 12 × 4 = 48; 300 + 4 = 304. Place value: 36 hundreds shared among 12: 3 hundreds each (12 × 3 = 36); 4 tens cannot be shared among 12 so 0 tens (the zero holds the tens place); 48 ones among 12: 4 each. Both give 304. Check 12 × 304 = 3648.
- T7.C1 what the remainder means: 94 coconuts: 94 ÷ 8: quotient 11, remainder 6 (94 = 8 × 11 + 6). Packing in bags of 8: 11 full bags and 6 coconuts left, the 6 need one more bag: 12 bags. Sharing among 8 customers: 11 coconuts each, 6 left over that are not shared. The remainder is used differently in each story.
- T7.C2 missing numbers in a bill (practice values): onions ₹18 for 1 kg, 45 kg: total = 18 × 45 = ₹810. Missing quantity: total ₹990 at ₹18 for 1 kg: 990 ÷ 18 = 55 kg (check 18 × 55 = 990). Missing cost: total ₹1,260 for 36 kg: 1,260 ÷ 36 = ₹35 for 1 kg (check 35 × 36 = 1,260). Total = cost of 1 kg × quantity, so the missing number is the total divided by the number you know.
- T7.C3 break a problem into steps (practice values): 4 baskets with 36 coconuts each are packed equally into 8 sacks. Step 1 what is asked: coconuts in each sack. Step 2 what is given: 4 baskets, 36 in each, 8 sacks. Step 3 find all the coconuts: 4 × 36 = 144. Step 4 share into 8 sacks: 144 ÷ 8 = 18. Check 8 × 18 = 144. If the question asked for money earned, a number is missing (the price of a coconut), so the problem cannot be solved until it is given.
- T8.C1 how the quotient changes (practice values): 480 ÷ 12 = 40. Double the dividend: 960 ÷ 12 = 80 (the quotient doubles). Double the divisor: 480 ÷ 24 = 20 (the quotient halves). Double both: 960 ÷ 24 = 40 (the quotient stays the same). A rectangle: area N = side D × side Q.
- T9.C1 both sides of the equal sign: a statement is true when both sides have the same value. 6 × 5 = 20 + 10: both are 30, true. 18 ÷ 3 = 2 × 4: 6 and 8, false. Complete 9 × 4 = ___ + 16: 36 − 16 = 20; check 20 + 16 = 36.
- T10.C1 always, sometimes or never: "Adding 2 to an even number gives an even number" always (4 + 2 = 6, 10 + 2 = 12, 16 + 2 = 18); "Adding 1 to an even number gives an even number" never (4 + 1 = 5, 10 + 1 = 11); "Multiplying a number by 3 gives an even number" sometimes (3 × 4 = 12, even; 3 × 5 = 15, odd). One example that fails shows it is not always true; no example that works shows it is never true.
