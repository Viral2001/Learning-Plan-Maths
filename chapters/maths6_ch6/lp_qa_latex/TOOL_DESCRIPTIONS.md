# maths6_ch6 - tool descriptions (11 tools)

Tool Name: Edge or Inside

Tool Description:

The student sees a rectangle drawn on a faint grid with a round handle on every corner, and a small panel with two switches: Walk, which is teal, and Squares, which is amber. No numbers, units or scores appear anywhere on the screen. Before anything is switched on, a prompt asks the student to guess whether the teal walk will ever go inside the rectangle.

The student drags a corner and the rectangle stretches or shrinks, snapping to the grid. Switching on Walk draws a teal line that starts at one corner, goes all the way round the edge and comes back to the place where it began. Switching on Squares fills the inside with amber squares, row by row, and the filling stops exactly at the edge. Either switch can be turned off again, and both can be on together. With both on, the student drags a corner and watches the teal line and the amber squares follow the new size: the line always stays on the edge and the squares always stay inside.

Four short prompts guide the play: make the rectangle a different size, switch on the walk and watch where it goes, switch on the squares and watch where they go, then switch on both and drag a corner to see what changes. Nothing is marked wrong, and the tool says so in a quiet line: 'Nothing is marked wrong here. Try it any way you like.' When both switches are on it adds: 'The teal walk stays on the edge and the amber squares stay inside.' Because there is nothing to get wrong there are no hints, and the student stops whenever ready. Afterwards the tutor asks where the teal walk went and where the amber squares went.

Through doing, the student learns that one shape carries two different things to look at: an edge that can be walked along, like the fence Akshi puts round the plot, and an inside that can be filled, like the ground Toshi covers with tiles. The chapter then gives these two jobs their names.

(tool id M1.S1.T1.C2.2D1, concept M1.S1.T1.C2, topic: Two Questions About One Shape)

Tool Name: Boundary Walk

Tool Description:

A shape sits on a faint grid with the length of every side written beside it. Along the bottom runs an empty walk bar with a running total above it, and a side panel holds the task, a shape picker and an I am done button. Before tapping anything, the student guesses the total walk for the shape in front of them.

The student taps a side to walk it. The side turns teal, a teal piece of its length joins the bar and the running total grows by that length. Tapping a walked side again un-walks it and takes its length out of the bar. A side walked twice shows as a repeated piece on the bar, so the student can see the repeat and undo it. Pressing I am done checks the total. If a side has not been walked the tool says 'One side has not been walked yet', and when the total is right the whole boundary flashes green while the walk bar and the outline pulse together.

Four tasks follow. On a rectangle 12 cm by 8 cm, a student who walks only one length and one breadth sees the bar stop half full, then walks all four sides and gets 12 + 8 + 12 + 8 = 40 cm, which is 2 times (12 + 8). A triangle with sides 4 cm, 5 cm and 7 cm gives 16 cm, and because the three sides differ, one side times three would be wrong. A square park with sides of 75 m gives 300 m for one round, and the student then types the distance for three rounds, 900 m. The last shape is an L with six sides, 8, 6, 2, 4, 6 and 2 cm. The student must walk all six and finds 28 cm: no rule for four sides applies, so every side is added once.

Hints come in order: trace the edge once, starting and stopping at the same corner; count the sides walked against the sides the shape has; then look along the bar for the side that is still missing. Afterwards a free rectangle opens whose corners can be dragged, with its perimeter live. Through doing, the student learns that a perimeter is the total of the sides, each counted once, and that the rules for rectangles, squares and triangles are quick ways of adding the same walk.

(tool id M1.S2.T2.C7.2D1, concept M1.S2.T2.C7, topic: Walking the Boundary)

Tool Name: Wire Bender

Tool Description:

A straight teal wire lies across the top of the screen with unit ticks along it and a box for its length. Below it is a workspace where the wire will bend, with a strip for wire left over, and a shape picker offers triangle, square, pentagon, hexagon and rectangle. Before bending anything, the student guesses whether the wire will be shorter, longer or the same when it is straightened again.

The student types a length and picks a shape. The wire bends into that shape with equal sides and the length of one side appears. A rectangle can be reshaped by dragging a corner: the length and the breadth change while the wire stays exactly the same length, so a longer length always means a shorter breadth. Pressing Straighten returns the shape to the straight wire, and the length box flashes green because the wire is exactly as long as before. A shape that needs more wire than there is draws 'The wire does not reach.' A regular shape that leaves wire unused draws 'There is wire left over. A regular shape has to use it all.' and the strip shows how much.

Four cards use the wire. A 16 cm wire becomes a rectangle 5 cm by 3 cm, is straightened, and becomes a square with side 4 cm. A 36 cm string becomes a square, an equal-sided triangle and a regular hexagon, with sides 9 cm, 12 cm and 6 cm, so the more sides there are, the shorter each one is. For a rectangle with perimeter 12 m and length 3 m, the student takes the two lengths away from 12, shares what is left and finds a breadth of 3 m, so it is a square. The last card asks for a regular pentagon from a 22 cm wire with sides of whole centimetres. A side of 4 cm leaves 2 cm over, 5 cm needs 25 cm and does not reach, and the side counter reads 4 and 2/5 cm. The student presses check, learns the card cannot be done, and picks a reason from three: 22 does not share equally between 5 sides, a pentagon cannot be made from wire, or the wire is too short.

Hints go in order: the wire is the perimeter of whatever it makes; take away the sides you know and share what is left; a regular shape shares the wire equally, so divide by the number of sides. Through doing, the student learns that bending never changes the wire, so every shape made from it has the same perimeter and only the way the length is shared between the sides changes.

(tool id M1.S3.T3.C10.2D1, concept M1.S3.T3.C10, topic: Perimeter Puzzles)

Tool Name: Track Runner

Tool Description:

A rectangular track is drawn on a faint grid with the length of each side written beside it and a runner marker waiting at the start. Under the track sit a distance dial, a round counter and a leftover readout, and a flag button marks where the runner stands. Before the dial is touched, the student guesses where the runner will be when the distance reaches one and a half rounds.

The student drags the dial and the runner moves round the track. The trail changes shade at every full round, so the loop is easy to see, and the round counter and leftover update as the runner goes. Dropping a flag writes the runner's spot as whole rounds plus a leftover. The track size can be changed, and a second track can be switched on beside the first so two runners can be compared. If a flag is dropped in the wrong place for the task, the tool does not move it. It shows the correct split in grey beside the student's split and says 'Check the leftover.' When the answer is right the trail and the flag flash green together.

Four tasks follow. On a track 70 m by 40 m the student finds one round, 220 m, and runs 5 rounds for 1,100 m. On the same track a flag at 1,000 m gives 4 full rounds, which is 880 m, and 120 m past the start. On the track 60 m by 30 m, where one round is 180 m, flags at 250 m, 500 m and 1,000 m give 70 m past the start after 1 round, 140 m after 2 rounds, and 100 m after 5 rounds. The last task uses two square tracks with sides of 100 m and 150 m and a finish flag in the middle of one side. The student drags each start point back from the flag, against the arrows, while the distance to the flag shows live, until each runner covers exactly 350 m. The inner start is 50 + 100 + 100 + 100 back, and the outer start is 75 + 150 + 125 back.

Hints go in order: first find one round from the sides; then see how many whole rounds fit inside the distance and take them away; then measure the leftover from the start; for the last task, walk backwards from the flag, half a side first. Through doing, the student learns that a distance run on a loop is whole rounds plus a leftover, and that the leftover is less than one round and tells exactly where the runner stands.

(tool id M1.S6.T6.C29.2D1, concept M1.S6.T6.C29, topic: Round and Round the Track)

Tool Name: Dot Grid Builder

Tool Description:

The student faces a large dot grid. In one corner a small strip always shows one straight unit and one diagonal unit side by side, and at the bottom two tallies, s and d, both start at zero. Before drawing, the student guesses whether a shape made only of slanted edges has a longer or a shorter edge than a shape with the same number of straight edges.

Tapping a dot starts a shape and tapping a neighbouring dot draws an edge. The edge colours itself the moment it is drawn: teal if it goes straight across or up and down, reddish purple if it slants to the next row and column, and the matching tally goes up by one. Tapping the starting dot closes the shape, locks the tallies and writes the perimeter in two piles, for example 6s + 3d. Undo removes the last edge and clear empties the grid. If the student taps both tallies to add them, the tool says 'These cannot be added: s and d are different lengths.' A shape left open says 'Close the shape first: come back to your starting dot.' In free play a gallery keeps every shape the student draws.

Four tasks follow. First the student copies a triangle with 6 straight units and 3 slanted units and writes 6s + 3d, seeing that it is more than 9 units because three of its edges are longer. Next the third of the four chapter shapes is drawn and its tallies read 12s + 6d. In the third task the student draws a closed shape of only straight edges and another of only slanted edges with the same number of edges, such as a square and a tilted square, and finds the slanted one has the longer edge in total, since each slanted unit is longer than a straight unit. The last task asks for a closed shape with exactly one straight unit and every other edge slanted. The shape refuses to close, the open gap is marked, and the student picks a reason from three: with a single straight edge you cannot get back to where you started, slanted edges are not allowed on a dot grid, or the grid is too small.

Hints go in order: look at the colour of each edge; sort the edges into two piles; write the piles as s and d without joining them. Through doing, the student learns that a straight unit and a diagonal unit are different lengths, so a perimeter on a dot grid is counted in two separate piles.

(tool id M1.S8.T8.C42.2D1, concept M1.S8.T8.C42, topic: Straight Units and Slanted Units)

Tool Name: Split and Rejoin

Tool Description:

Two pale paper pieces lie in a workspace, each 6 cm by 2 cm, with each piece's own perimeter, 16 cm, written beside it. At the side sit a join readout, a large perimeter readout for the current shape, and a paper readout pinned underneath it that reads 24 sq cm. Before moving a piece, the student guesses whether a longer join will make the perimeter bigger or smaller.

The student drags a piece and turns it a quarter turn at a time. When an edge lines up the piece snaps to the other piece, the touching edge turns grey, and the join readout shows the length of the join and twice the join. The large readout shows the perimeter of the new shape. Dropping one piece on top of the other gives 'Paper cannot sit on paper', and the Separate button pulls the pieces apart so each shows its own perimeter again. Before building, the student types a prediction of the perimeter. A prediction of 32 is answered by showing the grey edges that nobody walks along.

Four tasks follow. Joined end to end along their 2 cm edges the pieces make a strip 12 cm by 2 cm, and the join of 2 cm gives 16 + 16 minus 2 times 2 = 28 cm. The pieces joined along 3 cm give 16 + 16 minus 2 times 3 = 26 cm. The L shape and the T shape both join along 2 cm and both read 28 cm, although they look different. The student then builds any shape with perimeter exactly 22 cm: 32 minus 22 means 10 cm must be lost, so the join is 5 cm, and the student slides one piece along the other until the long edges touch along 5 cm. In the last task the student moves the pieces into three different arrangements and reads the paper readout each time.

Hints go in order: find each piece's perimeter first; look at which edges turn grey; the perimeter is the two perimeters added minus twice the join. Through doing, the student learns that joined edges stop being boundary, that a longer join leaves a shorter perimeter, and that the paper stays 24 sq cm every time, so only the edge changes.

(tool id M1.S10.T10.C50.2D1, concept M1.S10.T10.C50, topic: Split and Rejoin)

Tool Name: Floor Coverer

Tool Description:

A floor made of pale amber unit squares fills the centre of the screen with its length and width written beside it. A counter shows the number of squares and the same number as length times width, and three readouts underneath show the whole area, the covered area and the area left over, always in square metres. Before a carpet is laid, the student guesses how many squares of floor will still show.

The student drags the corner of the floor to resize it, from 1 by 1 up to 15 by 15, and the counter updates as the floor grows. Count mode switches between the number and the squares being counted one by one. Dragging a carpet or a flower bed onto the floor dims the squares under it, dragging its corner resizes it, and dragging it off the floor removes it. A cover pushed past the edge says 'The carpet has to fit on the floor.' An answer typed without units is accepted and shown back with square metres added.

Four tasks follow. On a floor 5 m by 4 m the student lays a square carpet of side 3 m: 20 sq m minus 9 sq m leaves 11 sq m showing. On a plot 12 m by 10 m, four square flower beds of side 4 m go in the corners: 120 sq m minus 4 times 16 sq m leaves 56 sq m. For a grove 100 m by 50 m the student types the sizes and the floor redraws each square as 10 m by 10 m, with the scale shown; the whole area reads 5,000 sq m, and with 25 sq m for each tree the number of trees is 5,000 divided by 25 = 200. In the last task two floors, 6 m by 4 m and 8 m by 3 m, stand side by side. Both readouts show 24 sq m, but the perimeters are 20 m and 22 m. The tool says nothing and leaves the comparison to the student.

Hints go in order: find the area of the whole floor by length times width; find the area of the cover the same way; take the cover away from the whole rather than adding it. Through doing, the student learns that area is counted in squares, that what is left showing is the whole minus the covered part, and that two floors can have the same area and different perimeters.

(tool id M2.S12.T12.C57.2D1, concept M2.S12.T12.C57, topic: Covering a Region)

Tool Name: Grid Counter

Tool Description:

A figure is drawn in a thin dark outline on a square grid. Beside it a tally board keeps four counts, whole, more than half, less than half and exactly half, with the area in square units at the bottom, and a picker, a zoom button and a Show my total button sit below. Before tapping a square, the student guesses the area of the figure in square units.

The student taps each square under the figure, and each tap cycles the square through unmarked, whole, more than half, less than half and exactly half, each with its own colour and mark. The tally board counts as the student goes. Zoom on an edge square enlarges it so that more than half or less than half can be judged fairly. Show my total locks the tally and gives the area: whole squares plus more-than-half squares plus half of the exact halves, with the less-than-half squares ignored. The board reacts only to the total, never to a single tap. A total that does not match shows the four counts written as a sum, so the student can see which count to recheck.

Four tasks follow. The first of the four figures drawn on the grid has 3 whole squares and 2 exact halves, so 3 + 1/2 + 1/2 = 4 square units. The second has 6 whole and 6 halves, which is 9, and the third has 7 whole and 6 halves, which is 10. Next the student draws a circle of diameter 3 and counts it; any count from 6 to 8 is welcomed with 'Estimating is meant to be close, not exact', and about 7 square units is the usual answer. Finally the student switches the unit to circles and fills the same rectangle, counts, then packs the circles another way and counts again. The gaps between the circles are shown and both counts sit side by side with no comment, 42 and 44.

Hints go in order: count the squares that are completely inside first; then sort each edge square by how much of it is inside; then add the whole squares, the more-than-half squares and half of the exact halves. Through doing, the student learns to estimate the area of an awkward shape in square units with the four rules, and sees that circles leave gaps and give different counts, which is why squares are the unit of area.

(tool id M2.S15.T15.C72.2D1, concept M2.S15.T15.C72, topic: Counting Squares)

Tool Name: Nine Squares

Tool Description:

Nine amber squares wait in a tray beside a grid. Three readouts sit above the grid: the area, pinned at 9, the perimeter, live, and a strip that collects every different perimeter the student finds. A task panel holds the current task and an Impossible button. Before placing a square, the student guesses whether a tight block or a spread-out line will have the longer edge.

The student drags squares onto the grid, and each one snaps to the grid and to its neighbours. When two squares share a side that side turns grey and leaves the edge, and the perimeter readout drops. A square that touches another only at a corner gets 'Squares must share a whole side', and a figure that breaks into two pieces gets 'Keep it all in one piece.' Squares can be moved or returned to the tray at any time, and the area readout never changes from 9.

Four tasks follow. First the student uses all nine squares to find the smallest and largest perimeters: the 3 by 3 block gives 12 and a straight line of nine gives 20, and the strip shows both. Next the student makes a figure with perimeter 18, for example a row of 7 with two squares on top of its two left-hand squares, then finds a second figure by moving one square to a place that hides the same number of sides. In the third task the student looks for a second figure with perimeter 12 and presses Impossible, then picks a reason from three: only the 3 by 3 block packs nine squares that tightly, the same nine squares always give the same perimeter, or a perimeter of 12 means an area of 12. In the last task a figure with perimeter 24 appears and a tenth square unlocks. On a flat side it hides 1 side and adds 3 and the perimeter becomes 26, in a notch of two sides it stays 24, and in a slot of three sides it falls to 22.

Hints go in order: count the sides each new square touches; every shared side is hidden from the walk; take those away from the 4 sides the square brings. Through doing, the student learns that the same area can have many perimeters, that tighter packing hides more sides, and that a new square can raise, lower or keep a perimeter.

(tool id M2.S17.T17.C77.2D1, concept M2.S17.T17.C77, topic: Same Area, Different Boundary)

Tool Name: Half the Rectangle

Tool Description:

A grid fills the screen, and a task panel holds a shape library with the triangles BAD and ABE in rectangle ABCD and five larger figures. Two readouts sit one above the other with a large half sign between them: the area of the rectangle above and the area of the triangle below. Before sliding any corner, the student guesses whether the triangle's area will grow, shrink or stay the same.

The student taps three grid points to draw a triangle with one side lying along a grid line as its base. The snug rectangle on that base draws itself in grey, reaching up to the line through the top corner, and the lower readout always shows exactly half of the upper one. If no side lies along a grid line, the tool asks the student to move a corner so one does, because the rectangle needs a base. Dragging any corner makes both readouts follow. A cut tool drops a dashed line that splits a figure into pieces, each shaded in its own colour with its area added to a column and a running sum.

Four tasks follow. In rectangle ABCD, 5 squares wide and 4 high, the student finds both triangles BAD and ABE are 10 square units, half of 20, even though they look different, and cuts ABE with a line from E straight down to see 6 + 4 = 10. Next the student draws a triangle, drags its top corner along the top side with the base fixed, and sees the readout stay put while everything else moves. The tool says nothing. Then five figures are cut into rectangles and triangles, such as a 4 by 5 rectangle with two thin triangles of 2 each, and the areas are 24, 30, 48, 16 and 12 square units. The last task asks for a triangle whose area is not half of its snug rectangle. Every try gives exactly half, so the student presses Impossible and picks a reason from three: a triangle on the same base as a rectangle and between the same parallels is always half of it, only right-angled triangles are half, or a top corner in the middle gives a different share.

Hints go in order: which side is the base; which rectangle has the same base and the same height; cut the triangle with a line from the top corner and halve each part. Through doing, the student learns that a triangle is half of the rectangle on its base wherever the top corner lies, and that any figure with slanted sides can be split into rectangles and triangles and added.

(tool id M2.S18.T18.C80.2D1, concept M2.S18.T18.C80, topic: Half of a Rectangle)

Tool Name: Room Planner

Tool Description:

A plot is divided into rooms by dark walls, with the sizes and areas that are known written in and blanks for the rest. A total box beside it stays grey until every room is filled, and a plan picker switches between two houses, four area mazes and a blank plot. Before filling anything, the student guesses which blank can be worked out first.

Tapping a blank opens a number pad for a side or an area. Tapping a room highlights it and draws thin lines across the plan to every room that must share a measurement with it, because rooms that share a wall share that measurement. A correct entry locks in and the thin lines it satisfies light up briefly. A wrong entry gets 'This wall has to match the room next to it. Have a look.' The total box stays grey until every room is filled in.

In the first plan, Charan's house, the small bedroom is 15 ft wide with area 180 sq ft, so its other side is 180 divided by 15 = 12 ft. The hall shares its walls with it and so is also 12 ft tall, then the utility is 3 ft tall, the parking 3 ft, the garden 20 by 3 = 60 sq ft, and the hall is 20 by 12 plus a 5 by 5 gap under the toilet, 240 + 25 = 265 sq ft. The total box shows 35 by 30 = 1,050 sq ft, and adding the rooms agrees. In Sharan's house the utility is 10 ft tall, so 70 divided by 10 = 7 ft wide, giving hall 23 by 15 = 345, entrance 7 by 15 = 105, small bedroom 12 by 10 = 120 and toilet 5 by 10 = 50, in a 42 by 25 = 1,050 sq ft plot. Placed side by side, the two houses show equal areas but perimeters of 130 ft and 134 ft. In the four mazes the answers are 30 sq cm, 9 sq cm, 16 sq cm and 5 cm; in the first, 26 divided by 13 = 2 shows the right column is twice as wide, so the missing area is 15 times 2 = 30.

Hints go in order: look for a room with its area and one side known; tap it and follow the thin lines to its neighbours; divide the area by the known side. Afterwards the student draws rooms on a blank plot, hides some numbers and saves it as a puzzle for a friend. Through doing, the student learns that every missing number is forced by the rooms around it, so it can be reasoned out instead of measured.

(tool id M2.S20.T20.C97.2D1, concept M2.S20.T20.C97, topic: Breaking a Shape Apart)
