# maths8_ch12 - tool descriptions (10 tools)

Tool Name: Data Explorer: Balance and Change

Tool Description:

The student sees a dot plot lying on a number line like a plank on a see-saw. The first card shows dots at 3, 5, 6, 8 and 13, with a movable triangle under the line that marks the balance point. Two read-outs hang above the plot, one for the total distance from the triangle to the dots on its left and one for the total on its right. Beside the plot are an Add a value button and a transform panel with plus k, minus k and times k.

Before touching anything the student predicts where the triangle must sit for the plank to stay level, then drags it. The plank tilts towards the heavier side and the totals change as the triangle moves: at 6 they read 4 and 9, at 8 they read 10 and 5, and at 7 both read 7, so the plank levels and turns green. A faint marker at 8, halfway between the end dots 3 and 13, shows that the mean is not the midpoint of the extremes. Every other spot unbalances the plank, so the student sees there is only one centre.

Six cards follow, each asking for a prediction first. The student checks that the two totals are equal at the mean 7. Then a dot at 10 is added and the triangle slides to 7.5, so the student says the mean rises. A dot added exactly on the triangle changes nothing, and both totals grow by zero. With plus k set to 4 every dot slides 4 to the right together and the triangle goes with them, so the student predicts 11 and sees the old mean plus 4 written beside it. With times 3 the dots spread out to 9, 15, 18, 24 and 39, and the student predicts 21. The last card asks the student to drag one dot so that the mean becomes 8, and the triangle follows the dot live. A wrong prediction shows the two distance totals so the student sees which side is heavier. The student may also add a pair of values, one 4 below the mean and one 4 above, here 3 and 11, and see the mean stay at 7.

Hints go in order: where would a see-saw under the dots have to sit to stay level? Then: compare the total distance on the left with the total on the right. Then: a value added above the mean makes the left side longer, so the balance point moves right. Last, the value is shown with both totals and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that the mean is the one point where the distances on the two sides are equal, that a value above it pulls it up, one below pulls it down and one equal to it leaves it, and that shifting or scaling every value shifts or scales the mean in the same way.

(tool id M1.S2.T2.C8.2D1, concept M1.S2.T2.C8, topic: The Balancing Act: How the Mean Changes)

Tool Name: Missing Value Lab: Mean and Median

Tool Description:

A row of value cards runs across the top of the screen, and one card is smudged into an ink blot. Above it a switch chooses Mean or Median, and a target line states the given mean or median and how many values there are. Below the row sit a running total and a sorted order strip that is used in median mode.

In Mean mode the student first says what the target tells about the total. Card 1 has four wrestlers who weigh 40, 42, 44 and one smudged weight, with a mean of 43. The student writes the total, 4 times 43 equals 172, then types a weight into the blot and watches the running total move. A wrong try such as 44 shows a total of 170 and a mean of 42.5 beside the target 43. The right weight, 46, locks, and the rule appears: 172 minus the known 126 gives 46. Card 2 is a coconut harvest of 15 trees with a mean of 25.6 but no list of counts. The cards are replaced by one total bar. The student finds 25.6 times 15 equals 384, takes off the 3 that was counted too many to reach 381, and divides by 15 to get the corrected mean 25.4, a drop of 0.2, which is the 3 shared over 15 trees. Card 3 gives five numbers with mean 12 and four known, for example 9, 11, 14 and 15. The total must be 60, the known ones make 49, and the blank is 11.

In Median mode the sorted strip does the work. Card 4 shows 9, 12, 15, 20 and a blank, with a median of 15. As the student drags values into the blank, the five cards re-sort and the middle card lights up. A 10 leaves 12 in the middle, 13 puts 13 there, and 15 or any larger value puts 15 there, so the student finds that the blank can be anything from 15 upwards. Card 5 uses the set 9, 12, 15, 17, 20 and asks for a value that keeps the median at 15. Adding 15 works, the middle two both being 15, while 11 drops the median to 13.5 and 25 lifts it to 16. Card 6 asks for two values that raise the median. Adding 25 and 30 gives 17, but adding 5 and 25 leaves it at 15, because one value below cancels one above.

Hints go in order: what does the mean tell you about the total of all the values? Then: find the target total, then subtract the values you already have. Then: total needed minus the sum of the known values is the missing value. Last, the missing value is shown with its working and a similar card follows. The student states the rule in words at the end of each mode. Through doing, the student learns that a known mean fixes the total, so a missing value is the total minus the known values, and that a known median fixes the middle position, so ordering the values shows what the missing one must satisfy.

(tool id M1.S5.T5.C24.2D1, concept M1.S5.T5.C24, topic: Tinkering with the Median and Finding the Unknown)

Tool Name: Frequency Table Lab

Tool Description:

The student opens a table of the family sizes of a class of 36 students. The value column runs 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10, and the frequency column reads 3, 11, 9, 7, 3, 1, 1 and 1, with every frequency editable. Beside them are a value times frequency column, a running total column, a Total and Count row, and read-outs for the mean and the median. Under the table a dot plot of stacked dots is redrawn the moment a frequency changes.

First the student predicts. The sizes run from 3 to 10, and 4 occurs 11 times while 10 occurs once, so will the true average be bigger or smaller than 6.5, the middle of the two ends? Then the student fills the value times frequency column: 9, 44, 45, 42, 21, 8, 9 and 10. The total is 188 over a count of 36, so the mean is about 5.22, and the balance point of the dot plot sits there, below 6.5. For the median the student first says that with 36 values it lies between the 18th and 19th. The running total column reads 3, 14, 23 and on up to 36, and the row for size 5 is highlighted because it holds positions 15 to 23. Both middle values are 5, so the median is 5 and not a single value had to be written out.

The six cards are the mean (about 5.22), the median (5), a change of one frequency with a prediction, a table of the student's own with mean equal to median, the shortcut check, and a table with a median of 3. For the third card the student raises the frequency of size 10 from 1 to 2, predicts that the mean rises, and sees it move to about 5.35. For the fourth the student can try a table that is the same on both sides of its middle value. For the fifth the lab shows the tempting shortcuts beside 5.22: adding the eight sizes once each and dividing by 8 gives 6.5, and adding the frequencies and dividing by 8 gives 4.5. A frequency average earns a warning with the value times frequency steps shown. For the last the student chooses values and frequencies until the running total passes the middle position at the value 3.

Hints go in order: what does each frequency say about how many times a value appears? Then: multiply each value by its frequency before adding. Then: the total of value times frequency, divided by the total frequency, is the mean. Last, the steps are shown, the median row is highlighted and a new table follows. Through doing, the student learns that each value counts as often as it occurs, and that the median is found where the running total passes the middle position.

(tool id M1.S9.T9.C42.2D1, concept M1.S9.T9.C42, topic: Mean and Median with Frequencies)

Tool Name: Spreadsheet Simulator

Tool Description:

The student sees a small spreadsheet of Sudhakar's class marks. Column letters A to H run across the top and row numbers down the side. Row 1 holds the headings Name, Odia, Telugu, English, Maths, Social, Science and Total, and rows 2 to 8 hold Ratna, Nagesh, Ashwin, Farooq, Mrinal, Gowri and Pankaj with six marks each. Above the grid are a name box that shows the cell under the pointer and a formula bar, and beside it are two buttons, SUM and AVERAGE, that start a formula over a selected range.

The student begins by finding cells. Before clicking, the student predicts where Farooq's Maths mark sits by counting the column letter and the row number, then clicks it. The name box reads E5 and the formula bar shows 42. Next the student drags across a run of cells. The selection glows and its name appears as Start:End. Across Nagesh's row it reads B3:G3 and the tool counts 6 cells, and down the English column for the first five students it reads D2:D6.

Then comes the typing. In H3 the student types =SUM(B3:G3), and the result 250 is checked against the marks added by hand: 41, 43, 48, 39, 40 and 39. For Gowri, who scored 27, 29 and 34 in Odia, Telugu and English, the student first guesses whether the average is above or below 30, then types =AVERAGE(B7:D7) and sees that the three cells are added and divided by 3, giving 30. A wrong range shows the cells it really used and their values: B7:C7 uses only 27 and 29 and gives 28, and B3:F3 leaves out G3 and gives 211 instead of 250. On the next card the student changes one cell, for example Gowri's Odia mark from 27 to 30, predicts the new sum and average, and watches every formula that includes the cell update, here from 90 to 93 and from 30 to 31. On the last card the student types =AVERAGE over a whole subject column, such as B2:B8 for Odia, and compares it with the Telugu column to see which subject has the higher average.

Hints go in order: which letter and number name the cell you want? Then: a range joins two corner cells with a colon. Then: SUM over the range adds the values, and AVERAGE divides that sum by the number of cells. Last, the range is highlighted, the sum and average are shown and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a cell is named by its column and row, that Start:End names a run of cells, and that SUM and AVERAGE do in one step what adding and dividing do by hand.

(tool id M1.S13.T13.C53.2D1, concept M1.S13.T13.C53, topic: Spreadsheets: Totals and Averages Fast)

Tool Name: Line Graph Builder

Tool Description:

The student faces a blank grid with a data table beside it. The axis names and the scale are boxes for the student to fill: what goes along the bottom, what goes up the side, where the scale starts, its top value and its step. Below the grid are a Plot button, a Join button and a switch that adds a second series with its own mark and colour.

Card 1 is a shop's week. Customers visiting from Monday to Sunday are 16, 19, 10, 14, 20, 22 and 35. The student writes days along the bottom and number of customers up the side, then chooses a scale. A top value of 40 in steps of 5 fits the largest value, 35. Each table row is dragged onto the grid, with time across and the value up, and the points are then joined in the order of the days. A point dropped in the wrong spot shows the table value it should match. Card 2 adds the customers who purchased, 10, 8, 7, 11, 12, 16 and 26, as a second line with a different mark and colour and a key. The student sees that both lines rise towards the weekend and that purchasing stays below visiting every day.

Card 3 plots Punjab's monthly highest temperature, which runs from about 19 degrees in January up to 38 in June, just under 35 in July and about 23 in December. Card 4 is a deliberate mistake. The student sets the top of the scale to 30 and the hot months, such as June at 38, appear as markers that do not fit, so the student sees why the scale must be chosen to fit the biggest value. Card 5 gives the price of iodised salt each January from 2016 to 2025 for six states. The student picks three, for example Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal, chooses a scale from 0 to 30 and plots each as its own line. Card 6 asks which line rises most and why a line graph suits the data. West Bengal rises most, from 9.47 to 23.99, about 14.52 rupees, and a line graph shows how a value changes from one year to the next. After each finished graph the tool asks for a one-line summary of the trend.

Hints go in order: what goes along the bottom and what goes up the side? Then: pick the scale so the biggest value fits and the points are not squeezed. Then: each row of the table is one point, with time across and value up. Last, the points are placed and joined in order, the trend is described and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns the four moves of a line graph, which are to label the axes, pick the scale, plot each point and join them in order, and that two lines on one grid need different marks or colours and a key.

(tool id M2.S15.T15.C59.2D1, concept M2.S15.T15.C59, topic: Line Graphs: Temperature in Two States)

Tool Name: Graph Interpreter

Tool Description:

A busy line graph fills the screen. The years 2012 to 2024 run along the bottom, the number of objects launched into space runs up the side to 3000, and four lines labelled World, United States, China and Russia each carry a different mark. Beside the graph are a steepness ruler and a statement panel in which every statement gets a Valid or Not valid switch.

The student works in two steps. Step 1 asks what is given, the axes, the scale and what each line stands for, before any reading is allowed. Step 2 asks what the graph says. Tapping a point shows its place on the graph, and the student reads the value off the scale. Card 1 asks for the World count in 2023, and an answer near 2900 is accepted as about 2900. Card 2 compares the United States rise from 2022 to 2023 with the rise from 2023 to 2024. The student lays the ruler along each segment and sees about 280 against about 30, so the steeper segment is the bigger change. Card 3 asks for two consecutive years in which the World count at least doubled. Sliding the ruler along, the student finds 2016 to 2017, about 230 to 480, and 2019 to 2020, about 600 to 1270.

Card 4 shows four statements to mark. The World count rose every year is not valid, because the line falls in 2018 and in 2024. The United States launched about three fourths of the World count in 2022 to 2024 is valid, about 2250 out of 2850. Nepal launched nothing in 2012 to 2024 is not valid, because Nepal is not on the graph and a missing line says nothing. China and Russia together made about 400 in 2024 is valid, about 280 plus 120. A wrong mark highlights the part of the graph that supports or breaks the statement. Card 5 asks why the three countries do not make up the World line. In 2024 they add to about 2650 against about 2850, so about 200 objects come from countries that are not shown. Card 6 opens a second graph of the main source of lighting in rural and urban homes. The student again names the axes and the lines, then marks statements. A statement is valid only if the lines support it, so one about power cuts is not, since no line shows power cuts. Each graph ends with one summary sentence from the student.

Hints go in order: what are the axes and what does each line stand for? Then: find the value on the line and read across to the scale. Then: compare how steep the two segments are, since the steeper one is the bigger change. Last, the reading and the steepness are shown, the statement is judged and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns to read the axes and the scale first and the shape second, and that a statement is valid only when the graph supports it.

(tool id M2.S18.T18.C66.2D1, concept M2.S18.T18.C66, topic: Reading Line Graphs: Objects in Space)

Tool Name: Rain Map Compare

Tool Description:

A map of India fills the left side of the screen, with six city pins waiting beside it: Kovalam, Udupi, Mumbai, Rameswaram, Chennai and Puri. On the right is a line graph of monthly average rainfall from January to December, on which any city's line can be switched on or off, and under it a grouping panel.

The student drags each pin to the city's place on the map. A pin dropped in the wrong place returns to the map with its coast named. A placed city brings its line onto the graph, and all lines share one scale, so the student must compare them on the same scale before saying that one city gets more rain. Card 1 uses the three west coast cities. The student switches their lines on, drops a peak marker on the highest point of each and reads the month underneath. All three peak in June to August, and Udupi reaches about 880 millimetres in July. Tapping a point reminds the student that the value is an average of that month over several years, so four Junes of 820, 900, 760 and 920 millimetres give 850, a typical June and not a real one.

Card 2 does the same for the east coast. Rameswaram gets most of its rain in October to December and very little from January to September, Chennai starts getting rain from June and peaks in November, and Puri peaks in July to September. A wrong peak month shows the highest point of that city's line. Card 3 asks which city has the highest peak, and the answer is Udupi. Card 4 asks the student to place low-point markers and find the dry months common to all six, which are January to March. Card 5 asks the student to drag the cities into groups by peak season: June to August, July to September and October to December. The grouping is checked against the peak seasons. Card 6 asks why Puri differs from the other east coast cities. It lies on the east coast but peaks in July to September, within the months of the south west monsoon, while Rameswaram and Chennai peak in the months of the north east monsoon. So the coast alone does not decide the month, the winds do.

Hints go in order: which cities lie on the west coast and which on the east coast? Then: look for the highest point on each line and read the month below it. Then: compare peak months across the six lines on one scale. Last, the peak months are shown on the map and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns to read peaks and dry months off a line graph, and to link when a place gets its rain with its coast and its monsoon.

(tool id M2.S21.T21.C76.2D1, concept M2.S21.T21.C76, topic: Catch the Pattern in Rain)

Tool Name: Infographic Map: Rice and Wheat

Tool Description:

A tall map of India with every state coloured fills the screen, and beside it runs a colour bar from minus 100 at the wheat end to plus 100 at the rice end. On the right are a ranking list, a threshold slider and a note box. One state, Karnataka, is shown grey with a question mark. A switch can draw the red line that splits the rice states from the wheat states.

The student taps states to read them. A tap shows the state's name, its value and its colour marked on the bar. Card 1 asks for Punjab, about minus 78, and what that says: Punjab strongly prefers wheat. Bihar, close to 0, is about balanced. On the cards that follow, the student taps and ranks the top five rice states and the top five wheat states. The rice list is Manipur about plus 100, Nagaland plus 99, Mizoram plus 97, Tripura plus 96 and Meghalaya plus 95. The wheat list is Rajasthan about minus 93, Haryana minus 81, Punjab minus 78, Madhya Pradesh minus 60 and Delhi minus 45. A wrong ranking shows the true order with the values.

Next the student drags the threshold slider outwards from 0. States whose value lies inside the band light up, and with a band of minus 20 to plus 20 states such as Bihar and Maharashtra, at about minus 15, are among them. The student names a few balanced states from what lights up. For Karnataka the student first guesses rice or wheat, then reads the neighbours' values: Goa plus 57, Kerala plus 79, Tamil Nadu plus 85, Andhra Pradesh plus 92 and Maharashtra minus 15. Four of the five lean to rice, so the student places a guess on the bar. A guess in the rice leaning range of roughly plus 60 to plus 80 is accepted as reasoned, a guess that ignores the neighbours is questioned, and the tool says that a value found this way is an estimate, not a fact. The last card asks what plus 100 means. A claim that it means only rice is eaten is corrected with the scale text: it means that the gap between rice and wheat eating is the largest, not that no wheat is eaten.

Hints go in order: what does the colour of a state stand for? Then: use the scale at the top to turn the colour into a number. Then: positive is rice, negative is wheat, and near zero is balanced. Last, the value is shown with its meaning and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns to read a colour scale as a number line, to rank and compare values on a map, and to estimate a hidden value from its neighbours.

(tool id M2.S24.T24.C86.2D1, concept M2.S24.T24.C86, topic: Infographics: Rice or Wheat?)

Tool Name: Activity Strip Painter

Tool Description:

A long strip of 48 empty boxes runs across the screen from midnight to midnight. Each box is numbered and stands for 30 minutes, and the strip is marked at midnight and noon. Under it are a colour key of activities such as sleeping, eating, school, studying, travelling, friends and media, a paint brush, and a read-out of the hours spent on each activity. A second strip below the first is used for comparing.

The student picks a colour and drags the brush across boxes to paint. As the brush moves, the box under it shows its start time, since box n starts (n minus 1) times 30 minutes after midnight, and the read-out counts the painted boxes and turns them into hours at two boxes to an hour. Card 1 asks for 8 hours of sleep, which takes 16 boxes. Card 2 asks the student to paint a school day from 9:30 am to 4:30 pm, which runs from box 20 to box 33 and covers 14 boxes, or 7 hours. A wrong box count shows the boxes selected and the hours they make. A day painted as 18 boxes of sleep, 14 of school and 16 of other things reads 9, 7 and 8 hours, which add up to the 24 hours of a day.

Card 3 reverses the task. The student is given box 19 and has to find when it starts. After 18 boxes, which is 9 hours, it begins at 9:00 am. Card 4 asks the student to paint their own weekday on the top strip and a holiday on the second strip. If sleep is 8 hours on a weekday and 10 on a holiday, that is 16 boxes against 20, which is 4 more boxes, or 2 more hours. The comparison highlights the boxes that differ. Card 5 compares the student's strip with an adult's. Card 6 shows a ready-made strip, and the student finds the longest block and names the activity from the key. The student can guess the day too: a long block of class colour with travel on either side suggests a school day, and a long unbroken block of friends and media suggests the day of a long movie. One colour used for two activities is flagged against the key, since the same colour must always mean the same activity.

Hints go in order: how many boxes make one hour? Then: two boxes are an hour, so count the boxes and divide by two. Then: the boxes from the start of the block to the end give the hours. Last, the boxes are counted, the hours are shown and a new card follows. Through doing, the student learns that a whole day can be held on 48 boxes, that counting boxes gives time, and that two strips painted in one key can be compared box by box.

(tool id M2.S25.T25.C90.2D1, concept M2.S25.T25.C90, topic: What Can a Strip Say? Sleepy-Deepy)

Tool Name: Data Project Workspace

Tool Description:

The student opens an empty project workspace. A question box sits at the top, below it a data table with a row for each day or each person, and beside it automatic read-outs for the mean and the median. A switch chooses a strip or a line graph, a comparison column waits for an adult's or a group's data, and a findings note is at the bottom.

The workspace begins with the question, not the data. The student writes a question, such as how long the family sleeps, and a guess, such as whether children, adults and the elderly sleep the same. The graph stays closed until both are written. Then the student chooses what to record, for whom and for how many days, and picks one of two projects: a week of family sleep, or the daily timings of Grade 8 schools. Card 1 asks the student to enter seven days of sleep and read the mean and the median. If the two differ, the tool says why. For 9, 8.5, 10 and 9 hours the mean is about 9.1 and the median 9, because the 10 pulls the mean up a little.

Card 2 asks the student to show the data on a strip or a line graph, whichever suits it, with the 30 minute boxes on a strip counting hours. For example, 1.5 hours outdoors is 3 boxes. Card 3 adds an adult's data in the comparison column, so the student can set the two side by side and say what is similar and what is different. Card 4 pools a group's sleep data by age group, children, adults and elderly, and shows a mean and median for each. Card 5 collects school timings. A school day of 9:30 am to 4:30 pm is 7 hours with class time and breaks, and the student averages the days of several schools. Card 6 asks for a finding and a new question. A finding that the data does not support is flagged with the values, and the note cannot be saved without a question.

Hints go in order: what question do you want your data to answer? Then: decide what to record, for whom and for how many days. Then: enter the values, then compare the mean with the median. Last, a sample entry and graph are shown to start and the student continues with their own data. Through doing, the student learns the whole path of an investigation: ask, collect, summarise with a mean and median, show in a graph, say what was found, and end with a new question.

(tool id M3.S26.T26.C93.2D1, concept M3.S26.T26.C93, topic: Your Own Data Story)
